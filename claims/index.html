Claim check

101 common ideas about PMDD, ADHD, autism and the experiences often linked to them in women, each restated as what the research supports.

How to read this

Each card states what the research supports, in plain words. The strength of evidence tells you how sure we can be: Established several good studies or reviews agree; Supported replicated, with some limits; Preliminary early, small or indirect studies. Open Read the evidence to see the popular version you may have heard, what the studies found, and the studies themselves.

The popular versions are paraphrased in our own words, not quoted from anyone. Evidence last reviewed October 2026. How we rate evidence.

What the research is clearest about

  • Asthma and eczema are modestly more common in children with ADHD, but food allergy has not shown a link.
  • Autism and ADHD often occur together: roughly 28–39% of autistic people have ADHD, and about 21% of children with ADHD are autistic.
  • Childhood neglect, including emotional neglect, is linked with higher rates of depression later in life.
  • Emotional dysregulation is common and impairing in children and adults with ADHD, though it is not an official diagnostic criterion.
  • Histamine-producing brain cells help maintain wakefulness and attention.
  • In menstruating women with low ferritin but normal haemoglobin, iron reduced self-reported fatigue in randomised trials.
  • Mast cell activation syndrome is a defined diagnosis that requires lab-confirmed mediator rises, not symptoms alone.
  • Most people diagnosed with POTS are young women, often with symptoms starting in adolescence.
  • PMDD is confirmed by daily symptom ratings over at least two cycles, not by a blood test or a one-off questionnaire.
  • PMDD is linked with substantially higher rates of suicidal thoughts, suicide attempts and death by suicide at a group level.

Diagnosis & being recognised

Why women and girls are missed, and what does or doesn't rule ADHD or autism in or out.

ADHD occurs at every IQ level, and high-IQ people with ADHD have more difficulties than equally bright people without it.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Higher IQ goes with fewer ADHD traits on average, but ADHD still occurs in very bright children and adults, who had more impairment, comorbidity and work and family problems than high-IQ peers without ADHD.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“A high IQ protects you from ADHD.”

The popular version goes further: a lower average rate is not protection, and ADHD is found at every IQ level.

What the studies found

High-IQ (IQ 120 or more) children with ADHD (49) had more grade repetition, comorbidity and impairment than 92 high-IQ children without ADHD (Antshel 2007). High-IQ adults with ADHD (64 vs 53 high-IQ controls) had lower quality of life, poorer work and family functioning, and more accidents and depression (Antshel 2008). ADHD and IQ are negatively correlated on nearly all constructs, but ADHD occurs at every level (Rommelse 2016).

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Anxiety and depression are very common with ADHD and can be what gets noticed first, so ADHD is worth asking about when those treatments only partly help. We did not find a study giving a precise misdiagnosis rate for women (search last updated October 2026).

You may have heard

“Women with ADHD are often treated for anxiety or depression for years before anyone spots the ADHD.”

The popular version is specific to women and to 'years'; the evidence shows the pattern in adults generally, without a measured delay.

What the studies found

In a US national survey (3,199 adults aged 18-44), most adults with ADHD had never been treated for it, though many had been treated for other mental or substance disorders (Kessler 2006). ADHD carries roughly 4-5 times the odds of anxiety and depression in both sexes (pooled ORs 5.0 and 4.5; Hartman 2023), and reviews note that comorbid anxiety and depression can lead to missed diagnoses in women (Quinn 2014). We did not find a study giving a precise misdiagnosis rate for women (search last updated October 2026).

Doing well at school does not rule out ADHD; it occurs in high-IQ people, whose coping can delay diagnosis.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Good grades don't rule out ADHD. High-IQ children with ADHD showed the same comorbidity and treatment patterns as other children with ADHD, and coping strategies may make ADHD easier to miss.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“If you did well at school, you can't have ADHD.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs the other way.

What the studies found

A review of 41 articles on women and girls concluded that satisfactory academic achievement should not rule out ADHD (Quinn 2014). In a population birth cohort, children with ADHD and high IQ had the same comorbidity and treatment patterns as other children with ADHD, and better reading (Katusic 2011). A systematic review found limited but consistent evidence that ADHD is valid in high-IQ people and that their coping can lead to a missed diagnosis (Rommelse 2016).

Link to this claim

Girls with ADHD are diagnosed later and less often than boys, and disruptive behaviour predicts diagnosis more strongly in girls.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Girls with ADHD tend to be diagnosed later and less often, partly because diagnosis tends to follow disruptive behaviour that many girls don't show. Experts also point to coping strategies, gender bias and co-occurring conditions.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Girls with ADHD get missed because they're quiet and daydreamy rather than disruptive.”

The popular version matches the evidence well, though 'quiet and daydreamy' simplifies several reasons girls are missed.

What the studies found

In 19,804 Swedish twins, hyperactivity/impulsivity and conduct problems predicted a clinical ADHD diagnosis more strongly in girls than in boys (conduct OR 1.43 per point), suggesting quieter girls are missed (Mowlem 2018). Girls meet criteria at just under half the rate of boys, approaching parity by adulthood, and are diagnosed later and less often medicated (Hinshaw 2021; Martin 2024). An expert consensus names compensatory strategies, gender bias and comorbidity as barriers to recognition (Young 2020).

Link to this claim

Sensory sensitivity is common in adults with ADHD as well as in autism, so on its own it does not indicate autism.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Sensory sensitivity is common in ADHD too, especially in women, and in one study it tracked ADHD severity rather than autistic traits. Sensory over-responsivity is a core feature of autism but is not diagnostic by itself.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“If you have sensory sensitivities, you must be autistic.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs the other way: sensitivity is not unique to autism.

What the studies found

116 adults with ADHD had more sensory hyper- and hyposensitivity than norms, linked to ADHD severity but not to autistic symptoms, and 43% of women vs 22% of men reported atypical sensitivity (Bijlenga 2017). In an online survey, 69% of people with ADHD symptoms reported light sensitivity vs 28% without (Kooij 2014). Sensory over-responsivity is more specific to autism than to other conditions (Ben-Sasson 2019) but is not diagnostic alone.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Suicidality is very common in autistic and possibly autistic adults, and many adults are missed, so keeping autism in mind makes sense. We did not find a study testing whether universal screening reduces suicide (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Every adult mental-health assessment should screen for autism with standard questionnaires (such as the AQ-10, CAT-Q and RAADS-R), and doing so would reduce suicide.”

The popular version goes further: the reason for concern is sound, but the questionnaires work less well in clinics than in validation studies, and the suicide benefit is untested.

What the studies found

The rationale is sound: suicidality is very common in autistic and possibly autistic adults (Newell 2023), and many adults, especially women, are missed (Lai 2015). But the tools are weaker in real clinical settings than in their validation studies. In 476 adults referred for assessment, the AQ had specificity 0.29, and 64% of those below cut-off were in fact autistic; anxiety inflated scores (Ashwood 2016). The AQ-10 and RAADS-R were validated mainly by comparing known cases with controls (Booth 2013; Ritvo 2010). The CAT-Q measures camouflaging and its validation reports good reliability but no diagnostic cut-off (Hull 2018). We did not find a study testing whether universal screening reduces suicide (search last updated October 2026).

You may have heard

“Being identified as autistic or ADHD late in life brings relief and grief together, including an ongoing, often unrecognised grief for the years spent not knowing.”

The popular version matches what women describe in interviews, but the idea of an ongoing, unrecognised grief has not been studied directly.

What the studies found

Qualitative studies of late-diagnosed women describe this mix. Diagnosis shifted self-criticism toward self-compassion but was painful so late (Leedham 2020). Describing this grief as 'ambiguous' borrows the theory of ambiguous loss (Boss 2016), which was developed for families; we did not find a study applying it to late-diagnosed adults.

In small studies, high-IQ adults with ADHD often scored in the average range on thinking tests despite having ADHD.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

In bright adults, test scores can look normal even when ADHD is present, so real-life impairment matters more than test results. The studies are small, and lab tests generally agree poorly with everyday functioning.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“A high IQ masks ADHD, so it doesn't show up on testing.”

The popular version goes further: high IQ blunts test differences in some studies, but many bright adults with ADHD still show impairment on testing.

What the studies found

In a small study (51 adults with ADHD, 33 controls), the higher-IQ ADHD group differed from controls on only one test measure, while the standard-IQ group was impaired on many (Milioni 2016). High-IQ adults with ADHD scored worse than high-IQ controls but within the 'average' range (Antshel 2010), and 73% of 157 adults with ADHD and IQ 120 or more were impaired on at least 5 of 8 executive-function measures (Brown 2009). The evidence is small and cross-sectional, and lab tests generally agree poorly with everyday functioning (Toplak 2012).

Masking & giftedness

Compensating, camouflaging and the 'gifted kid' story.

Camouflaging is reported across genders and in ADHD as well as autism, though autistic women report it more than autistic men.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Camouflaging happens across genders and diagnoses; undiagnosed adults with social difficulties and adults with ADHD also do it. It is more common in autistic women and is linked to poorer mental health.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Masking is something only autistic women do.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs the other way: masking is more common in autistic women but not exclusive to them.

What the studies found

Undiagnosed adults with social difficulties described compensation strategies similar to those of diagnosed autistic adults (Livingston 2019), and adults with ADHD also camouflage, though less than autistic adults (van der Putten 2024). Females used more camouflaging strategies than males in a meta-analysis (Cruz 2025), so it is more common in women but not exclusive to them. In autistic adults, camouflaging was one predictor of suicide risk (Cassidy 2018).

Link to this claim

Gifted people score somewhat higher on intellectual curiosity and imagination, while differences in emotional and sensory intensity are small.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

What are called 'overexcitabilities' look largely like the personality trait openness to experience. Evidence that gifted people are more emotionally or sensorily intense is weak, and a large UK study found less anxiety in high scorers.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Gifted people live with 'overexcitabilities': heightened emotional, sensory and intellectual intensity that shapes their lives.”

The popular version goes further: the differences are mainly intellectual and imaginative, and small for emotional and sensory intensity.

What the studies found

The idea uses Dabrowski's term 'overexcitability' (OE). A meta-analysis found gifted samples score higher on overexcitability questionnaires, but differences were medium only for intellectual and imaginational OEs, small for emotional and sensual OEs and not significant for psychomotor OE, and the authors note important limitations (Winkler 2016). OEs appear to be largely the personality trait 'openness to experience' (Vuyk 2016). Surveys of high-IQ society members reporting more anxiety, autism and allergies rely on self-selected members (Karpinski 2018; Fries 2022). In UK Biobank, high intelligence went with less anxiety and PTSD but more allergies (Williams 2022).

Link to this claim

In large population studies, high intelligence was linked with fewer mental disorders, not more, and giftedness has no clinical definition.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Calling giftedness a neurodivergence is a terminology choice research can't settle. The clinical claim that giftedness is a neurodevelopmental condition like autism or ADHD is untested in the studies we found, though it can co-occur with both.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Giftedness is a form of neurodivergence, alongside autism and ADHD.”

The popular version mixes an identity choice with a clinical claim; the clinical part has not been shown.

What the studies found

Two different questions are mixed here. The first is about language: 'neurodivergence' is a broad community and identity term, not a diagnosis, and whether people include giftedness under it is a choice about terminology that research can't settle. The second is a testable clinical claim: that giftedness is a neurodevelopmental condition like autism or ADHD, with its own clinical features and needs. That claim is untested in the studies we found: giftedness has no diagnostic definition beyond ability or achievement thresholds, and its genetic links point in different directions for the two conditions. Autism polygenic risk is slightly positively associated with cognitive ability (Clarke 2015), while intelligence appears protective against ADHD (Savage 2018). High intelligence was not associated with more mental disorders in population data (Williams 2022; Gale 2010), and a systematic review of gifted children found inconsistent evidence on emotional and behavioural problems (Tasca 2022).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Adults who were 'gifted kids' experience a distinctive kind of burnout, different from ordinary overwork burnout.”

The popular idea has not been studied; the nearest evidence concerns autistic burnout and the mental health of highly intelligent people.

What the studies found

We did not find a research construct of 'gifted burnout' (search last updated October 2026). Autistic burnout has qualitative and early measurement research (Raymaker 2020; Arnold 2023), and autistic young people who compensate socially and have higher IQ report more anxiety (Livingston 2018). But population studies show high intelligence goes with less anxiety and fewer mental disorders (Williams 2022; Koenen 2009), so being gifted alone is not a known risk factor. Where the pattern fits, unrecognised autism or ADHD in a capable person, plus long-term compensation, may be a more plausible explanation than giftedness itself, though this has not been tested directly.

Link to this claim

Coping strategies that hide symptoms are a recognised barrier to diagnosing ADHD in women, according to expert consensus and interview studies.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Many women develop coping strategies that hide ADHD and can delay diagnosis. Adults with ADHD do camouflage, but less than autistic adults, and ADHD-specific masking has not yet been properly measured.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Women with ADHD 'mask' their symptoms, and that's why they're diagnosed so late.”

The popular version goes further: it treats masking as the main reason for late diagnosis, while it is one of several.

What the studies found

An expert consensus lists compensatory strategies that mask symptoms as one barrier to recognising ADHD in women (Young 2020), and 32 adult outpatients described many self-made coping strategies before diagnosis (Canela 2017). We did not find a validated ADHD-specific masking measure (search last updated October 2026).
