Diagnosis & being recognised

Why women and girls are missed, and what does or doesn't rule ADHD or autism in or out.

Holds up

Mostly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Girls with ADHD get missed because they're quiet and daydreamy rather than disruptive.

In 19,804 Swedish twins, hyperactivity/impulsivity and conduct problems predicted a clinical ADHD diagnosis more strongly in girls than in boys (conduct OR 1.43 per point), suggesting quieter girls are missed (Mowlem 2018). Girls meet criteria at just under half the rate of boys, approaching parity by adulthood, and are diagnosed later and less often medicated (Hinshaw 2021; Martin 2024). An expert consensus names compensatory strategies, gender bias and comorbidity as barriers to recognition (Young 2020).

Closer to the evidence: Girls with ADHD tend to be diagnosed later and less often, partly because diagnosis tends to follow disruptive behaviour that many girls don't show.

Sources
  • Mowlem 2018
  • Hinshaw 2021
  • Martin 2024
  • Young 2020

Mostly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Late identification as autistic or ADHD brings relief and grief together, an ongoing, unrecognised ('ambiguous') grief for the years spent not knowing.

Qualitative studies of late-diagnosed women describe exactly this mix. Diagnosis shifted self-criticism toward self-compassion but was painful so late (Leedham 2020). Women described years of pretending to be normal (Bargiela 2016). A synthesis of women diagnosed with ADHD in adulthood found self-acceptance after diagnosis alongside the costs of years undiagnosed (Attoe 2023). The 'ambiguous' framing borrows Pauline Boss's theory of ambiguous loss (Boss 2016). That theory was developed for families, and it has not been studied in late-diagnosed adults.

Closer to the evidence: Many women diagnosed late describe relief and grief together; this is well documented in qualitative studies, though not yet measured in larger samples.

Sources
  • Leedham 2020
  • Bargiela 2016
  • Attoe 2023
  • Boss 2016

Mostly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Women with ADHD are often treated for anxiety or depression for years before anyone spots the ADHD.

In a US national survey (3,199 adults aged 18-44), most adults with ADHD had never been treated for it, though many had been treated for other mental or substance disorders (Kessler 2006). ADHD carries roughly 4-5 times the odds of anxiety and depression in both sexes (pooled ORs 5.0 and 4.5; Hartman 2023), and reviews note that comorbid anxiety and depression can lead to missed diagnoses in women (Quinn 2014). No study gives a precise misdiagnosis rate for women.

Closer to the evidence: Anxiety and depression are very common with ADHD and can be what gets noticed first, so ADHD is worth asking about when those treatments only partly help.

Sources
  • Kessler 2006
  • Hartman 2023
  • Quinn 2014
  • Ginapp 2022

Partly right

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
A high IQ masks ADHD, so it doesn't show up on testing.

In a small study (51 adults with ADHD, 33 controls), the higher-IQ ADHD group differed from controls on only one test measure, while the standard-IQ group was impaired on many (Milioni 2016). High-IQ adults with ADHD scored worse than high-IQ controls but within the 'average' range (Antshel 2010), and 73% of 157 adults with ADHD and IQ 120 or more were impaired on at least 5 of 8 executive-function measures (Brown 2009). The evidence is small and cross-sectional, and lab tests generally agree poorly with everyday functioning (Toplak 2012).

Closer to the evidence: In bright adults, test scores can look normal even when ADHD is present, so real-life impairment matters more than test results.

Sources
  • Milioni 2016
  • Antshel 2010
  • Brown 2009
  • Toplak 2012

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Every adult mental-health assessment should screen for autism using the AQ-10 (6+), CAT-Q (100+) and RAADS-R (65+), and this will reduce suicide.

This is usually put forward as a clinical proposal. The rationale is sound: suicidality is very common in autistic and possibly autistic adults (Newell 2023), and many adults are missed (Lai 2015). But the tools are weaker in real clinical settings than in their validation studies. In 476 adults referred for assessment, the AQ had specificity 0.29, and 64% of those below cut-off were in fact autistic; anxiety inflated scores (Ashwood 2016). The AQ-10 and RAADS-R were validated mainly by comparing known cases with controls (Booth 2013; Ritvo 2011). The CAT-Q validation reports good reliability but no diagnostic cut-off (Hull 2018). No study has tested whether universal screening reduces suicide.

Closer to the evidence: Clinicians should keep autism in mind in adult mental-health assessments; questionnaires can prompt a referral but cannot confirm or rule out autism, and no study shows screening alone prevents suicide.

Sources
  • Newell 2023
  • Lai 2015
  • Ashwood 2016
  • Booth 2013
  • Ritvo 2010
  • Hull 2018

Not supported

False   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
A high IQ protects you from ADHD.

High-IQ (IQ 120 or more) children with ADHD (49) had more grade repetition, comorbidity and impairment than 92 high-IQ children without ADHD (Antshel 2007). High-IQ adults with ADHD (64 vs 53 high-IQ controls) had lower quality of life, poorer work and family functioning, and more accidents and depression (Antshel 2008). ADHD and IQ are negatively correlated on nearly all constructs, but ADHD occurs at every level (Rommelse 2016).

Closer to the evidence: ADHD occurs at every IQ level, and bright people with ADHD still struggle more than equally bright people without it.

Sources
  • Antshel 2007
  • Antshel 2008
  • Rommelse 2016

False   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
If you did well at school, you can't have ADHD.

A review of 41 articles on women and girls concluded that satisfactory academic achievement should not rule out ADHD (Quinn 2014). In a population birth cohort, children with ADHD and high IQ had the same comorbidity and treatment patterns as other children with ADHD, and better reading (Katusic 2011). A systematic review found limited but consistent evidence that ADHD is valid in high-IQ people and that their coping can lead to a missed diagnosis (Rommelse 2016).

Closer to the evidence: Good grades don't rule out ADHD; many bright girls cope at school at a high hidden cost.

Sources
  • Quinn 2014
  • Katusic 2011
  • Rommelse 2016

False   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
If you have sensory sensitivities, you must be autistic.

116 adults with ADHD had more sensory hyper- and hyposensitivity than norms, linked to ADHD severity but not to autistic symptoms, and 43% of women vs 22% of men reported atypical sensitivity (Bijlenga 2017). In an online survey, 69% of people with ADHD symptoms reported light sensitivity vs 28% without (Kooij 2014). Sensory over-responsivity is more specific to autism than to other conditions (Ben-Sasson 2019) but is not diagnostic alone.

Closer to the evidence: Sensory sensitivity is common in ADHD too, especially in women; it is part of autism but doesn't by itself mean you're autistic.

Sources
  • Bijlenga 2017
  • Kooij 2014
  • Ben-Sasson 2019
  • Tavassoli 2013

Masking & giftedness

Compensating, camouflaging and the 'gifted kid' story.

Partly right

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Many 'gifted kids' are identified as autistic and/or ADHD only in adulthood, often after burnout, because their ability hid traits that were always there.

This idea comes from clinical practice rather than from a study. Parts are supported. Autistic adolescents who compensate socially have higher IQ and executive function but more anxiety, and are not 'milder' cases (Livingston 2019). In adults diagnosed late, ability and coping seem to soften rather than remove traits (Happé 2016). Higher IQ can mask ADHD on executive tests (Milioni 2016), and gifted children were over-represented, at 8.8%, in one ADHD clinic sample (Cornoldi 2023). The genetics differ by condition: autism polygenic risk is slightly positively associated with cognitive ability (Clarke 2016), while intelligence appears protective against ADHD (Savage 2018). No study has followed gifted children into adulthood to test the full pathway.

Closer to the evidence: High ability can delay recognition of autism and ADHD by helping people compensate, sometimes until demands exceed their resources; how often this happens to gifted children has not been measured.

Sources
  • Livingston 2018
  • Happé 2016
  • Milioni 2016
  • Cornoldi 2023
  • Clarke 2015
  • Savage 2018
  • Rommelse 2016

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Masking is exhausting and over time erodes the ability to notice one's own hunger, fatigue and distress signals.

The exhaustion part is well described. Autistic adults report exhaustion as a consequence of camouflaging (Hull 2017), and more camouflaging is associated with worse mental health (Cook 2021) and with suicidality (Cassidy 2020). Interoceptive and emotional-awareness differences are real in many autistic people (alexithymia 49.93% vs 4.89%; Kinnaird 2019), and some studies find reduced interoception in ADHD (Bruton 2025). But no study has tested whether masking causes poorer body awareness. The direction could run the other way, or both could reflect autistic traits.

Closer to the evidence: Masking is linked to exhaustion and poorer mental health, and many autistic and ADHD people have trouble reading body signals; whether masking causes that is not known.

Sources
  • Hull 2017
  • Cook 2021
  • Cassidy 2020
  • Kinnaird 2019
  • Bruton 2025

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Neuroimaging shows that masking is compensatory brain activity that doesn't switch off, even at rest.

In 110 healthy young adults without a diagnosis (mostly women), more camouflaging was associated with stronger default mode-cingulo-opercular resting-state connectivity only among those with higher autistic traits, and this mediated links with depression and anxiety; the design was cross-sectional (Guo 2026). In 119 adults, more camouflaging was associated with a heightened vmPFC self-representation response in autistic women only, on a task-based scan (Lai 2019). Camouflaging did not predict distress beyond social anxiety in 113 autistic adults (Roisenberg 2026).

Closer to the evidence: One study of students with autistic traits found more masking went with stronger links between brain networks at rest, tied to anxiety and low mood; it can't show masking causes this.

Sources
  • Guo 2026
  • Lai 2019
  • Roisenberg 2026
  • Cook 2021

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Women with ADHD 'mask' their symptoms, and that's why they're diagnosed so late.

An expert consensus lists compensatory strategies that mask symptoms as one barrier to recognising ADHD in women (Young 2020), and 32 adult outpatients described many self-made coping strategies before diagnosis (Canela 2017). Adults with ADHD scored higher on a camouflaging questionnaire than comparison adults but lower than autistic adults, and camouflaging tracked autistic traits rather than ADHD traits (van der Putten 2024). No validated ADHD-specific masking measure exists yet.

Closer to the evidence: Many women develop coping strategies that hide ADHD and can delay diagnosis, but 'masking' in ADHD hasn't yet been properly measured.

Sources
  • Young 2020
  • Canela 2017
  • van der Putten 2024
  • Attoe 2023

Overstated

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Gifted people live with 'overexcitabilities': a heightened emotional, sensory and intellectual intensity that shapes their lives.

Known here only from paywalled post titles, which use Dabrowski's term. A meta-analysis found gifted samples score higher on overexcitability questionnaires, but differences were medium only for intellectual and imaginational OEs, small for emotional and sensual OEs and not significant for psychomotor OE (Winkler 2016). OEs appear to be largely the personality trait 'openness to experience' (Vuyk 2016). Mensa surveys reporting more anxiety, autism and allergies rely on self-selected members (Karpinski 2018; Fries 2022). In UK Biobank, high intelligence went with less anxiety and PTSD but more allergies (Williams 2023).

Closer to the evidence: Gifted people tend to be more intellectually curious and imaginative (high openness); evidence that they are more emotionally or sensorily intense is weak.

Sources
  • Winkler 2016
  • Vuyk 2016
  • Karpinski 2018
  • Fries 2022
  • Williams 2022

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Masking causes autistic burnout and suicide.

A systematic review of 29 studies called the link between camouflaging and worse mental health 'preliminary' (Cook 2021). In 164 autistic adults, camouflaging was one of several predictors of suicide risk (Cassidy 2018), but the autistic burnout measure did not track masking in 141 adults (Arnold 2023). No longitudinal study shows masking leading to burnout and then suicidality.

Closer to the evidence: Masking is linked to poorer mental health in autistic people, but research hasn't shown that it directly causes burnout or suicide.

Sources
  • Cook 2021
  • Cassidy 2018
  • Arnold 2023
  • Cassidy 2020

Not supported

Unsupported   Unsupported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Former 'gifted kids' have a distinctive kind of burnout that differs from ordinary overwork burnout.

Known here from paywalled post titles. There is no research construct of 'gifted burnout'. Autistic burnout has qualitative and early measurement research (Raymaker 2020; Arnold 2023), and compensating autistic young people with higher IQ report more anxiety (Livingston 2019). But population studies show high intelligence goes with less anxiety and fewer mental disorders (Williams 2023; Koenen 2009), so being gifted alone is not a risk factor. Where it fits, the pattern is better explained by unrecognised autism or ADHD in a capable person.

Closer to the evidence: Exhaustion in capable adults who were 'gifted kids' may reflect unrecognised autism or ADHD and long-term compensation; giftedness alone is not linked to more burnout or mental illness.

Sources
  • Raymaker 2020
  • Arnold 2023
  • Livingston 2018
  • Williams 2022
  • Koenen 2009

Unsupported   Unsupported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Gifted and neurodivergent children grasp mortality unusually early, sometimes at age 3–4.

This comes from adults' recollections in therapy. Most children understand that death is permanent, ends bodily function and is universal by about 5 to 7 years (Speece 1984). No study has compared this by IQ or neurotype, so 'unusually early' is untested.

Closer to the evidence: Many adults remember worrying about death young; most children understand death by about age 5–7, and whether gifted or neurodivergent children get there earlier hasn't been studied.

Sources
  • Speece 1984

Unsupported   Unsupported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Gifted and neurodivergent people have a distinct 'existential depression', in several types, that is accurate perception rather than illness and that standard treatment misses.

No peer-reviewed study of 'existential depression' as a construct in gifted or neurodivergent people was found. The idea comes from gifted-education books. Evidence on emotional problems in gifted children is mixed (Tasca 2022), and high intelligence is not associated with more mental disorder in population data (Williams 2022). Typologies offered as 'field guides' are reflective tools, not diagnoses, which is usually stated. The risk is that depression with treatable features goes untreated, a risk the writing itself often warns about.

Closer to the evidence: Some people's low mood centres on meaning and mortality, and that is worth exploring in therapy, but there is no evidence that gifted people have a separate kind of depression that standard treatment can't help.

Sources
  • Tasca 2022
  • Williams 2022

Unsupported   Unsupported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Giftedness is a form of neurodivergence, alongside autism and ADHD.

This is a framing (known here from a paywalled post title), not a testable finding. Giftedness has no diagnostic definition beyond ability or achievement thresholds. Its links with autism and ADHD point in different directions. Autism polygenic risk is slightly positively associated with cognitive ability (Clarke 2016), while intelligence appears protective against ADHD (Savage 2018). High intelligence is not associated with more mental disorders in population data (Williams 2023; Gale 2010). A systematic review of gifted children found inconsistent evidence on emotional and behavioural problems (Tasca 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Giftedness is a difference in ability that can co-occur with autism or ADHD and complicate their recognition, but it is not itself a clinical or neurodevelopmental category.

Sources
  • Clarke 2015
  • Savage 2018
  • Williams 2022
  • Gale 2010
  • Tasca 2022

False   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Masking is something only autistic women do.

Undiagnosed adults with social difficulties described compensation strategies similar to those of diagnosed autistic adults (Livingston 2019), and adults with ADHD also camouflage, though less than autistic adults (van der Putten 2024). Females used more camouflaging strategies than males in a meta-analysis (Cruz 2025), so it is more common in women but not exclusive to them. In autistic adults, camouflaging was one predictor of suicide risk (Cassidy 2018).

Closer to the evidence: Camouflaging happens across genders and diagnoses; it is more common in autistic women and is linked to poorer mental health.

Sources
  • Livingston 2019
  • van der Putten 2024
  • Cruz 2025
  • Cassidy 2018

Unsupported   Unsupported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Neurodivergence (gifted-neurodivergent) is connected to heightened intuition and 'energetic' or metaphysical abilities.

This is usually offered as philosophy or personal framework rather than as a research finding. No studies in this library, or known to us, test 'energetic' abilities in autistic, ADHD or gifted people, and the terms are not defined in a way research can measure. Openness to experience, which is higher in gifted samples (Vuyk 2016), is the nearest measurable trait.

Closer to the evidence: Some neurodivergent people describe strong intuition or a sense of perceiving more; this is a lived-experience description that has not been studied.

Sources
  • Vuyk 2016

Unsupported   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Predictive-processing neuroscience shows that neurodivergent and gifted brains run faster, higher-resolution, more confident prediction models.

Predictive processing is a leading framework, and popular accounts usually say so. But the autism versions propose the opposite of 'stronger predictions'. They suggest weaker priors, so that perception leans less on expectation (Pellicano 2012). Alternatively, prediction errors may be given an inflexibly high weight, so sameness feels safer (Van de Cruys 2014), or sensory evidence may be over-weighted relative to prior beliefs (Lawson 2014). A review of 47 empirical studies mainly found difficulty learning predictive relationships, especially when cues were faint or inconsistent (Cannon 2021). No eligible review on ADHD or giftedness was found.

Closer to the evidence: Predictive-processing theories suggest autistic perception may rely less on prior expectations or weigh surprises too heavily, not that neurodivergent brains predict better; for ADHD and giftedness the theory is untested.

Sources
  • Pellicano 2012
  • Van de Cruys 2014
  • Lawson 2014
  • Cannon 2021

Autism & AuDHD

How autism and ADHD overlap, and what is specific to autism.

Holds up

True   Established

Popular claim, paraphrased
Lots of people with ADHD are also autistic, and vice versa.

Meta-analyses estimate ADHD in 28% (Lai 2019) to 38.5% (Rong 2021) of autistic people, and autism in 21% of children and adolescents with ADHD (Hollingdale 2019). In 1.9 million Swedes, autism carried far higher odds of ADHD (OR 22.33), with a twin and sibling pattern consistent with shared genetics (Ghirardi 2017). There is still no meta-analysis of autism in adults with ADHD.

Closer to the evidence: Autism and ADHD often occur together and share genetic roots, so if you have one, it's reasonable to ask about the other.

Sources
  • Lai 2019
  • Rong 2021
  • Hollingdale 2019
  • Ghirardi 2017

Partly right

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic adults are 6x more likely to have alexithymia.

No source for '6x' was found. A meta-analysis of 15 studies found 49.93% of autistic people vs 4.89% of neurotypical people met TAS-20 alexithymia criteria, a ratio of about 10 (Kinnaird 2019). Alexithymia contributed to autistic burnout in a systematic review of 48 studies (Ali 2025).

Closer to the evidence: About half of autistic adults have alexithymia, roughly ten times the rate in non-autistic people, and it may make burnout harder to notice.

Sources
  • Kinnaird 2019
  • Ali 2025

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic inertia (being stuck unable to start, or unable to stop) is a real autistic experience.

A qualitative analysis of 501 Reddit posts with 9,955 comments described inertia as 'all or nothing' extremes of staying at rest or in motion, ranging from joyful to highly disabling and linked with fatigue; it corroborated earlier interview studies, and the authors note the topic is scarcely acknowledged in academic research (Ward 2026). There is no measure, prevalence estimate or comparison with non-autistic people or ADHD.

Closer to the evidence: Many autistic people describe inertia, being stuck unable to start or to stop, and early qualitative research takes it seriously; it hasn't yet been measured or compared with ADHD-related difficulty starting tasks.

Sources
  • Ward 2026

Not supported

Unsupported   Unsupported

Popular claim, paraphrased
AuDHD is its own distinct condition.

There is no validated research definition of a combined 'AuDHD' profile. A review concludes autism and ADHD overlap heavily but remain distinct enough to warrant separate diagnoses (Antshel 2019). Having both matters: autistic adults with more ADHD symptoms reported less independence and lower quality of life (Yerys 2022), and an earlier ADHD diagnosis delayed autism diagnosis, by 2.6 years on average in girls (Kentrou 2019).

Closer to the evidence: 'AuDHD' is a useful community word for having both autism and ADHD, which is common and can delay diagnosis, but it isn't a separate diagnosis.

Sources
  • Antshel 2019
  • Yerys 2022
  • Kentrou 2019
  • Lai 2019

Emotions, mental health & risk

Big feelings, rejection, anxiety, and the risks that make recognition urgent.

Holds up

Mostly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
ADHD cuts years off your life, especially for women.

In UK primary care data (30,039 adults with diagnosed ADHD vs 300,390 matched), estimated life expectancy was 6.78 years shorter for men and 8.64 years for women, which the authors think is likely due to modifiable risk factors and unmet support needs (O'Nions 2025). A Danish cohort found about double the mortality rate, mainly from accidents, and a higher relative risk in women (MRR 2.85) than men (1.27) (Dalsgaard 2015). Starting ADHD medication was linked to lower 2-year mortality (HR 0.79; Li 2024).

Closer to the evidence: Adults diagnosed with ADHD die earlier on average, largely from preventable causes like accidents, and treatment and support appear to reduce that risk; it's an average, not a personal forecast.

Sources
  • O'Nions 2025
  • Dalsgaard 2015
  • Li 2024

Mostly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic people are up to seven times more likely to die by suicide than the general population, and nine times if they have no learning disability.

These figures are widely shared from charity summaries. Registry studies agree suicide risk is clearly raised, but the size depends on the country and on co-occurring conditions. In Denmark, autistic people had 3.75 times the suicide rate, and more than 90% of those who attempted or died by suicide had another psychiatric diagnosis (Kõlves 2021). In Sweden, risk was highest in autism without intellectual disability plus ADHD (suicide OR 13.09), and adjusting for psychiatric comorbidity reduced the risks (Hirvikoski 2020). In England, evidence of autism was found in 10.8% of suicide deaths against 1.1% expected (Cassidy 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Autistic people, especially those without intellectual disability, have a several-fold higher suicide risk, much of it tied to co-occurring depression, anxiety and ADHD.

Sources
  • Kõlves 2021
  • Hirvikoski 2020
  • Cassidy 2022

Mostly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic people make up about 1% of the population but about 11% of suicides.

One English study of suicide deaths found evidence of autism in 10.8% against 1.1% expected (OR 11.08, 95% CI 3.92-31.31), rising to 41.4% in an interview subsample when possible undiagnosed autism was counted (Cassidy 2022). The figure is accurate for that study, but it is one study with wide confidence intervals.

Closer to the evidence: In one English study, evidence of autism was about ten times more common among people who died by suicide than expected.

Sources
  • Cassidy 2022

Mostly true   Established

Popular claim, paraphrased
Big emotions and emotional dysregulation are part of ADHD.

Across 13 studies (N=2,535), adults with ADHD had much higher emotion dysregulation than controls (g=1.17), strongest for emotional lability (Beheshti 2020); in 77 youth studies the effect for emotional reactivity was d=.95 (Graziano 2016). Emotional symptoms are common and impairing, but there is no consensus on how to define them and they are not an official diagnostic criterion (Faraone 2018). Medication eases emotional lability about half as much as core symptoms (SMD -0.41 vs -0.8; Moukhtarian 2017).

Closer to the evidence: Emotional dysregulation is common and impairing in ADHD, though it isn't an official criterion and medication usually helps it less than it helps attention.

Sources
  • Beheshti 2020
  • Graziano 2016
  • Faraone 2018
  • Moukhtarian 2017
  • Lenzi 2018

Partly right

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic children with an IQ of 120 or more are almost six times more likely to have suicidal thoughts, and this likely applies to twice-exceptional adults too.

The child finding is reported accurately. In a clinic for gifted youth, parent-reported suicidal ideation was raised in autistic youth with IQ >= 120 (OR 5.9), and higher polygenic scores for cognitive performance went with more suicidal thoughts (Casten 2023). The extension to adults has not been tested. In the general population, high intelligence goes with less anxiety and PTSD (Williams 2023) and lower childhood IQ predicts more adult depression and anxiety (Koenen 2009). That makes the autism-specific finding notable but in need of replication.

Closer to the evidence: One study found that bright autistic children were more likely than other autistic children to have had suicidal thoughts; whether this holds in adults is unknown.

Sources
  • Casten 2023
  • Williams 2022
  • Koenen 2009

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Capable, low-maintenance children are easy to emotionally neglect, and this kind of neglect, an absence of attunement rather than an event, has lasting adult effects.

The adult-outcome part is well supported. Neglect was associated with depression (OR 2.11) and suicide attempts (OR 1.95) in a meta-analysis of 124 studies (Norman 2012). Emotional neglect was among the forms of maltreatment most strongly linked to depression across 192 samples (Humphreys 2020). The 'capable children are more often missed' part is a reasonable clinical observation but has not been studied. The term 'childhood emotional neglect' as a popular self-help concept comes from Jonice Webb's books.

Closer to the evidence: Emotional neglect, even without abuse, is linked to adult depression; whether capable children are more often overlooked is plausible but unstudied.

Sources
  • Norman 2012
  • Humphreys 2020

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Chronic stress dysregulates dopamine and blunts 'wanting' (anticipatory pleasure) more than enjoyment itself, which is why high-functioning people quietly lose desire.

A leading model proposes that anhedonia arises from stress disrupting brain reward systems, based largely on animal evidence that stress impairs dopamine pathways for incentive motivation (Pizzagalli 2014). The specific claim that wanting is affected more than enjoying is plausible but was not checked against abstracts in this library. The writing usually pairs this with advice to see a doctor, which is appropriate because anhedonia is a core symptom of depression.

Closer to the evidence: Long-term stress may dampen motivation and the pull of rewards; loss of interest is also a core sign of depression and worth getting checked.

Sources
  • Pizzagalli 2014

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Up to 66% of autistic adults have considered suicide.

The 66% comes from 374 adults newly diagnosed at one English specialist clinic, where 66% reported lifetime suicidal ideation and 35% plans or attempts (OR 9.6 for ideation vs the general population) (Cassidy 2014). A meta-analysis of 36 studies of autistic and possibly autistic people without intellectual disability found pooled lifetime ideation of 34.2% and attempts/behaviours of 24.3% (Newell 2023). 'Up to' is accurate for late-diagnosed clinic samples, but the typical figure is closer to one in three.

Closer to the evidence: About a third of autistic adults without intellectual disability report having thought about suicide; rates reach two-thirds in some samples of adults diagnosed late.

Sources
  • Cassidy 2014
  • Newell 2023

Overstated

Overstated   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic children are 28 times more likely to think about or attempt suicide (15% vs 0.5% in one study).

This figure is widely shared from a charity statistics page. The '28 times' figure comes from a single clinic study using parent report, which is not in this library. A meta-analysis of 47 papers on autistic youth found pooled prevalence of 25.2% for suicidal ideation and 8.3% for attempts. Self-reports gave higher estimates than parent reports, and heterogeneity was substantial (O'Halloran 2022). Risk is clearly raised, but a single multiplier from one study overstates how precise the evidence is.

Closer to the evidence: About one in four autistic young people have had suicidal thoughts, well above non-autistic peers; childhood adversity raises the risk and resilience lowers it.

Sources
  • O'Halloran 2022

Not supported

Unsupported   Unsupported

Popular claim, paraphrased
People-pleasing (or 'fawning') is an ADHD trait.

No peer-reviewed study has looked at people-pleasing-type constructs (sociotropy, self-silencing, fear of negative evaluation) in ADHD. What is documented is that anxiety is common with ADHD (pooled OR 5.0; Hartman 2023), that adults with ADHD often feel criticised (162 adults; Beaton 2022), and that self-esteem is lower in most controlled studies (Pedersen 2024).

Closer to the evidence: Some people with ADHD describe people-pleasing, perhaps after years of criticism or alongside anxiety, but it hasn't been studied as an ADHD trait.

Popular claim, paraphrased
Rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD) is a core part of ADHD that almost everyone with ADHD has.

'Rejection sensitive dysphoria' has no peer-reviewed validation and no questionnaire of its own. Closer to the evidence: Many people with ADHD describe intense pain around rejection and criticism; this fits the well-documented emotional difficulties in ADHD, but 'RSD' itself hasn't been tested as a distinct symptom.

Popular claim, paraphrased
The average life expectancy of autistic people is just 54 years.

This figure is widely shared from charity summaries. Premature mortality is real; the figure of 54 is not a life expectancy.

Popular claim, paraphrased
What gets diagnosed as generalised anxiety in bright or neurodivergent people is often accurate early pattern recognition, not excessive worry.

It is true that the GAD criteria do not ask whether a worry turns out to be accurate, and the idea is usually offered with fair caveats about false alarms. But the direct evidence runs the other way. In 29 people with GAD, 91.4% of recorded worries did not come true, and people who saw this most clearly improved most in treatment (LaFreniere 2020). In UK Biobank, high intelligence went with less generalised anxiety, not more (Williams 2022). No study has tested whether anxious gifted, autistic or ADHD adults forecast threats more accurately.

Closer to the evidence: Some worries do pick up real problems early, but in generalised anxiety most predicted bad outcomes don't happen; finding uncertainty hard to bear, rather than unusual foresight, is the better-supported explanation.

PMDD & the menstrual cycle

What PMDD is and isn't, how it's diagnosed, and how the cycle affects ADHD.

Holds up

Mostly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Doctors dismiss PMDD.

In an Australian survey of 267 people with PMDD, participants had seen an average of 5.1 providers seeking a diagnosis; 54% reported medical gaslighting, 56% misdiagnosis, and only 19% were diagnosed in line with DSM recommendations (Border 2026). Interviews with 27 women in nine countries described medical gas-lighting and diagnostic odysseys (Mosalisa 2026), and UK women described 'lost decades' of misdiagnosis (Osborn 2020). In 2,512 survey respondents, gynaecologists and psychiatrists were rated highest on PMDD knowledge (Hantsoo 2022). A scoping review lists limited provider knowledge and guideline non-adherence among barriers (Nayak 2025). All samples are self-selected and may over-represent people with poor experiences, so they show the problem is common, not how often it happens overall. Not every clinical disagreement is dismissal: a clinician may reasonably ask for prospective ratings, or consider PME or another diagnosis.

Closer to the evidence: Many people with PMDD report being dismissed or misdiagnosed, often for years. Bringing daily symptom ratings, asking what the clinician would need to confirm or rule out PMDD, asking for their reasoning to be recorded, and seeking a second opinion or referral to a gynaecologist or psychiatrist who knows PMDD can help.

Popular claim, paraphrased
PMDD seriously raises suicide risk.

A systematic review (13 studies; 10 meta-analysed) found that, compared with women without premenstrual disturbances, women with PMDD had higher odds of suicide attempt (odds ratio 6.97, 95% CI 2.98-16.29) and suicidal ideation (odds ratio 3.95, 2.97-5.24); the authors support routine suicide-risk assessment in moderate-to-severe premenstrual disturbance (Prasad 2021). In a Swedish nationwide matched cohort, 67,748 women with premenstrual disorders (identified from inpatient/outpatient diagnoses and drug dispensing) were compared with 338,740 matched women over a mean 6.2 years: all-cause mortality was not raised (adjusted HR 0.91, 0.82-1.02) but death by suicide was (HR 1.92, 1.43-2.60), and higher in those diagnosed before age 25 (HR 3.84, 1.18-12.45, a wide interval) (Opatowski 2024). These are group-level associations from observational data and cannot predict an individual's risk.

Closer to the evidence: PMDD is linked to substantially higher odds of suicidal thoughts and attempts at a group level; that can't predict any one person's risk, but it is a reason to ask directly and get help early. If you are having suicidal thoughts, get help now (see ../help.html).

Popular claim, paraphrased

Overstated   Established

Popular claim, paraphrased
If your ADHD, anxiety or depression gets worse before your period, that's PMDD.

Worsening of an existing condition before menstruation is called premenstrual exacerbation (PME); around 60% of women with mood disorders report it, and treatments that help PMDD show less or no benefit for it, from sparse and mostly small studies (Kuehner 2021). Prospective daily ratings, for example DRSP ratings scored with the C-PASS, establish whether the PMDD pattern is met; telling PMDD from PME, or recognising both, still needs a clinician's judgement (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). In 28 outpatients with suicidal thoughts, 93% had their peak around menstruation, yet none met full PMDD criteria (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Premenstrual worsening of ADHD, anxiety or depression is often premenstrual exacerbation (PME), which differs from PMDD; daily tracking plus a clinician's judgement helps tell them apart, and care or urgent help doesn't need to wait for it.

False   Established

Popular claim, paraphrased
A hormone blood test (or a one-off quiz) can tell you if you have PMDD.

PMDD is confirmed with prospective daily symptom ratings over at least two cycles; the C-PASS, one standard scoring system, matched expert diagnosis with an estimated 98% correct classification in 200 women, and retrospective reports of premenstrual worsening poorly predicted the prospective diagnosis (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). Hormone levels are typically normal in PMDD; symptoms reflect an abnormal response to normal hormone change (Schmidt 1998), so blood tests cannot diagnose it. A clinician can still assess someone provisionally and discuss care while they track, and urgent help never waits for a chart.

Closer to the evidence: Blood tests and one-off questionnaires can't confirm PMDD; prospective daily tracking over at least two cycles confirms the pattern, and a clinician can assess you and offer care while you track.

PMDD treatment

What the trials show for SSRIs, the pill, surgery and supplements.

Holds up

Mostly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
SSRIs work for PMDD within days, unlike for depression.

Sertraline's benefit appeared swiftly in the first month of treatment (Freeman 2004), and luteal-only sertraline already separated from placebo by the first treatment cycle (50% vs 26% responders) (Halbreich 2002). The 2024 Cochrane review found luteal-phase-only SSRIs beat placebo (SMD -0.39, moderate certainty), consistent with a fast onset, though continuous dosing was probably more effective on average (Jespersen 2024). No trial reports a day-by-day onset, and in Freeman 2004 placebo had caught up by the third month.

Closer to the evidence: SSRIs often start helping PMDD within the first cycle, faster than is usual for depression, which is why luteal-only dosing can work; daily dosing was more effective on average, and the schedule is decided with a prescriber.

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Calcium and vitamin B6 help PMDD.

In a multicentre RCT of 466 analysed women with moderate-to-severe PMS, calcium reduced luteal symptoms by 48% vs 30% with placebo by the third cycle (Thys-Jacobs 1998). A systematic review of 9 B6 trials (940 patients) found benefit (OR 2.32) but low trial quality (Wyatt 1999). A review of 31 nutrition trials found consistent benefits for B6, calcium and zinc on psychological PMS symptoms, though only 1 trial had low risk of bias (Robinson 2024). All of this is PMS, not confirmed PMDD; experts list calcium as warranting further exploration (Nevatte 2013). Safety: NHS advice says large amounts of supplemental vitamin B6 can cause peripheral neuropathy (loss of feeling in arms and legs), usually reversible on stopping but occasionally permanent.

Closer to the evidence: Calcium and vitamin B6 have some of the better supplement evidence for PMS, but they haven't been tested in diagnosed PMDD and are not substitutes for proven treatments; high-dose B6 can damage nerves, so check with a pharmacist or clinician.

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Taking an SSRI only in the two weeks before your period (or only on symptom days) works as well as taking it every day.

Both schedules have evidence. In the 2024 Cochrane review (34 RCTs), SSRIs beat placebo when taken continuously (SMD -0.69; 7 studies, 1055 participants) and in the luteal phase only (SMD -0.39; 6 studies, 687 participants), both moderate-certainty evidence; the review concluded SSRIs are probably more effective when taken continuously (P = 0.03 for subgroup difference) (Jespersen 2024). That is a between-subgroup comparison of placebo-controlled trials, so it describes an average, not what suits each person. In a three-arm RCT that compared schedules directly, luteal-phase sertraline did not differ from continuous dosing, and both beat placebo; women with symptoms after their period ended did worse on either schedule (Freeman 2004). Luteal sertraline beat placebo in 281 women with PMDD (responders 50% vs 26% by cycle 1) (Halbreich 2002). Symptom-onset dosing missed its primary outcome (P = .06), though it helped on depression and irritability scales (Yonkers 2015). The 2013 version of the Cochrane review had found no clear difference between schedules (Marjoribanks 2013); it is superseded by the 2024 update.

Closer to the evidence: Taking an SSRI only in the luteal phase does help PMDD, but the 2024 Cochrane review found daily dosing probably works better on average; starting only when symptoms begin has weaker evidence. Which schedule suits you is decided with your prescriber.

Popular claim, paraphrased
The pill treats PMDD.

Drospirenone-containing combined pills have the best evidence: the 2023 Cochrane review (5 RCTs, 858 women, most with PMDD) found they may improve overall premenstrual symptoms (SMD -0.41; low-quality evidence) and functional impairment, with effects that may be small to moderate; placebo also had a significant effect, and they may increase adverse effects (OR 2.31) and withdrawal for adverse effects (OR 3.41). In the larger trial the number needed to treat was 8 (Yonkers 2005; also Pearlstein 2005). Across 9 trials, combined pills improved overall premenstrual symptoms (SMD 0.41) but not depressive symptoms specifically (de Wit 2021). A continuous levonorgestrel pill gave mixed results (Halbreich 2012; Freeman 2012). Clinical guidance places the pill second line after SSRIs (Wagner-Schuman 2023). Combined pills carry a small blood-clot risk and are not suitable for everyone (for example migraine with aura, or age 35 or over and smoking), per NHS information.

Closer to the evidence: Some combined pills, especially those containing drospirenone, may reduce PMDD symptoms modestly, more for overall than mood symptoms; they aren't safe for everyone (clot risk, migraine with aura, smoking at 35+), and a pill can also worsen mood for some women.

Closer to the evidence: Removing the ovaries (usually with the womb, followed by HRT) ended cyclical symptoms in small, uncontrolled series of carefully selected women whom other treatments had failed; a hysterectomy that keeps the ovaries has not been shown to treat PMDD, and surgery is a last resort.

ADHD medication

How ADHD medication behaves in women and across the cycle.

Partly right

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
ADHD medications work differently in women.

A systematic review of 21 studies found at least one sex difference in each of 14 studies of medication effects, but the differences were inconsistent and need confirmation (Kok 2020). Small studies suggest sex-specific effects, such as QT prolongation on atomoxetine only in women (24 patients; Suzuki 2017) and appetite suppression by methylphenidate in women but not obese men in adults without ADHD (Davis 2012). In 1 229 972 new users, discontinuation patterns were similar across sex (Brikell 2024), and in Denmark women stayed on medication longer than men (5-year persistence 29% vs 23.5%; Ishøy 2024).

Closer to the evidence: There are hints that women respond to and tolerate ADHD medicines somewhat differently, but the evidence is thin and inconsistent; the same medicines work for women, and dose and choice should be tailored individually.

Midlife & menopause

Perimenopause and menopause with ADHD or PMDD.

Partly right

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Perimenopause makes ADHD much worse.

In 5,392 Icelandic women, severe perimenopausal symptoms were more common with self-reported ADHD (54.2% vs 30.1%), with the gap largest at ages 35-39 (Jakobsdottir Smari 2025). Closer to the evidence: Women with ADHD report more severe perimenopause symptoms, but how menopause changes ADHD itself, and how best to treat it, is barely studied.

Burnout & energy

Burnout, exhaustion and the claims about cells and mitochondria.

Popular claim, paraphrased

Popular claim, paraphrased
Recovering from neurodivergent burnout needs not rest or less stimulation, but permission to do things the neurodivergent way.

Autistic adults linked recovery to acceptance and social support, time off/reduced expectations, and doing things in an autistic way/unmasking (Raymaker 2020). A systematic review of 48 studies found rest, solitude and sensory relief, self-understanding and support helped (Ali 2025). Stimming was described as self-regulation (Kapp 2019), and motivated special interests went with higher wellbeing while very intense engagement went with lower wellbeing (Grove 2018). No intervention trial exists.

Closer to the evidence: Autistic adults say recovery takes real rest and sensory relief plus permission to do things their way, including unmasking, stimming and interests they enjoy.

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Adults with ADHD show higher markers of oxidative cell damage and lower antioxidant defences.

The first meta-analysis (6 studies) found an oxidative stress association that lost significance after correction, and normal antioxidant status (Joseph 2015). A 2026 meta-analysis of 20 studies in children and adolescents found higher blood total oxidant status (SMD 0.94) and urinary 8-OHdG (0.21) and lower total antioxidant status (-0.53), but no difference in blood 8-OHdG, MDA, NO or SOD (Suwindi 2026). Adult data are a single study of 60 adults showing lower antioxidant-regulating proteins (Gürbüzer 2026).

Closer to the evidence: In children with ADHD some oxidative stress markers are higher and overall antioxidant capacity slightly lower; evidence in adults is very limited.

Unsupported   Unsupported

Popular claim, paraphrased
ADHD burnout is a recognised condition.

There is no validated 'ADHD burnout' construct and no prevalence data. In 171 employees, those with ADHD reported more job burnout, explained by executive-function difficulties (Turjeman-Levi 2024), and 62% of adults with ADHD in a clinic study met criteria for significant fatigue (Rogers 2016). ICD-11 classes burn-out as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition.

Closer to the evidence: Many adults with ADHD describe cycles of exhaustion and job burnout, but 'ADHD burnout' isn't a defined or diagnosable condition.

Unsupported   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Existing in a neurotypical world costs ADHD and autistic adults more energy; your cells keep the score.

No study has measured energy expenditure in neurodivergent adults. An energetic model of allostatic load proposes that chronic stress adds an energetic burden that competes with repair, as a testable model (Bobba-Alves 2022). A hypothesis paper extends this to autism, proposing camouflaging as chronic stress leading to mitochondrial allostatic load and burnout, and notes these mechanisms are largely unexplored (Mahony 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Researchers have proposed that chronic stress has an energy cost and that this may apply to neurodivergent people in environments that don't fit them, but it hasn't been measured.

Mitochondria under strain (more lactate, fewer antioxidants) are central to neurodivergent burnout.

No study has measured mitochondria in autistic, ADHD or occupational burnout. A systematic review found 23 experimental stress-mitochondria studies, all in male laboratory animals, and only 6 observational human studies (Picard & McEwen 2018). In humans, mothers caring for an autistic child had lower leukocyte mitochondrial health than control mothers (Picard 2018). Linking this to autistic burnout is a hypothesis (Mahony 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Chronic stress may affect mitochondria, but no study has yet looked at mitochondria in burnout.

The body: gut, immune, iron & POTS

Allergies, histamine, the gut, endometriosis, iron and POTS.

Holds up

Mostly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
ADHD and IBS go together.

A meta-analysis of 23 studies (over 1.9 million people) linked ADHD with IBS (OR 1.58) and constipation (OR 1.97), and constipation; studies of diagnosed organic gut disease were excluded, so that question wasn't tested (Blake 2026). In females, Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis were also linked to ADHD in Norwegian registers (Hegvik 2018). Young adults with ADHD in an Israeli conscript cohort had more IBS (OR 1.67) but not IBD or coeliac disease (Kedem 2020). There is no meta-analysis specific to adult women.

Closer to the evidence: IBS and constipation are more common with ADHD; these are functional gut problems, and the link doesn't mean ADHD causes gut disease.

  • Blake 2026
  • Kedem 2020
  • McKeown 2013
  • Hegvik 2018

Mostly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Endometriosis often gets misdiagnosed as IBS.

Women with endometriosis were 3.5 times more likely to have had an IBS diagnosis beforehand, and 2.5 times more likely to receive a new IBS diagnosis even after endometriosis was confirmed (Seaman 2008). In 171 women, median time from symptoms to diagnosis was 10.4 years and 74% received at least one false diagnosis (Hudelist 2012). The two conditions also genuinely overlap (summary RR 2.39; Saidi 2020), and the ESHRE guideline challenges laparoscopy as the only gold-standard test (Becker 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Endometriosis is often first labelled as IBS and takes years to diagnose; the two can also occur together, so ongoing pelvic and bowel pain deserves a gynaecological look.

Mostly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Low ferritin can leave you exhausted even if you're not anaemic, and iron helps.

In 198 menstruating women with ferritin below 50 and normal haemoglobin, 12 weeks of iron reduced fatigue by 47.7% vs 28.8% on placebo (Vaucher 2012); an earlier trial found benefit only when ferritin was 50 or lower (Verdon 2003). A meta-analysis of 18 trials confirmed less self-reported fatigue (SMD -0.38) but no gain in objective physical capacity (Houston 2018). Iron deficiency is common: 38.6% of US females aged 12-21 had ferritin below 25 (Weyand 2023, verified against full text).

Closer to the evidence: Iron deficiency without anaemia is common in menstruating women and can cause fatigue that improves with iron; ask for ferritin, not just haemoglobin.

Mostly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
POTS is mostly a women's condition.

More than 75% of patients are female, and most present between 15 and 25 years (Sheldon 2015); a typical patient is a woman of child-bearing age whose symptoms often start in adolescence (Vernino 2021). In 4835 people with diagnosed POTS from an online survey, 94% were female (Shaw 2019), though this sample was self-selected. Trial samples are similarly mostly women (Taub 2021: 95.5%).

Closer to the evidence: Most people diagnosed with POTS are young women, but men get it too, and why women are more affected is not fully understood.

Mostly true   Established

Popular claim, paraphrased
People with ADHD are more likely to have allergies, asthma and eczema.

An umbrella review rated the link with childhood eczema (OR 1.31) as 'convincing' and with asthma (OR 1.51) as 'highly suggestive' (Kim 2020). The ADHD-asthma link held after adjusting for family factors (OR 1.45; Cortese 2018). Food allergy was not associated (OR 1.13, not significant; Miyazaki 2017), the associations are modest, and most data are from children.

Closer to the evidence: Asthma and eczema are modestly more common in people with ADHD; this is a link, not evidence that allergies cause ADHD, and food allergy shows no link.

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Antihistamines make ADHD brain fog worse.

First-generation (sedating) antihistamines impair attention, working memory and vigilance, with next-day carryover, while second-generation ones largely do not (Kay 2000). In a driving simulator, diphenhydramine 50 mg impaired driving more than alcohol at about 0.1% BAC, and people's own sense of drowsiness did not predict this (Weiler 2000). Antihistamines have not been studied in adults with ADHD; in children with eczema, prior antihistamine use was linked to more ADHD symptoms (OR 1.88; Schmitt 2018).

Closer to the evidence: Older sedating antihistamines like diphenhydramine impair attention in everyone and may add to ADHD difficulties; newer non-drowsy ones generally don't.

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Endometriosis and ADHD are linked.

One large Swedish register study found a two-way link: endometriosis was followed by a higher ADHD risk (sibling-adjusted HR up to 1.98), and earlier ADHD also predicted later endometriosis (Gao 2020). Endometriosis is also linked to depression (OR 3.61) and anxiety (OR 2.61) in UK Biobank (Koller 2023). This rests mainly on one study, and the mechanism is unknown.

Closer to the evidence: One large study found endometriosis and ADHD occur together more than expected; it's an early finding worth more research.

  • Gao 2020

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
POTS gets worse before your period.

The menstrual cycle does seem to affect POTS symptoms, but the small studies point to the period itself, not the days before. Women with POTS (65) reported more lightheadedness than controls in every phase, worst during menses (Peggs 2012). In 10 women with POTS, standing tolerance was poorer and presyncope more common in the early follicular phase than the mid-luteal phase (Fu 2010). Another study of 10 women found no phase difference in heart rate, sympathetic nerve activity or presyncope (Stickford 2015). No study has looked specifically at the premenstrual (late-luteal) days.

Closer to the evidence: Many women with POTS notice symptoms change across the cycle; the small studies so far suggest lightheadedness tends to be worst during the period itself.

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Research shows autism and ADHD express themselves in the body, through fibromyalgia, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, GI distress, POTS, migraines and chronic pain.

This is often presented as established by research. Associations are real for some conditions. People with EDS have higher rates of autism (RR 7.4) and ADHD (Cederlöf 2016). 51% of neurodivergent adults in one clinic sample had joint hypermobility vs 20% in the general population, and hypermobility mediated links to orthostatic intolerance and pain (Csecs 2022). Young autistic adults report more seizures, depression, hypertension and allergies (Fortuna 2015). However, a school sample found no link between hypermobility and neurodevelopmental problems (Glans 2025). The evidence shows co-occurrence, not that these conditions are expressions of neurodivergence.

Closer to the evidence: Autism and ADHD co-occur more often than expected with some physical conditions, notably hypermobility/EDS and related pain and dizziness, but the reasons are not yet known.

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Low iron (ferritin) causes ADHD.

In children, ferritin is modestly lower in ADHD (SMD -0.40 and g=-0.25 in two meta-analyses) but serum iron does not differ (Wang 2017; Tseng 2018). A widely cited early finding of ferritin below 30 in 84% of children with ADHD (Konofal 2004) has not held up at that size, and the only placebo-controlled iron trial in ADHD had 23 children (Konofal 2008). Adult data are almost absent.

Closer to the evidence: Ferritin is slightly lower on average in children with ADHD; low iron is worth checking and treating, but it doesn't cause ADHD.

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Salt and water cure POTS.

People with POTS can have low blood volume without the expected hormone response (Raj 2005), which is the rationale for fluids and salt. But consensus rates 2-3 L water and 10-12 g salt daily as a weak (Class IIb) recommendation, while a structured exercise programme is rated higher (Class IIa) (Sheldon 2015). Across 28 mostly small studies, volume, vascular, heart-rate and exercise treatments each helped 51%-72% of patients, and the evidence was called 'extremely limited' (Wells 2018). In a small trial, exercise training improved quality of life where propranolol did not (Fu 2011). Most adolescents improve over years, but only 19% reported complete resolution (Bhatia 2016).

Closer to the evidence: Extra fluids and salt are a sensible first step for many people with POTS, alongside graded exercise and other measures, but they help rather than cure.

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Stimulants are dangerous if you have POTS.

ADHD medicines raise pulse and blood pressure on average: in adults by 4.37 bpm (methylphenidate) to 5.8 bpm (viloxazine) in short-term trials, with stimulants no worse than atomoxetine or viloxazine (Farhat 2025; Idrees 2023). In large observational studies, ADHD medication was not significantly associated with cardiovascular disease, though a modest rise in arrhythmia risk could not be ruled out (Zhang 2022). In POTS specifically, a single dose of atomoxetine raised standing heart rate (121 vs 105 bpm) and worsened symptoms (Green 2013), modafinil did not raise standing heart rate (Kpaeyeh 2014), and in an uncontrolled series 14 of 18 patients with refractory POTS reported improvement on methylphenidate (Kanjwal 2012). The Heart Rhythm Society advises against norepinephrine-reuptake-blocking drugs in POTS (Sheldon 2015). There are no trials of stimulants in people with both ADHD and POTS.

Closer to the evidence: ADHD medicines can raise heart rate a little, and atomoxetine worsened POTS in a short trial; whether a stimulant is suitable for someone with POTS is an individual decision with their doctor, and the evidence is thin.

Popular claim, paraphrased
ADHD is caused by gut bacteria, and probiotics can treat it.

Reviews describe ADHD microbiome findings as heterogeneous with no consistent differences (Bundgaard-Nielsen 2020; Gkougka 2022), and a multi-cohort adult analysis found large differences between cohorts (Jakobi 2024). Across psychiatric disorders, microbiome changes are mostly shared rather than specific to one disorder (Nikolova 2021). No probiotic trial in ADHD met the library's inclusion bar.

Closer to the evidence: Gut-brain research is interesting, but no consistent ADHD microbiome has been found and probiotics aren't a proven ADHD treatment.

Popular claim, paraphrased
ADHD, POTS, hypermobile EDS and MCAS are really one syndrome.

Some overlaps are real but partial. About 31% of 91 people with POTS met hEDS criteria (Miller 2020), and neurodivergent adults were more often hypermobile, which was linked to orthostatic symptoms (Csecs 2022). In autistic people referred to autonomic clinics, most had an autonomic diagnosis and most of those had hEDS (Owens 2021). But a review found no original study combining hEDS, POTS and MCAS and no shared mechanism (Kucharik 2019), and a 2025 systematic review found no study meeting strict diagnostic criteria for mast cell disorders in POTS or EDS (Farley 2025). Mast-cell markers were abnormal in 29 of 69 POTS patients in one clinic, but single tests are not diagnostic (Kohno 2021). No study has examined ADHD together with all three.

Closer to the evidence: Hypermobility, POTS and neurodivergence overlap more than expected, but they are separate conditions; a shared syndrome, and especially a mast-cell link, has not been shown.

Histamine intolerance or 'high histamine' causes or worsens ADHD.

Popular claim, paraphrased
Low iron (ferritin) causes POTS, and iron fixes it.

In 24 adolescents with POTS, half had low iron stores (ferritin 25 μg/L or less) vs 14% in the US paediatric population (Jarjour 2013). People with POTS can have reduced red-cell and plasma volume (Raj 2005). No controlled study or treatment trial tests whether correcting iron improves POTS.

Popular claim, paraphrased
MCAS (mast cell activation syndrome) drives ADHD and neurodivergence.

There are no ADHD-specific peer-reviewed data on mast cells. The main source is a hypothesis paper about a subgroup of autism based largely on parent-reported 'allergic-like' symptoms (Theoharides 2012). Consensus criteria require typical symptoms, a documented rise in tryptase or other mast-cell mediators, and response to mast-cell treatment (Valent 2012).

Closer to the evidence: There is no evidence that MCAS causes ADHD; MCAS is a specific diagnosis that needs lab-confirmed mediator rises, not just symptoms.

Unsupported   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
POTS brain fog is really undiagnosed ADHD (or the same thing).

Concentration problems are near-universal in POTS: 94% of 4835 survey respondents (Shaw 2019) and 132 of 138 in a brain-fog survey, worsened by standing, fatigue and dehydration (Ross 2013). Attention and executive function were worse while standing in POTS than in controls (Miller 2020). People with POTS scored higher than controls on ADHD inattention scales, but these symptoms were not present in childhood, which ADHD requires (Raj V 2009). No study reports how often ADHD and POTS co-occur.

Closer to the evidence: POTS often causes attention and memory problems that can look like ADHD; ADHD is a lifelong pattern starting in childhood, so new 'brain fog' with POTS needs a careful history before assuming ADHD.

False   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
POTS is just anxiety.

In a small physiology study, heart rate rose 39 bpm in POTS vs 19 bpm in controls during venous pooling, sham pooling caused no rise, and heart-rate responses to mental stress were the same; anxiety scores were higher in POTS but were unrelated to the heart-rate response (Masuki 2007). In 21 patients with POTS, lifetime anxiety disorders, including panic disorder, were not more common than in the general population (Raj V 2009). Raised worry in POTS is focused on body sensations rather than trauma or social anxiety (Owens 2017). The Heart Rhythm Society consensus states the tachycardia is not caused by anxiety (Sheldon 2015).

Closer to the evidence: POTS is a physical problem with heart-rate and blood-volume regulation; people with POTS may feel anxious about their symptoms, but anxiety does not cause the racing heart.

  • Masuki 2007
  • Raj 2009

Sleep, senses & daily life

Body clock, body signals, time, clutter and nail biting.

Partly right

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
'Time blindness' is an ADHD symptom that explains why you're always late.

Across 55 studies, people with ADHD had more trouble discriminating very short durations, more variable estimates of multi-second intervals, and errors consistent with a faster internal clock (Marx 2022). Timing deficits appear independent of other executive functions and are reduced by methylphenidate (Noreika 2013). But these are lab tasks, mostly in young people; the link to everyday lateness has not been rigorously studied.

Closer to the evidence: People with ADHD tend to judge time less accurately in lab tests, which may help explain lost time and lateness, though everyday lateness itself is rarely studied.

  • Marx 2022
  • Noreika 2013

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Adults with ADHD show reduced interoception, so they miss the signs of burnout.

A systematic review of 18 studies found 3 of 5 case-control studies showed reduced interoception in ADHD, with moderate quality and small samples (Bruton 2025). In 30 adults with ADHD vs 57 controls, heartbeat-counting accuracy, confidence and self-reported accuracy were lower after controlling for alexithymia and autistic traits (Göz Tebrizcik 2025); earlier small studies disagreed (Kutscheidt 2019; Wiersema 2018). No study links interoception to recognising burnout.

Closer to the evidence: Small studies suggest adults with ADHD may be less accurate at sensing body signals; whether this delays noticing burnout hasn't been studied.

Popular claim, paraphrased
Doom piles (clutter you can't deal with) are an ADHD thing.

'Doom piles' are not studied as such, but hoarding is. About 20% of adults in an ADHD clinic had clinically significant hoarding vs 2% of matched controls, linked specifically to inattention (Morein-Zamir 2022); hoarding disorder affected 32.1% of adults with ADHD vs 8% with OCD and 4% of controls (Grassi 2023). In a European population survey, childhood inattention was associated with lifetime hoarding symptoms (OR 6.04) (Fullana 2013), and people who hoard report more ADHD symptoms (Hartl 2005). Samples are small, and hoarding is more than ordinary clutter.

Closer to the evidence: Clutter and difficulty sorting and discarding are more common with ADHD, especially inattention, and can reach the level of hoarding; 'doom piles' themselves haven't been studied.

Popular claim, paraphrased
Nail biting and skin picking are ADHD traits.

Among 63 children referred for nail biting, 74.6% had ADHD (Ghanizadeh 2008), but that is a clinic sample of children; there are no adult or female epidemiological data on nail biting and ADHD. Among 308 adults with hair-pulling disorder, 15.3% met the ADHD threshold, ADHD did not change pulling severity, and stimulant use did not appear to affect it (Chesivoir 2022). Closer to the evidence: Nail biting and other body-focused repetitive behaviours seem common with ADHD, but adult data are thin, and stimulants don't appear to make them worse.

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
ADHD is really a circadian rhythm (body clock) disorder.

Later chronotype and delayed melatonin onset are consistent findings in ADHD (62 studies; Coogan 2017), and 31 of 40 adults with ADHD had sleep-onset insomnia with a delayed melatonin rhythm (Van Veen 2010). But adult polysomnography shows no objective difference, even though self-reported sleep is worse (Diaz-Roman 2018). The idea that delayed rhythm causes ADHD symptoms applies, as a hypothesis, to a subgroup (Bijlenga 2019).

Closer to the evidence: Many adults with ADHD have a delayed body clock that can worsen symptoms, but ADHD isn't simply a sleep disorder.

Popular claim, paraphrased
Melatonin treats ADHD.

In 51 adults with ADHD and delayed sleep, 0.5 mg melatonin shifted the body clock by 1 h 28 min and reduced ADHD symptoms by 14%, but this disappeared 2 weeks after stopping (van Andel 2021), and without behavioural coaching actual sleep times did not shift (van Andel 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Melatonin can help people with ADHD fall asleep earlier, but it is a sleep aid, not an ADHD treatment.

NAC (N-acetylcysteine) stops nail biting and skin picking.

Popular claim, paraphrased
Morning sleep inertia is longer and more intense in ADHD and autism, so it can take hours to 'come online'.

People with ADHD have poor interoception and can't feel hunger, thirst or the need to pee.

Closer to the evidence: Some people with ADHD say they miss body signals like hunger or thirst, but research on interoception in ADHD is tiny and mixed.
