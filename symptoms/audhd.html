ADHD and autism: features and experiences

Features of ADHD and autism as they can show up in women in their twenties, drawn from diagnostic criteria, questionnaires, research and women's own accounts. A selection from the literature we have reviewed, not a complete list.

Recognising yourself here can be a good reason to ask for an assessment. It is not a diagnosis: many of these experiences also happen with anxiety, depression, trauma, thyroid problems, iron deficiency and other conditions. A diagnosis needs a clinician. Crisis support if you are struggling now.

How diagnosis works, in brief. ADHD and autism are separate diagnoses, each with its own criteria, and many people meet both. Both need features present since childhood (even if they were hidden), showing up across settings and causing real difficulty, judged by a clinician from your history and ideally someone who knew you then. Missing school reports or a childhood informant do not rule a diagnosis out. Many items below are associated experiences or related conditions, not diagnostic criteria. More detail.

157 items: 50 from diagnostic criteria, 18 from validated questionnaires, 77 from research, 12 from women's accounts in qualitative studies. Each shows its evidence grade and sources.

Attention & focus

    • Sending an email to the wrong person or with the attachment missing
    • Getting a calculation wrong at work because a step was skipped, not because the maths was hard
    • Proofreading a document three times and still missing typos that a colleague spots at once

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Inattention to detail / careless mistakes

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A1(a)

    Often hidden by over-checking and perfectionism. Women may spend far longer than peers re-checking work, so the error rate looks normal while the effort cost is not.

    Also seen in: Inattention is not specific to ADHD: it is common in anxiety, depression, trauma/PTSD, sleep deprivation, burnout, thyroid disease, iron deficiency, POTS, substance use and some medications.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Young 2020

    • Re-reading the same paragraph of a journal article several times
    • Losing the thread halfway through a team meeting or webinar
    • Starting a long form and drifting off to something else after a few minutes

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Difficulty sustaining attention

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A1(b)

    In women this is often internal (drifting off quietly) rather than visibly off-task, so teachers and partners may not notice it.

    Also seen in: Inattention is not specific to ADHD: it is common in anxiety, depression, trauma/PTSD, sleep deprivation, burnout, thyroid disease, iron deficiency, POTS, substance use and some medications.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Young 2020, Quinn 2014, Kessler 2005

    • Nodding along and then realising you've missed the question you were asked
    • A partner saying 'you never listen' when you genuinely meant to
    • Missing directions someone has just given you

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Appearing not to listen in direct conversation

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A1(c)

    Often read as rudeness, self-absorption or 'ditziness' in women rather than an attention problem.

    Also seen in: Also consider hearing problems, auditory processing difficulty, anxiety (worry pulling attention inward) and depression.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Young 2020

    • A notification, a noise or a passing thought derails a task completely
    • Opening the laptop to do one thing and ending up somewhere else entirely
    • Finding open-plan offices almost impossible to work in

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    High distractibility (external and internal)

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A1(h)

    In adults, DSM-5-TR includes distraction by unrelated thoughts. This internal distractibility is easy to miss in quiet women.

    Also seen in: Inattention is not specific to ADHD: it is common in anxiety, depression, trauma/PTSD, sleep deprivation, burnout, thyroid disease, iron deficiency, POTS, substance use and some medications.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Mowlem 2016

    • Lying in bed with thoughts racing between unrelated topics
    • Having a song, a to-do list and a conversation all running in your head at once
    • Feeling exhausted by your own thoughts

    ADHD Questionnaire item Supported

    Excessive spontaneous mind wandering

    Very common in inattentive presentations and easy to miss because it is invisible. Often misattributed to anxiety.

    Also seen in: Racing or ruminative thoughts also occur in anxiety, depression and hypomania. In ADHD the content is typically wide-ranging rather than worry-focused.

    Sources: Mowlem 2016, Bozhilova 2018

    • Staring into space and losing minutes without noticing
    • Feeling mentally sluggish and slow to process, especially in the morning
    • Being called 'away with the fairies' or a 'space cadet'

    ADHD Questionnaire item Supported

    Cognitive disengagement syndrome (formerly sluggish cognitive tempo)

    Overlaps with the 'dreamy' inattentive picture often described in girls. Linked to internalising symptoms and poorer sleep in a mostly female adult sample.

    Also seen in: CDS is not a DSM diagnosis. It overlaps with depression, sleep problems, hypothyroidism, anaemia and medication effects, and can occur without ADHD.

    Sources: Becker 2023, Barkley 2012, Becker 2018, Fredrick 2021

    • Spending eight hours on a new hobby or a research rabbit-hole without noticing
    • Missing meals or the toilet because you're absorbed
    • Being unable to drag yourself away from a game or a project to go to bed

    ADHD Questionnaire item Preliminary

    Hyperfocus

    Often used as 'proof' that someone can't have ADHD ('but you can focus for hours'). Attention in ADHD is better described as interest-driven than absent.

    Also seen in: Hyperfocus is not a diagnostic criterion, and measures are new. Autistic monotropic absorption can look similar.

    Sources: Hupfeld 2018, Hupfeld 2024, Ozel-Kizil 2016

    • Doing excellent work under a deadline and nothing for weeks before
    • Handling an emergency calmly and then being unable to do the dishes
    • Being great at a new job for six months, then struggling once it becomes routine

    ADHD Research finding Preliminary

    Context-dependent attention / delay aversion

    Because attention can be excellent in some contexts, women (especially high-achievers) are often told they 'can't' have ADHD.

    Also seen in: This is a widely described pattern but not a diagnostic criterion. The underlying theory (dual-pathway / delay aversion) is a model, not a test.

    Sources: Sonuga-Barke 2003, Jackson 2016, Rommelse 2016

    • Not hearing your name called while absorbed
    • Finding multitasking draining
    • Feeling jolted when interrupted

    Autism Research finding Preliminary

    Monotropic attention

    Also seen in: Monotropism is a theory, not a validated diagnostic feature. ADHD hyperfocus can look similar.

    Sources: Murray 2005

    • Spotting a single typo in a long document instantly
    • Noticing when one picture on the wall is moved
    • Remembering exact details others forget

    Autism Questionnaire item Supported

    Attention to detail

    Also seen in: Attention to detail is common in the general population and is not diagnostic.

    Sources: Baron-Cohen 2001

    • Making a spreadsheet before small decisions
    • Being slow to choose at restaurants
    • Distrusting your first impression

    Autism Research finding Preliminary

    Deliberative reasoning bias

    Also seen in: Small study with an all-male clinical sample.

    Sources: Brosnan 2016

    • Reports saying 'capable of more if she focused'
    • Being the 'dreamy' or 'messy' child
    • Constant reminders to stop talking

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Childhood onset (retrospective signs)

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD B

    Girls with ADHD are less often referred because they are less disruptive. In one large study, girls needed more hyperactivity or conduct problems than boys to get a diagnosis.

    Also seen in: DSM-5-TR requires several symptoms before age 12. School reports and a parent or sibling account help. Truly adult-onset ADHD-like symptoms usually have another explanation (Sibley 2018).

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Mowlem 2018, Quinn 2014, Hinshaw 2021, Martin 2024, Sibley 2018

    • Remembering being always in trouble for talking
    • Frequent tears or tantrums
    • Underachievement relative to ability

    ADHD Questionnaire item Supported

    Retrospective childhood ADHD symptoms (WURS domains)

    Retrospective scales lean towards conduct and temper items, so inattentive girls may score low.

    Also seen in: Recall of childhood is unreliable, and depressed adults also score highly on retrospective scales.

    Sources: Ward 1993, DSM-5-TR (APA 2022)

    • Forgetting the second half of a spoken instruction
    • Losing your train of thought when interrupted
    • Going upstairs three times for the same thing

    ADHD Questionnaire item Supported

    Working memory difficulty (inattention/memory problems)

    Also seen in: Working memory also suffers with anxiety, depression, sleep deprivation, perimenopause and POTS.

    Sources: Erhardt 1999, Kessler 2010, Wilson 2023

Hyperactivity & restlessness

    • Leg bouncing under the desk in meetings
    • Twirling hair or rings, clicking pens
    • Needing to doodle to listen

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Fidgeting

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A2(a)

    Female fidgeting tends to be small and socially acceptable (hair, jewellery, doodling) and is rarely recognised as hyperactivity.

    Also seen in: Anxiety, stimulant or caffeine effects and akathisia from some medications can cause restlessness.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Young 2020, Loyer Carbonneau 2020

    • Making repeated trips to the kitchen or bathroom during a long meeting
    • Standing or pacing while on the phone
    • Struggling through long flights, films or dinners

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Difficulty staying seated

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A2(b)

    Women often channel this into 'helpful' movement such as refilling drinks or tidying, which hides it.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Loyer Carbonneau 2020

    • Feeling 'buzzy' or keyed up while sitting still
    • An urge to move or do something during downtime
    • Finding a lazy Sunday more stressful than a busy one

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Restlessness (often subjective in adults)

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A2(c)

    DSM-5-TR notes that in adults hyperactivity may show only as a feeling of restlessness. This is the typical adult female form, and girls show less overt hyperactivity than boys.

    Also seen in: Anxiety disorders, hyperthyroidism, stimulant use and restless legs syndrome can feel similar.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Kooij 2018, Young 2020, Loyer Carbonneau 2020

    • Scrolling the phone while watching TV while folding laundry
    • Being unable to lie on a beach without a book, podcast or plan
    • Finding meditation unbearable

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Difficulty with quiet leisure

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A2(d)

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022)

    • Packing every weekend with plans
    • Friends calling you 'a lot' or 'high energy'
    • Feeling physically uncomfortable in long, slow situations such as restaurants or waiting rooms

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Constant busyness / difficulty being still

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A2(e)

    Can be mistaken for an energetic, high-achieving personality or for hypomania.

    Also seen in: Bipolar hypomania is episodic with reduced need for sleep and elevated mood. ADHD restlessness is persistent and trait-like.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022)

    • Being told at school that you were 'too chatty'
    • Replaying conversations afterwards and cringing at how much you said
    • Going off on long tangents

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Excessive talking

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A2(f)

    Verbal hyperactivity ('chatty') is a common female form of hyperactivity and is often tolerated or praised rather than recognised.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Young 2020, Quinn 2014

Impulsivity

    • Jumping in with an answer and getting it wrong because the question wasn't finished
    • Saying something tactless and regretting it immediately
    • Finishing a colleague's sentence

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Speaking before thinking

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A2(g)

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022)

    • Abandoning a queue or an online checkout because it's slow
    • Getting very frustrated in traffic
    • Struggling to wait for a reply to a message

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Difficulty waiting

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A2(h)

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Jackson 2016

    • Jumping into conversations you weren't part of
    • Taking over a task from a colleague because they're 'too slow'
    • Using someone's things without asking

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Interrupting or intruding

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A2(i)

    Women often suppress this through intense self-monitoring, which adds to social fatigue.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022)

    • Buying something now instead of saving for the thing you really want
    • Leaving a stable job because it has become dull
    • Picking the faster, worse option just to get it over with

    ADHD Research finding Supported

    Delay discounting / delay aversion

    Also seen in: Steep delay discounting is also seen in substance use disorders and under stress.

    Sources: Jackson 2016, Sonuga-Barke 2003

    • Impulse buys online late at night
    • Paying fines or late fees for forgotten bills
    • Lots of unused gym memberships or subscriptions

    ADHD Research finding Supported

    Financial impulsivity / poor financial management

    Also seen in: Impulsive spending also occurs in hypomania/bipolar disorder, depression (retail therapy) and compulsive buying.

    Sources: Pelham 2020, Daley 2019

    • Resigning in a moment of frustration
    • Moving cities on a whim
    • Committing to a relationship or a big purchase within days

    ADHD Research finding Supported

    Impulsive decision-making

    Also seen in: Impulsivity also features in borderline personality disorder, bipolar disorder and substance use.

    Sources: Biederman 2006, Hinshaw 2021

    • Firing off an email you wish you could unsend
    • Saying something harsh in an argument and apologising later
    • Quitting something in a moment of frustration

    ADHD Research finding Supported

    Emotional impulsivity

    Also seen in: Also characteristic of BPD. In BPD it is typically tied to fear of abandonment and unstable identity (Asherson 2014).

    Sources: Faraone 2018, Shaw 2014, Erhardt 1999, Asherson 2014

Executive function & daily life

    • Half-painted room, half-written thesis chapter, half-unpacked suitcase
    • Starting to tidy the kitchen and ending up reorganising a drawer in another room
    • Many abandoned online courses or hobbies

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Not following through on tasks

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A1(d)

    May be disguised by last-minute all-nighters that get things finished but at high personal cost.

    Also seen in: Low motivation from depression, and fatigue from any cause, can look similar. ADHD is lifelong and not tied to low-mood episodes.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019)

    • Bills, letters and forms piling up unopened
    • A messy, disorganised workspace or inbox despite repeated 'fresh starts'
    • Not knowing where to begin with a multi-step project such as moving house

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Organisational difficulty

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A1(e)

    Many women build elaborate external systems (planners, lists, apps) that work for a while and then collapse. Visible 'organisation' can co-exist with a high internal cost.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Kessler 2010, Canela 2017

    • Leaving a grant application or tax return until the night before
    • Avoiding a long reading assignment even though you're interested in the subject
    • Paying late fees rather than filling in a tedious form

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Avoidance of mentally demanding tasks

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A1(f)

    Often moralised as laziness or lack of willpower, which feeds shame and low self-esteem.

    Also seen in: Depression, anxiety (avoidance) and burnout can produce similar avoidance.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019)

    • Regularly searching for keys or phone before leaving the house
    • Leaving cards, bags or umbrellas in shops and on trains
    • Buying duplicates because the original has vanished

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Frequently misplacing everyday items

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A1(g)

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019)

    • Missing a GP or dentist appointment despite reminders
    • Forgetting a friend's birthday you genuinely care about
    • Forgetting to reply to messages for days or weeks

    ADHD Diagnostic criterion Established

    Everyday forgetfulness

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR ADHD A1(i)

    Women are often socially penalised for forgetting relational tasks (birthdays, replies) and may experience this as a moral failing.

    Also seen in: Forgetfulness also occurs with depression, sleep loss, anxiety, POTS 'brain fog', perimenopause and some medications.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Beaton 2022

    • Spending an hour 'getting ready' to send a two-line email
    • Not booking a haircut for months even though you want one
    • Needing an external deadline or another person present (body doubling) to get going

    ADHD Questionnaire item Supported

    Task initiation / self-motivation deficit (executive function)

    Commonly described by late-diagnosed women as a central, shame-laden difficulty, often interpreted by others as laziness.

    Also seen in: Depression (anhedonia, low energy), burnout and autistic inertia can all look similar. Task-initiation trouble that is lifelong and present when mood is good points more to ADHD.

    Sources: Barkley 2011, Kessler 2010, Silverstein 2018

    • Sitting for an hour unable to get up and shower
    • Being unable to stop a task to eat or sleep
    • Needing a specific trigger or another person to get moving

    Autism Reported by women Preliminary

    Autistic inertia

    Also seen in: Barely studied. Overlaps with ADHD task-initiation difficulty, hyperfocus, depression and catatonia (see claim daily-autistic-inertia-is-real).

    Sources: Ward 2026

    • Struggling to plan a week of meals
    • A simple life admin task taking an entire afternoon
    • Needing help with tasks others think are basic

    Both Research finding Supported

    Executive function difficulties

    Also seen in: Executive difficulties are transdiagnostic (depression, anxiety, burnout, sleep loss, brain injury).

    Sources: Antshel 2019, Barkley 2011, Yerys 2022

    • Thriving at school, then failing modules at university
    • Losing control of bills and meals after moving out
    • Problems surfacing after a promotion added unstructured responsibility

    Both Diagnostic criterion Supported

    Symptoms emerging when demands exceed capacity

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR Autism C

    DSM-5-TR explicitly allows for autism to become fully apparent only when demands exceed capacity. For ADHD, symptoms must have existed earlier but are often scaffolded by family and school.

    Also seen in: A first-ever onset of difficulties in adulthood points to other causes (depression, burnout, trauma, substance use, medical illness).

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Young 2020, Rommelse 2016

    • Spending the morning on a trivial task while a deadline looms
    • Freezing in front of a long to-do list
    • Answering easy emails instead of the important one

    ADHD Questionnaire item Supported

    Planning / prioritisation (executive function)

    Also seen in: Overwhelm from depression and anxiety can produce similar paralysis.

    Sources: Kessler 2010, Barkley 2011, Silverstein 2018

Time

    • Starting an essay the night before it's due, every single time
    • Doing tax returns in the last hour before the deadline
    • Relying on adrenaline to get anything done

    ADHD Research finding Preliminary

    Procrastination

    Last-minute success can hide the problem in high-ability women for years.

    Also seen in: Procrastination is not an official ADHD symptom. In the one direct study it was linked with inattention only, and it is very common in the general population.

    Sources: Niermann 2014, Young 2020

    • Thinking 'I have loads of time' and then being 20 minutes late
    • Not noticing that two hours have passed
    • Planning far more into a day than is possible

    ADHD Research finding Supported

    Altered time perception / time management difficulty

    Chronic lateness in women is often judged as disrespect or selfishness rather than understood as an executive difficulty.

    Also seen in: Lab timing differences are real, but 'time blindness' does not fully explain lateness (see claim time-blindness-explains-lateness). Depression slows time perception too.

    Sources: Marx 2022, Noreika 2013, Fredrick 2021

    • Double-booking yourself
    • Agreeing to a favour on the spot and then having no time for it
    • A to-do list that would take three days crammed into one

    ADHD Research finding Preliminary

    Planning and time management difficulty

    Also seen in: Overcommitting is also driven by people-pleasing, anxiety or hypomania.

    Sources: Barkley 2011, Turjeman-Levi 2024

Emotion

    • Snapping at a partner over something small, then feeling fine ten minutes later
    • Crying from frustration when a plan falls through
    • Being told you're 'too sensitive' or 'dramatic'

    ADHD Research finding Supported

    Emotional lability / emotion dysregulation

    Women's emotional symptoms are often attributed to hormones, anxiety or personality disorder rather than recognised as part of ADHD.

    Also seen in: Not a DSM ADHD criterion. Emotional lability also occurs in borderline personality disorder, bipolar disorder, PMDD, trauma and autism. In ADHD it tends to be chronic, quick and reactive rather than episodic.

    Sources: Beheshti 2020, Shaw 2014, Faraone 2018, Skirrow 2014

    • Feeling rage at a website that won't load
    • Getting overwhelmed by a minor setback at the end of a long day
    • Shouting at objects

    ADHD Research finding Supported

    Irritability / low frustration tolerance

    Also seen in: Irritability is also a feature of depression, anxiety, PMDD/premenstrual exacerbation and sleep deprivation.

    Sources: Skirrow 2014, Beheshti 2020, Erhardt 1999

    • Being unable to work for the rest of the day after a tense email
    • Needing a long time to recover after an argument
    • Not knowing what to do with a strong feeling except wait it out

    ADHD Questionnaire item Supported

    Difficulties in emotion regulation (strategies, goal-directed behaviour, impulse control)

    Also seen in: The DERS is transdiagnostic: scores are high in BPD, PTSD, depression and PMDD as well.

    Sources: Bjureberg 2015, Hirsch 2018, Beheshti 2020

    • Feeling crushed by mild feedback at work
    • Reading a short text reply as a sign someone is annoyed with you
    • Withdrawing from friends after a perceived slight

    ADHD Research finding Preliminary

    Rejection sensitivity

    Women describe masking these reactions, which can lead to withdrawal and loneliness.

    Also seen in: 'Rejection sensitive dysphoria' (RSD) is not a diagnostic or validated research construct, and evidence that it is core to ADHD is weak (Canu 2007 found no elevation; see claim rsd-is-a-core-adhd-symptom). Rejection sensitivity is also high in BPD, social anxiety, depression and after trauma.

    Sources: Rowney-Smith 2026, Bondü 2014, Beaton 2022, Downey 1996, Berenson 2009, Canu 2007

    • Being unable to stop thinking about an unfair decision at work
    • Feeling physically agitated by injustice in the news
    • Speaking up in situations others let slide

    ADHD Research finding Preliminary

    Justice sensitivity

    Also seen in: Evidence is from one small adult pilot and one adolescent study. Strong reactions to unfairness are also commonly described by autistic people, but there are few studies.

    Sources: Schäfer 2012, Bondü 2014

    • Feeling like a fraud at work despite good results
    • Believing you're lazy or stupid
    • Comparing yourself with friends who seem to manage life easily

    ADHD Questionnaire item Supported

    Low self-esteem / negative self-concept

    Late-diagnosed women often describe years of self-blame before diagnosis.

    Also seen in: Low self-esteem is non-specific and common in depression, trauma and many conditions.

    Sources: Cook 2014, Pedersen 2024, Erhardt 1999, Attoe 2023

    • Realising you were angry only days later
    • Answering 'I don't know' when asked how you feel
    • Noticing a racing heart before noticing you're anxious

    Autism Research finding Established

    Alexithymia

    Burnout research lists alexithymia as a contributor, often unrecognised in women diagnosed late.

    Also seen in: Alexithymia affects about half of autistic people but not all. It is also raised in depression, eating disorders, PTSD and ADHD.

    Sources: Kinnaird 2019, Bird 2013

    • Crying from overwhelm after a chaotic day
    • Being unable to calm down after an unexpected change
    • Intense frustration when interrupted

    Autism Research finding Supported

    Emotion dysregulation in autism

    Also seen in: Emotion dysregulation is transdiagnostic (ADHD, BPD, PTSD, PMDD). Context matters: in autism it is often linked to overload and change.

    Sources: Mazefsky 2013

    • Big reactions to small setbacks
    • Feeling flooded and unable to think
    • Needing hours alone to recover from an emotional event

    Both Research finding Supported

    Emotion dysregulation (transdiagnostic)

    Also seen in: Strongly overlaps with BPD, PMDD, bipolar disorder and trauma. Pattern, triggers and lifelong course help to differentiate.

    Sources: Beheshti 2020, Mazefsky 2013, Shaw 2014

Social communication

    • Pre-planning what to say before a phone call
    • Long silences, or talking at length because you're unsure when to stop
    • Feeling conversations are a performance you've rehearsed

    Autism Diagnostic criterion Established

    Differences in social-emotional reciprocity (conversation)

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR Autism A1

    Many autistic women have learned conversational rules explicitly and appear fluent, so the effort is invisible to clinicians. ICD-11 notes that some people function adequately through exceptional effort.

    Also seen in: Social anxiety also makes conversation effortful, but the cause is fear of judgement rather than uncertainty about the unwritten rules. The two often co-exist.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Hull 2017, Lai 2015

    • Dreading office kitchen chit-chat
    • Feeling relaxed only when the conversation turns to a shared interest
    • Not knowing how to respond to 'how are you?'

    Autism Diagnostic criterion Established

    Differences in social-emotional reciprocity (social chat)

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR Autism A1

    Also seen in: A dislike of small talk is common in the general population and in introverts. On its own it says little.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Baron-Cohen 2001, Ritvo 2010

    • Explaining a favourite topic for twenty minutes
    • Being told 'you're lecturing'
    • Sharing facts as your way of connecting

    Autism Diagnostic criterion Established

    One-sided sharing of interests

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR Autism A1

    In women this may focus on socially typical topics (a TV series, psychology, animals), so it isn't flagged.

    Also seen in: Also seen in ADHD (talkativeness, tangents).

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Hull 2020

    • A friend saying you seemed cold when you were trying to help
    • Freezing when someone cries
    • Showing care through practical help rather than words

    Autism Diagnostic criterion Established

    Reduced or atypical sharing of emotions / affect

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR Autism A1

    Autistic women are often highly caring but express it in ways others misread.

    Also seen in: Difficulty identifying one's own emotions (alexithymia) may explain some of this rather than autism itself.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Bird 2013

    • Looking at someone's mouth or forehead instead of their eyes
    • Counting seconds of eye contact
    • Listening better when walking side by side

    Autism Diagnostic criterion Established

    Atypical eye contact (nonverbal communication)

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR Autism A2

    Many autistic women have trained themselves to make 'enough' eye contact, so normal eye contact in a clinic does not rule out autism.

    Also seen in: Social anxiety and trauma can also cause gaze avoidance.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Hull 2017, Hull 2018

    • Not realising someone was annoyed until they said so
    • Missing that a friend was hinting they wanted to leave
    • Replaying conversations to decode them

    Autism Diagnostic criterion Established

    Difficulty understanding nonverbal communication

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR Autism A2

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019)

    • 'Resting' face read as angry
    • Being told you sound flat or monotone
    • Being asked 'what's wrong?' when you're fine

    Autism Diagnostic criterion Established

    Atypical facial expression, gesture or prosody

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR Autism A2

How diagnosis works

How diagnosis actually works

ADHD: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022)

  • Symptom threshold (Criterion A). There are 9 inattention symptoms (A1a–i) and 9 hyperactivity-impulsivity symptoms (A2a–i). A child needs 6 or more in a domain. From age 17 the threshold is 5.
  • Symptoms must have lasted at least 6 months.
  • They must be out of keeping with developmental level and must harm functioning.
  • Childhood onset (B). Several symptoms were present before age 12. The rule is that several symptoms were present, not that a diagnosis was made then.
  • Pervasiveness (C). Several symptoms show up in two or more settings, such as home, work or study, and friendships.
  • Impairment (D). There is clear evidence that the symptoms interfere with, or lower the quality of, social, educational or work functioning.
  • Exclusion (E). The symptoms do not occur only during a psychotic disorder and are not better explained by another mental disorder (mood, anxiety, dissociative, personality, substance intoxication or withdrawal).
  • Specifiers.
  • Presentation (past 6 months): combined, predominantly inattentive, or predominantly hyperactive-impulsive.
  • In partial remission.
  • Severity: mild, moderate or severe.
  • Other categories. "Other specified" and "unspecified" ADHD cover impairing presentations that fall short of full criteria.
  • Adult wording. The adult examples allow hyperactivity to show as subjective restlessness, and distractibility to include unrelated thoughts. Both matter for women.
  • Autism alongside ADHD. Since DSM-5 (2013), ADHD and autism can be diagnosed together. DSM-IV did not allow this.

ADHD: ICD-11 (WHO 2019), 6A05

  • ICD-11 describes a persistent pattern (at least 6 months) of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity.
  • It starts during development, typically in early to mid childhood.
  • It is outside the normal range for age and intellectual level, and it interferes with academic, occupational or social functioning.
  • No symptom count. Clinicians judge severity and pervasiveness instead.
  • Presentations: predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, combined, other specified, or unspecified.
  • Adults. ICD-11 notes that hyperactivity in adults may show mainly as inner restlessness or discomfort with quiet, sedentary activity.

Autism: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022)

  • A. Social communication and interaction. All three areas must be present, now or in the past:
  • A1: reciprocity in conversation and in sharing interests and emotions.
  • A2: nonverbal communication (eye contact, gesture, facial expression, tone) and how it fits with speech.
  • A3: developing, keeping and understanding relationships, and adjusting behaviour to context.
  • B. Restricted and repetitive patterns. At least two of four, now or in the past:
  • B1: repetitive movements, use of objects or speech (stimming, echolalia, scripted phrases).
  • B2: insistence on sameness, routines, rigid thinking, distress at small changes.
  • B3: highly intense or narrow interests.
  • B4: sensory over- or under-reactivity, or unusual sensory interests.
  • C. Early onset. Signs must be present in early development. DSM-5-TR explicitly allows that they may not fully show until social demands exceed capacity, or may be masked by learned strategies later in life.
  • D. Clinically significant impairment.
  • E. Not better explained by intellectual disability or global developmental delay.
  • Specifiers.
  • Severity level (1, 2 or 3: requiring support, substantial support, very substantial support), rated separately for A and B.
  • With or without intellectual impairment.
  • With or without language impairment.
  • Associated with a known genetic, medical or environmental factor.
  • Associated with another neurodevelopmental, mental or behavioural problem.
  • With catatonia.

Autism: ICD-11 (WHO 2019), 6A02

  • ICD-11 describes persistent difficulty starting and sustaining reciprocal social interaction and communication, plus restricted, repetitive and inflexible patterns of behaviour, interests or activities that are clearly atypical for age and culture.
  • Sensory hyper- or hypo-sensitivity falls within the repetitive and restricted domain.
  • Onset is during development. Features may not become fully apparent until demands exceed capacity.
  • ICD-11 notes that some people function adequately in many contexts through exceptional effort, so their difficulties may not be visible to others. This is the clearest manual-level recognition of camouflaging.
  • Subtypes are by intellectual development and functional language (6A02.0–6A02.5).

What a real assessment involves

  • Developmental history and an informant. Both conditions require evidence from childhood.
  • A parent or older sibling interview, school reports and childhood photos or videos all help.
  • Adult diagnosis is harder when no informant is available, when learned or camouflaging strategies hide traits, and when co-occurring conditions are present (Lai 2015, 10.1016/s2215-0366(15)00277-1).
  • Structured interviews.
  • ADHD: DIVA (Ramos-Quiroga 2016, 10.1177/1087054716646451) or similar. The interview covers childhood and adulthood across life domains.
  • Autism: interviews and observation such as ADI-R and ADOS-2 (these are proprietary instruments; no DOI is cited here).
  • Women who camouflage can score below cut-offs on observational measures. Autistic women's observed social presentation was less autistic than their traits predicted (Lai 2016, 10.1177/1362361316671012; Cruz 2025, 10.1007/s11065-023-09630-2).
  • Questionnaires screen; they do not diagnose.
  • ASRS: the 6-item screener missed about a third of community cases (Kessler 2005, 10.1017/S0033291704002892).
  • AQ: did not predict diagnosis in a referral clinic. Sensitivity was 0.77 and specificity 0.29, and anxiety can inflate scores (Ashwood 2016, 10.1017/s0033291716001082).
  • RAADS-R: its first validation compared clearly defined groups (Ritvo 2010, 10.1007/s10803-010-1133-5).
  • Cognitive tests do not diagnose.
  • Lab executive-function tests correlate poorly with real-life ratings (Toplak 2012, 10.1111/jcpp.12001).
  • IQ-index profiles are not sensitive or specific (Wilson 2023, 10.1093/arclin/acad073).
  • High-IQ adults with ADHD can look near-normal on tests (Milioni 2016, 10.1177/1087054714554933).
  • Adult-onset caution. When people were followed carefully from childhood, apparent late-onset ADHD usually had another explanation, most often heavy substance use or a complex psychiatric history (Sibley 2018, 10.1176/appi.ajp.2017.17030298). Other cohorts report adult cases without childhood diagnoses (Moffitt 2015, 10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.14101266; Agnew-Blais 2016, 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2016.0465). This remains debated. Clinically, a first-ever onset in adulthood is a reason to look hard for other causes.
  • Co-occurrence.
  • About 28% of autistic people meet ADHD criteria (Lai 2019, 10.1016/S2215-0366(19)30289-5). Another pooled estimate is 38.5% current (Rong 2021, 10.1016/j.rasd.2021.101759).
  • About 21% of young people with ADHD meet the autism threshold (Hollingdale 2019, 10.1017/S0033291719002368).
  • An ADHD diagnosis first delays autism diagnosis, more so in girls (Kentrou 2019, 10.1177/1362361318785171).

Why women are missed (the female presentation)

  • ADHD.
  • Women and girls show quieter, more inattentive and internalising presentations, compensatory strategies and comorbid anxiety or depression (Young 2020, 10.1186/s12888-020-02707-9; Hinshaw 2021, 10.1111/jcpp.13480; Quinn 2014, 10.4088/pcc.13r01596).
  • Girls needed more disruptive behaviour than boys to be diagnosed (Mowlem 2018, 10.1007/s00787-018-1211-3).
  • Females are diagnosed about 4 years later, after more psychiatric care (Skoglund 2023, 10.1111/jcpp.13920).
  • Autism.
  • The male:female ratio is closer to 3:1 than 4:1 once screening is used, suggesting diagnostic bias (Loomes 2017, 10.1016/j.jaac.2017.03.013).
  • The evidence supports a female-typical presentation with more camouflaging, but this is still being characterised (Hull 2020, 10.1007/s40489-020-00197-9; Cruz 2025).
  • Late-diagnosed women describe "pretending to be normal" and being misdiagnosed (Bargiela 2016, 10.1007/s10803-016-2872-8; Gesi 2021, 10.3390/brainsci11070912; Leedham 2020, 10.1177/1362361319853442).

Differential diagnoses and look-alikes

Any of the following can produce several items on the list. They can also co-occur with ADHD or autism, so finding one does not rule the other out.

Look-alike Overlapping items Distinguishing points
Anxiety disorders (GAD, social anxiety, panic) inattention, restlessness, avoidance, social difficulty, intolerance of uncertainty Worry or fear drives the difficulty. In autism or ADHD the difficulty predates the anxiety and persists when anxiety is treated. GAD can inflate AQ scores (Ashwood 2016). Intolerance of uncertainty is transdiagnostic (Gentes 2011, 10.1016/j.cpr.2011.05.001).
Depression concentration, motivation, task initiation, self-care, withdrawal, fatigue Episodic, with a change from baseline. ADHD and autism are lifelong traits.
Bipolar disorder restlessness, talkativeness, impulsive spending, sleep change Episodes of elevated mood and reduced need for sleep (Asherson 2014, 10.1185/03007995.2014.915800).
Borderline personality disorder emotional lability, impulsivity, rejection sensitivity, unstable relationships Fear of abandonment, chronic emptiness, identity disturbance, recurrent self-harm and stress-related dissociation are more specific to BPD (Asherson 2014). The two often co-occur.
PTSD / complex trauma hypervigilance, concentration, emotional dysregulation, people-pleasing, dissociation or shutdown Linked to trauma history and re-experiencing. Autistic and ADHD women have high victimisation rates, so both may be present.
OCD ordering, rituals, rigidity, need for sameness OCD rituals are driven by intrusive fears and relieve anxiety. Autistic routines bring comfort or predictability.
Eating disorders rigid food rules, restriction, bingeing Starvation itself raises rigidity and social withdrawal (Dinkler 2020, 10.1111/jcpp.13265). Reassess traits once nutrition is restored.
Sleep disorders (apnoea, delayed sleep phase, insomnia, narcolepsy) inattention, fatigue, irritability, daytime sleepiness Sleep assessment. A delayed body clock is common in ADHD but is a separate, treatable problem (Coogan 2017, 10.1007/s12402-016-0214-5).
Iron deficiency, with or without anaemia; restless legs fatigue, poor concentration, restless legs Check ferritin. Iron treatment reduced fatigue in non-anaemic women with low ferritin (Verdon 2003, 10.1136/bmj.326.7399.1124).
Thyroid disease restlessness (overactive), slowness and fog (underactive) Blood tests.
POTS / dysautonomia, long COVID brain fog, fatigue, dizziness New onset with orthostatic symptoms. POTS inattention was not present in childhood (Raj 2009, 10.1136/jnnp.2008.144360).
Substance use and medication effects inattention, impulsivity, sleep A timeline relative to use. Apparent late-onset ADHD is often substance-related (Sibley 2018).
Hearing loss, auditory processing disorder, specific learning disorders, DCD/dyspraxia, developmental prosopagnosia not listening, noise difficulty, clumsiness, face recognition Audiology, educational or OT assessment.

What is NOT a symptom, or not established, despite popular belief

  • Partly supported claims that the inventory reflects with caveats:
  • "PDA" (pathological demand avoidance) is not a recognised diagnosis (Kildahl 2021, 10.1177/13623613211034382).
  • "Hyper-empathy" and the AuDHD "push-pull" between routine and novelty have no peer-reviewed adult studies.
  • ADHD "waiting mode" has no peer-reviewed adult studies.
  • "Everyone is a bit ADHD or autistic": traits are continuous in the population, but diagnosis requires early onset plus impairment.
