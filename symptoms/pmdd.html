Symptoms / PMDD: symptoms and patterns

PMDD: symptoms and patterns

The eleven diagnostic symptom groups of PMDD, the timing that defines it, and related physical, cognitive and cycle-linked changes. A selection from the literature we have reviewed, not a complete list.

Recognising yourself here can be a good reason to ask for an assessment. It is not a diagnosis: many of these experiences also happen with anxiety, depression, trauma, thyroid problems, iron deficiency and other conditions. A diagnosis needs a clinician. Crisis support if you are struggling now.

How PMDD is diagnosed, in brief. A clinician looks for at least five symptoms across the eleven diagnostic groups, including at least one of the four core mood groups (mood swings, irritability or anger, low mood, anxiety). They appear in the week before a period, improve within a few days after it starts and are minimal in the week after; occur in most cycles over the past year; and cause real distress or interference. Daily ratings over two cycles confirm the pattern, but a clinician can assess you and offer care while you track. Several items below belong to the same diagnostic group, so your ticks are not a symptom count. More detail.

98 items: 34 from diagnostic criteria, 2 from validated questionnaires, 56 from research, 6 from women's accounts in qualitative studies. Each shows its evidence grade and sources.

Core mood

    • Laughing with friends, then suddenly close to tears over a small comment
    • Feeling emotionally 'raw' for a few days and then it lifts once my period starts

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Marked affective lability

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD B1

    Also seen in: Rapid mood shifts across the whole month can also occur in bipolar disorder, borderline personality disorder and ADHD-related emotional dysregulation; the cyclical timing is what matters here.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Landén 2003, Yonkers 2008

    • Crying at adverts or small setbacks
    • Tears welling up in meetings or at work

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Marked affective lability (sudden sadness or tearfulness)

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD B1

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Yonkers 2008

    • Reading a short text reply as a sign someone is angry with me
    • Feeling devastated by mild feedback that wouldn't bother me a week later

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Increased sensitivity to rejection (facet of affective lability)

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD B1

    Also seen in: Rejection sensitivity is also common in ADHD, depression and borderline personality disorder; ask whether it reliably spikes premenstrually.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Eisenlohr-Moul 2022 (hormone-withdrawal trial)

    • Snapping at my partner or kids over small things
    • Feeling irritated by noises, people or questions I'd normally shrug off

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Marked irritability

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD B2

    Also seen in: Irritability is also prominent in depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, hyperthyroidism and sleep deprivation.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Landén 2003, Hartlage 2012, Yonkers 2008

    • Shouting or slamming doors, then feeling ashamed afterwards
    • Anger that comes on fast and feels hard to stop

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Marked anger

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD B2

    Also seen in: If anger leads to aggression or fear for anyone's safety, this needs prompt help whatever the cause.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Comasco 2021, Gröndal 2025

    • Regular fights with my partner in the same part of each cycle
    • Friction with colleagues that doesn't happen the rest of the month

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Increased interpersonal conflicts

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD B2

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Haidarieh-Zadeh 2026, Ussher 2017

    • Feeling sad for days for no clear reason
    • Losing the sense that things will be okay

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Markedly depressed mood

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD B3

    Also seen in: Low mood that persists through the week after the period suggests depression, possibly with premenstrual exacerbation. Hartlage 2012 found depressed mood less frequent in PMDD than other mood symptoms.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Yonkers 2008, Hartlage 2012

    • Thinking my life or relationships are doomed, then seeing things differently a week later
    • Feeling there is no point in trying

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Feelings of hopelessness

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD B3

    Also seen in: Hopelessness is linked to suicide risk; see the Safety items.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Eisenlohr-Moul 2022 (hormone-withdrawal trial)

    • Replaying mistakes and calling myself stupid
    • Feeling I'm a bad partner, parent or friend

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Self-deprecating thoughts

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD B3

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Endicott 2006 (DRSP)

    • A knot in my stomach and racing thoughts
    • Worrying much more about work, health or relationships

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Marked anxiety and tension

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD B4

    Also seen in: Anxiety present most of the month suggests an anxiety disorder (which can also worsen premenstrually); also consider hyperthyroidism and caffeine or stimulant effects.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), ICD-11 (WHO 2019), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Yonkers 2008

    • Startling easily
    • Feeling restless and wound up all evening

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Feeling keyed up or on edge

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD B4

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Endicott 2006 (DRSP)

Thinking & focus

    • Interpreting neutral faces or messages as hostile

    PMDD Research finding Preliminary

    Negative attentional bias

    Also seen in: Negative attentional bias is also typical of depression and anxiety.

    Sources: Henderson 2025

    • Losing track mid-conversation

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Subjective difficulty concentrating

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD C2

    Also seen in: Concentration problems are also part of ADHD, depression, anxiety, iron deficiency, thyroid disease and poor sleep.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Eisenlohr-Moul 2022 (hormone-withdrawal trial)

    • Struggling to find words

    PMDD Research finding Preliminary

    Subjective cognitive complaints

    Also seen in: Objective tests of memory and attention have not shown consistent changes across the cycle or in PMDD; complaints may reflect mood, sleep or other conditions.

    • Deadlines slipping only in that week
    • Household systems falling apart for a few days each month

    PME Research finding Preliminary

    Luteal-phase executive function difficulty

    Also seen in: Evidence is from small studies, mainly in women with ADHD, PMS or PMDD.

    Sources: Wynchank 2025, Eisenlohr-Moul 2022 (hormone-withdrawal trial)

Energy & sleep

    • Needing to lie down after work
    • Skipping exercise because I physically can't face it

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Lethargy, easy fatigability, lack of energy

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD C3

    Also seen in: Fatigue is common in iron deficiency (especially with heavy periods), thyroid disease, depression, sleep disorders and anaemia.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Bernstein 2014

    • Sleeping 10 hours and still waking tired
    • Napping every afternoon that week

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Hypersomnia

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD C5

    Also seen in: Persistent excessive sleepiness also occurs in depression, sleep apnoea, iron deficiency and hypothyroidism.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Endicott 2006 (DRSP)

    • Lying awake with racing thoughts
    • Waking at 3 a.m. and not getting back to sleep

    PMDD Diagnostic criterion Established Part of a PMDD diagnostic group

    Insomnia

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD C5

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Baker 2018

    • Waking up unrefreshed
    • Restless nights with cramps or hot feelings

    Cycle-linked Research finding Supported

    Premenstrual and menstrual poor sleep quality

    Also seen in: Painful periods disrupt sleep in their own right.

    Sources: Baker 2018, Iacovides 2015 (dysmenorrhea)

    • Changing pads or tampons every 1-2 hours
    • Feeling exhausted all month

    Cycle-linked Research finding Established

    Heavy menstrual bleeding with iron deficiency

    Also seen in: Iron deficiency can mimic or worsen premenstrual fatigue and poor concentration; a ferritin test can check it.

    Sources: Munro 2023, Weyand 2023, Auerbach 2025

    • Symptoms that persist through the week after my period

    Cycle-linked Research finding Preliminary

    Possible thyroid dysfunction (look-alike)

    Also seen in: Thyroid problems were found in a minority of women with PMS, but thyroid treatment did not treat PMS; testing helps rule out a look-alike rather than explain PMDD.

    Also seen in: Lightheadedness also occurs with POTS, iron deficiency/anaemia, dehydration and low blood pressure; studies are small.

    • Stiffness in the mornings that week

    Joint pain

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD C7

    Also seen in: Persistent joint pain, swelling or stiffness can reflect inflammatory or hypermobility-related conditions.

    • Feeling like I've done a workout I didn't do

    Muscle pain

    Criterion: DSM-5-TR PMDD C7

    Also seen in: Counts within the single physical-symptoms item.

    Sources: DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), Endicott 2006 (DRSP), Mitchell 2022

    Also seen in: Headache is not one of the DSM-5-TR PMDD physical examples, but is commonly reported with PMDD. Sudden severe headache or headache with neurological symptoms needs urgent care.

    • Bruising or injections hurting more than expected

    Cycle-linked Research finding Preliminary

    Heightened pain sensitivity with dysmenorrhea

    Also seen in: In healthy pain-free women, well-controlled studies mostly find no cycle effect on experimental pain.

How diagnosis works

How diagnosis works

PMDD (DSM-5-TR, APA 2022)

  • Symptom count. At least 5 of 11 symptoms. At least 1 must be a core mood symptom:
  • B1: mood swings or sudden tearfulness, including greater sensitivity to rejection.
  • B2: marked irritability or anger, or more conflict with other people.
  • B3: marked low mood, hopelessness or self-critical thoughts.
  • B4: marked anxiety, tension or feeling on edge.
  • The other symptoms (C1–C7) make up the count of five:
  • less interest in usual activities
  • difficulty concentrating
  • tiredness or low energy
  • appetite change, overeating or cravings
  • sleeping too much or too little
  • feeling overwhelmed or out of control
  • physical symptoms (breast tenderness or swelling, joint or muscle pain, bloating, weight gain)
  • All physical symptoms together count as one symptom (C7). Headache is commonly reported but is not one of the DSM physical examples.
  • Timing (Criterion A). In most cycles over the past year:
  • symptoms are present in the final week before menses,
  • they start to improve within a few days of bleeding starting, and
  • they are minimal or absent in the week after menses.
  • Impact (D). The symptoms cause clinically significant distress, or interfere with work, school, social life or relationships.
  • Not just another condition getting worse (E). The picture is not simply premenstrual worsening of another disorder, such as depression, panic, a personality disorder or ADHD. PMDD can still co-occur with these.
  • Daily ratings (F). The pattern must be confirmed by prospective daily ratings across at least 2 symptomatic cycles. Until then, a diagnosis can only be provisional.
  • Other causes ruled out (G). The symptoms are not caused by a substance, medication or another medical condition.
  • Evidence behind the thresholds. In daily-rating data, the worst days usually ran from about 3–4 days before menses through the first 2–3 days of bleeding, and 4 or more symptoms best predicted impairment (Hartlage 2012, 10.1001/archgenpsychiatry.2011.1368). So impairing symptoms below the count of five still deserve care (see also Halbreich 2003, 10.1016/s0306-4530(03)00098-2).

ICD-11 (WHO 2019)

  • ICD-11 lists premenstrual dysphoric disorder as a diagnosis in its own right. It describes a cyclical pattern of mood, physical or cognitive symptoms that:
  • start in the days before menses,
  • ease within a few days of bleeding starting,
  • are minimal about a week after, and
  • include at least one mood symptom and cause significant distress or impairment.
  • Re-check this wording and the code against the WHO ICD-11 browser before publishing. It was not re-verified in this session.

Tools

  • DRSP (Endicott 2006, 10.1007/s00737-005-0103-y). A daily record that rates the DSM PMDD symptoms and the impairment criteria.
  • C-PASS (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017, 10.1176/appi.ajp.2016.15121510). Turns 2 or more months of DRSP ratings into a standardised diagnosis. It classified about 98% of cases correctly against expert clinicians.
  • Retrospective recall is unreliable. A person's memory of premenstrual worsening predicted the prospective diagnosis poorly (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017).
  • The PSST and other retrospective questionnaires can only screen. They cannot diagnose.
  • ISPMD consensus and standards (O'Brien 2011; Nevatte 2013; Ismaili 2016) say the same: a careful history plus 2 months of diaries that show symptoms relieved by menstruation.

PMDD vs PMS vs PME

What it is Key distinguishing feature
PMDD Severe, mainly mood-driven premenstrual disorder (DSM/ICD) ≥5 of 11 symptoms including ≥1 core mood symptom; a symptom-free week after menses; confirmed by daily ratings
PMS / core premenstrual disorder (ISPMD) Clinically significant premenstrual symptoms (physical and/or psychological) that cause impairment Same cyclical on/off timing, but the PMDD count or mood requirement is not met
Common premenstrual changes Mild changes that most menstruating people notice (Yonkers 2008, 10.1016/s0140-6736(08)60527-9) No significant distress or impairment, so not a disorder
PME (premenstrual exacerbation) An underlying condition (depression, anxiety, bipolar, BPD, psychosis, ADHD, migraine, epilepsy, asthma, IBS…) present across the cycle that gets worse premenstrually Symptoms are still present in the week after menses. About 60% of women with mood disorders report PME (Kuehner 2021, 10.1007/s11920-021-01286-0)
Variant premenstrual disorders (ISPMD) Premenstrual symptoms linked to ovulation without menstruation, or set off by progestogens or hormonal contraception Context-dependent: e.g., symptoms continue after hysterectomy with ovaries kept, or start with a hormonal method. The core/variant split is from the ISPMD consensus (O'Brien 2011), but these sub-definitions are not in its abstract; check them against the full text before publishing.

PMDD and PME can co-occur. Pooled comorbidity between PMDD/PMS and mood disorders was 42–49% (Bengi 2025, 10.1192/bjp.2025.133). Daily ratings that cover the whole cycle are what separate the two.

Look-alikes and co-occurring conditions to consider

Condition Why it can look similar What helps distinguish it
Depression / anxiety disorders Low mood, anxiety, fatigue, poor concentration Symptoms continue in the week after menses, which suggests PME. Bengi 2025; Kuehner 2021; Landén 2003 (10.1002/da.10089): PMDD is characterised more by irritability and mood swings
Bipolar disorder Cyclical mood shifts, irritability, sleep change. Retrospective studies suggest 15–27% of women with bipolar disorder meet PMDD criteria (Teatero 2013, 10.1111/bdi.12138) Hypomanic or manic episodes, reduced need for sleep, episodes not tied to the cycle
Borderline personality disorder Mood swings, rejection sensitivity, anger Present across the month, with perimenstrual worsening (Eisenlohr-Moul 2018, 10.1017/s0033291718001253)

Thyroid disease Fatigue, low mood, anxiety, weight change, poor concentration In one study 10.5% of women with prospectively confirmed PMS had thyroid dysfunction, but thyroid treatment did not treat PMS (Schmidt 1993, 10.1210/jcem.76.3.8445024). Thyroid testing rules out a look-alike

Hormonal contraception New mood symptoms after starting a method Timing relative to starting the method. Registry associations with antidepressant use and suicide attempts (Skovlund 2016, 2018). Some combined pills are used to treat PMDD (Lopez 2012)

IBS, migraine, epilepsy, asthma, POTS Physical symptoms that vary across the cycle These are PME-type, cycle-linked conditions that need their own care

Safety guidance (suicidality and self-harm)

  • Premenstrual suicidal thoughts are documented and serious.
  • PMDD was associated with higher odds of suicide attempt (OR 6.97) and suicidal ideation (OR 3.95) (Prasad 2021, 10.1089/jwh.2021.0185).
  • Among people with PMDD diagnosed from daily ratings: lifetime active suicidal ideation 72%, attempts 34%, non-suicidal self-injury 51% (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022, 10.1186/s12888-022-03851-0).
  • Clinically diagnosed premenstrual disorders were linked to higher suicide mortality (HR 1.92), and the link was higher with diagnosis before age 25 (Opatowski 2024, 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.13394). This is directly relevant to women aged 20–30.
  • Daily ratings showed suicidal and self-harm ideation peaking in the late luteal phase and sometimes returning around ovulation (Ly 2026, 10.1016/j.psyneuen.2026.107949).
  • Suicide attempts and psychiatric admissions cluster around menstruation across diagnoses (Jang 2018, 10.1080/13811118.2018.1430638; Eisenlohr-Moul 2022, 10.1038/s41398-022-02294-1).
  • Resource wording:
  • Any thoughts of suicide or self-harm need support the same day, even if they "always pass when my period comes".
  • A cyclical pattern makes suicidal thoughts predictable. It does not make them less dangerous.
  • Plans or intent need emergency services or a crisis line straight away.
  • Encourage people to add suicidal-thought and self-harm items to their daily tracking (the IAPMD tracker does this) and to make a safety plan timed to their high-risk days.
  • For clinicians: ask about suicidality specifically by cycle phase, including in people who do not meet PMDD criteria (PME of suicidality).
  • present the list as a checklist that produces a diagnosis or score, or
  • suggest that anyone change medication (including stimulant doses) without their prescriber.

ADHD and autism

  • Prevalence. Provisional PMDD is more common in ADHD: Broughton 2025 (31.4–41.1% vs 9.8%); Dorani 2021; Tsuji 2026 (adjusted OR 6.49); Groenman 2026. These studies mostly use screening questionnaires, which overestimate prevalence (Reilly 2024, 10.1016/j.jad.2024.01.066).
  • ADHD symptoms across the cycle. Small studies suggest ADHD symptoms worsen in the luteal phase or the premenstrual week (de Jong 2023; Bürger 2024; Wynchank 2025). Other studies point instead to menstruation or the early follicular phase (Zaritsky 2025; Roberts 2018). Patterns probably vary between individuals.
  • Autism.
  • Survey data describe a cyclical amplification of sensory and emotion-regulation challenges (Steward 2018).
  • PMDD prevalence findings conflict: not increased in Groenman 2022, increased in Groenman 2026, and very high in the small older study by Obaydi 2008.
  • In Tsuji 2026, autistic traits were not associated with PMDD.
