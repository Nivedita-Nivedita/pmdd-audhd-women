AuDHD

Autism and ADHD together in women: recognition, masking, emotion, the body and burnout.

What is AuDHD?

AuDHD is an informal, community term for being both autistic and having ADHD. It is not a separate diagnosis, but the overlap is common: across 96 studies, 28% of autistic people also had ADHD (Lai 2019), and another meta-analysis put it at 38.5% (Rong 2021).

Women are more likely to be missed. Screening studies suggest the true male-to-female ratio in autism is closer to 3:1 than the 4:1 seen in clinics, pointing to a diagnostic bias against girls (Loomes 2017).

Evidence on sex differences and on premenstrual dosing is thin.

What the research says about common claims

Autism and ADHD often occur together: roughly 28–39% of autistic people have ADHD, and about 21% of children with ADHD are autistic.

Established evidence Several good studies or reviews agree.

Autism and ADHD often occur together and appear to share genetic roots, so if you have one, it's reasonable to ask about the other.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Lots of people with ADHD are also autistic, and vice versa.”

The popular version matches the evidence well.

What the studies found

Meta-analyses estimate ADHD in 28% (Lai 2019) to 38.5% (Rong 2021) of autistic people, and autism in 21% of children and adolescents with ADHD (Hollingdale 2019). In 1.9 million Swedes, autism carried far higher odds of ADHD (OR 22.33), with a twin and sibling pattern consistent with shared genetics (Ghirardi 2017). We did not find a meta-analysis of autism in adults with ADHD (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Autistic children are many times (sometimes quoted as 28 times) more likely than other children to think about or attempt suicide.”

The popular version goes further: it gives one precise multiplier that we could not confirm, while the pooled evidence shows raised risk without that precision.

What the studies found

We did not find the single-clinic, parent-report study usually given for the '28 times' figure among the studies we checked (search last updated October 2026), so we can't confirm it. A meta-analysis of 47 papers on autistic young people aged 25 or under found pooled prevalence of 25.2% for suicidal ideation, 8.3% for attempts and 0.2% for suicide deaths; self-reports gave higher estimates than parent reports, heterogeneity was substantial, adverse childhood experiences were strongly associated with higher risk and resilience with lower risk (O'Halloran 2022). Risk is clearly raised, but a single multiplier from one study overstates how precise the evidence is, and these group figures do not predict what will happen to any child. Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Up to two-thirds (66%) of autistic adults have considered suicide.”

The popular version takes the highest figure from one late-diagnosed clinic sample; the pooled figure is about half that.

What the studies found

The 66% figure comes from 374 adults newly diagnosed with Asperger syndrome at one English specialist clinic between 2004 and 2013: 66% (243/367) reported lifetime suicidal ideation, 35% (127/365) plans or attempts and 31% depression; lifetime ideation was more likely than in a general UK population sample (odds ratio 9.6, 95% CI 7.6-11.9), and the authors identify depression as an important potential risk factor (Cassidy 2014). A meta-analysis of 36 studies (48,186 autistic and possibly autistic participants without intellectual disability) found pooled prevalence of 34.2% for lifetime suicidal ideation, 21.9% for plans and 24.3% for attempts and behaviours, with high heterogeneity (Newell 2023). 'Up to 66%' is accurate for that late-diagnosed clinic sample; across studies the pooled figure is about a third. These are rates in groups, not predictions for an individual. Link to this claim

Autistic adults consistently describe burnout as chronic exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance to sensory input.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Autistic burnout is a consistently described experience across 48 studies. Current questionnaires correlate strongly with depression and cannot yet reliably tell the two apart, so whether it is a separate condition remains a measurement question.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Autistic burnout is a distinct condition, different from depression.”

The popular version goes further: the experience is well described, but research has not yet shown it can be measured separately from depression.

What the studies found

Autistic adults consistently describe burnout as chronic exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance to stimuli, arising from life's demands plus barriers to support (Raymaker 2020; Higgins 2021), and a systematic review of 48 studies describes the same pattern (Ali 2025). That makes it a recognised lived experience. Whether it can be told apart from depression is a separate, measurement question: in autistic adults, the main autistic burnout questionnaire correlated strongly with depression and could not reliably distinguish current from past burnout (Mantzalas 2024; Arnold 2023). In general working populations (not autistic samples), occupational burnout's core feature, exhaustion, correlated with depressive symptoms at r = .80 (Bianchi 2021); that finding is about work burnout and should not be read as evidence about autistic burnout. Autistic burnout is not a diagnosis in DSM-5 or ICD-11; ICD-11's 'burn-out' (QD85) refers only to chronic workplace stress. Lack of a validated way to separate the two does not mean autistic burnout is 'just depression'.

Link to this claim

Camouflaging is reported across genders and in ADHD as well as autism, though autistic women report it more than autistic men.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Camouflaging happens across genders and diagnoses; undiagnosed adults with social difficulties and adults with ADHD also do it. It is more common in autistic women and is linked to poorer mental health.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Masking is something only autistic women do.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs the other way: masking is more common in autistic women but not exclusive to them.

What the studies found

Undiagnosed adults with social difficulties described compensation strategies similar to those of diagnosed autistic adults (Livingston 2019), and adults with ADHD also camouflage, though less than autistic adults (van der Putten 2024). Females used more camouflaging strategies than males in a meta-analysis (Cruz 2025), so it is more common in women but not exclusive to them. In autistic adults, camouflaging was one predictor of suicide risk (Cassidy 2018).

Link to this claim

Having both autism and ADHD is common and linked with later autism diagnosis and lower quality of life, but it is diagnosed as two conditions.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

'AuDHD' accurately describes having both autism and ADHD, a real and common combination. We did not find a validated research definition of it as a distinct third condition (search last updated October 2026); 'not a separate diagnosis' does not mean 'not real'.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“AuDHD is its own distinct condition.”

The popular version goes further: the combination is well documented, but not as a separate condition.

What the studies found

'AuDHD' is a community word for having both autism and ADHD. That combination is real and common: in pooled studies of autistic people of all ages, 28% had ADHD (Lai 2019) or 38.5% currently (Rong 2021), and among children and adolescents with ADHD, 21% met the threshold for autism (Hollingdale 2019). The word needs no separate validation to describe that combination accurately. What has not been shown is the stronger claim that having both is a distinct third condition with its own profile: we did not find a validated research definition of one (search last updated October 2026), and a review concludes the two overlap heavily but remain distinct enough to warrant separate diagnoses (Antshel 2019). Having both matters: autistic adults with more ADHD symptoms reported less independence and lower quality of life (Yerys 2022), and in one survey an earlier ADHD diagnosis delayed autism diagnosis in children, by 2.6 years on average in girls (Kentrou 2019).

Link to this claim

In Swedish registers, autistic people were about two and a half times as likely to die during follow-up, from many different causes.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Autistic people die earlier than average from a range of causes, many medical. The often-quoted '54 years' is not a measured life expectancy, and no group figure predicts an individual's lifespan.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“The average life expectancy of autistic people is just 54 years.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs differently: the study reports raised odds of death, not an average lifespan of 54.

What the studies found

The Swedish registry study usually linked to this figure compared 27,122 autistic people with 2,672,185 matched controls and found that 2.60% vs 0.91% died during follow-up (odds ratio 2.56, 95% CI 2.38-2.76), with raised mortality across almost all cause categories (Hirvikoski 2016). Its abstract reports odds of death during follow-up, not a life expectancy; an average age at death among people who died during a limited follow-up is not the same thing. Premature mortality is real, and the authors attribute it to many different medical conditions; the figure of 54 is not a measured life expectancy, and no group figure predicts an individual's lifespan.

Link to this claim

In national registries, suicide is several times more common in autistic people, mostly among those with co-occurring conditions such as depression or ADHD.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Registry studies agree suicide risk is raised in autistic people, with the highest figures in autism without intellectual disability plus ADHD. We did not find a peer-reviewed source for the specific 7x and 9x figures (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Autistic people are several times more likely to die by suicide than the general population, with the highest figures (around seven to nine times) quoted for autistic people without a learning disability.”

The popular version is close, but the exact multipliers vary by country and measure and depend heavily on co-occurring conditions.

What the studies found

National registry studies agree that suicide is more common in autistic people, but the size depends on the country, the measure and co-occurring conditions, and we did not find a peer-reviewed source for the specific 7x and 9x figures. In Denmark (6,559,266 residents, 35,020 autistic), autistic people had a higher rate of suicide death (adjusted incidence rate ratio 3.75, 95% CI 2.85-4.92) and of suicide attempt (3.19); autism without another psychiatric diagnosis gave an attempt rate ratio of 1.33 (0.99-1.78), against 9.27 with another diagnosis, and more than 90% of those who attempted or died had another diagnosis (Kõlves 2021). In Swedish registers (54,168 autistic people and 270,840 matched controls), risk was highest in autism without intellectual disability plus ADHD (suicide odds ratio 13.09, 95% CI 8.54-20.08; attempt odds ratio 7.25), and adjusting for psychiatric comorbidity reduced the risks (Hirvikoski 2020). In 372 English coroner inquest records, evidence of autism was found in 10.8% of suicide deaths against 1.1% expected (odds ratio 11.08, 95% CI 3.92-31.31) (Cassidy 2022). These are group associations from registry and record studies; they do not predict what will happen to any individual, and they point to co-occurring depression, anxiety and ADHD as conditions to recognise and treat.
