Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Disclosure isn't all or nothing. You can tell no one, only the accessibility office, your supervisor, or everyone, and you can move along that range over time.

  • You can often ask for what you need without naming a diagnosis.

  • Telling people is hard to undo, so it is worth doing in stages.

Evidence grade: Preliminary (P). Our library has no research on disclosing ADHD or autism in academia. The little evidence we have comes from qualitative studies of adults with ADHD and from a review of autistic people at work. Most of this guide is lived-experience and practice guidance, labelled "Practical tip". It is not evidence, and it has no grade.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

How to read this guide

Research claims have a source and a grade: [E] established, [S] supported, [P] preliminary, [U] popular but unsupported. Practical tip marks advice that comes from experience, not from studies. This guide is for PhD students, postdocs and early-career researchers. It is not legal advice.

1. Disclosure is a spectrum

The research that exists shows that disclosure cuts both ways. A systematic review of 26 studies of autistic people at work found that disclosure brought acceptance and accommodations, but also risked stigma and discrimination Lindsay 2021 [P]. Rates of disclosure, and of getting accommodations, varied widely Lindsay 2021 [P]. These were workplace studies of autistic people, not academics, and not people with ADHD.

Practical tip: four common levels. 1. No one. You manage privately. You keep full control, but you can't easily get formal adjustments. 2. Accessibility office only. The office knows your diagnosis. Your department sees only the adjustments, not the reason. Check that this is how your institution works. 3. Your supervisor or line manager. They can adjust day-to-day work. How it goes depends heavily on the person. 4. Public. Colleagues, students, social media. This is the hardest level to reverse.

Practical tip. You can sit at different levels for different people. Some people start at level 2 and move to level 3 once they trust their supervisor.

2. Legal protections

We don't summarise the law here. It differs between countries, and between students and staff in the same country.

Practical tip: where to look. - Your university's disability or equality policy (there are often separate ones for students and for staff). - Your national equality or disability-rights body. - Your students' union advice service or your trade union.

Practical tip. Questions worth asking: Does ADHD or autism count as a disability under my country's law? Does protection depend on having a formal diagnosis? Who is my "employer" if I'm funded by a grant?

3. Disclosing needs without disclosing a diagnosis

What people describe matters. In written accounts from 162 adults with ADHD or high ADHD traits, inattentive behaviours were the ones most often criticised. Whether people felt understood shaped whether they experienced comments as criticism Beaton 2022 [P]. That suggests the framing of a request may matter, though no study has tested this.

Practical tip: describe the need, not the label. For example: - "I work best with written notes after meetings. Could we do that?" - "I'm much more productive later in the day. Could we meet after 11?" - "Breaking this into smaller deadlines helps me a lot." - "I find open-plan noise very draining. Could I work from the quiet room?"

Practical tip. These requests are reasonable for anyone. That is their strength: you don't need to justify them with a diagnosis. If they're refused and you need formal backing, you can move to the accessibility office later.

4. Timing: contracts, job market, tenure

What we don't know yet. We found no research on how disclosure timing affects hiring, contract renewal or tenure in academia. Nothing below is based on data.

Practical tip: questions to weigh. - Before an offer: Will disclosing in an application help me get adjustments at interview? Or might it bias the panel? Some people choose to wait until after an offer, unless they need an interview adjustment. - On a short contract: Do I need adjustments now to do this job well? If so, waiting may cost more than disclosing. - Before a promotion or tenure review: Who sits on the panel, and what will they see? Ask HR what is kept confidential.

Practical tip. If you need an adjustment for an interview (for example, questions in writing), you may be able to request it without giving a reason, or give the reason to HR only.

5. Undoing disclosure (you mostly can't, so stage it)

There is no research on this either. Once people know, you can't make them forget.

Practical tip: stage it. - Start with the smallest group that can meet your need. - Ask, before you tell them: "Who else will this be shared with?" - Put requests in writing and keep copies. - Wait to see how the first disclosure goes before widening it. - Public disclosure (talks, social media) can be powerful for others. It also follows you into every future job application. Choose it on purpose, not in a hard moment.

6. Stories from others (consented, anonymised)

What we don't know yet. We haven't yet collected consented, anonymised stories for this page. We won't make them up. They will be added once people have agreed to share them.

Meanwhile, qualitative research gives a sense of what adults describe. A rapid review of 35 qualitative studies of adults with ADHD covered adult diagnosis, self-perception and social interactions. It found that many reported symptoms overlap with other conditions and may contribute to misdiagnosis and delayed diagnosis Ginapp 2022 [P]. A synthesis of 8 studies of women diagnosed with ADHD as adults found four themes: effects on social and emotional wellbeing, difficult relationships, a lack of control, and self-acceptance after diagnosis Attoe 2023 [P]. In 11 autistic women diagnosed after age 40, "the impact of others after diagnosis" was one of the main themes Leedham 2020 [P]. None of these studies was about academia or about disclosure decisions specifically.

What you can do

  1. Decide your level for each person, not once for everyone. (Practical tip)
  2. Try asking for the adjustment without the label first. (Practical tip)
  3. Before you disclose, ask who will be told. (Practical tip)
  4. Keep written records of requests and replies. (Practical tip)
  5. Get advice from your students' union, trade union or national disability-rights body before big decisions. (Practical tip)

Talk to a clinician about…

  • What your assessment report says, and whether a shorter letter for your employer or university can be written that lists needs rather than details.
  • Whether low mood, anxiety or exhaustion are adding to the difficulty. These can be treated in their own right.

What we don't know yet

  • Any research on disclosure of ADHD or autism in academia. None was found.
  • Outcomes of disclosure for women with ADHD in any workplace. The review we cite is about autistic adults.
  • How timing (before or after an offer, contract or tenure) changes outcomes.
  • Whether describing needs without a diagnosis gets different responses. This is plausible but untested.
  • Consented stories for section 6 have not yet been collected.

Popular claims checked

  • women-mask-their-adhd: "Women with ADHD 'mask' their symptoms, and that's why they're diagnosed so late." Verdict: partly true (evidence grade: preliminary).
  • only-autistic-women-mask: "Masking is something only autistic women do." Verdict: false (supported).
  • masking-causes-burnout-and-suicide: "Masking causes autistic burnout and suicide." Verdict: overstated (preliminary).
  • adhd-can-start-in-adulthood: "ADHD can start in adulthood." Verdict: overstated (supported). "Late recognition" is more accurate, which can matter when explaining a recent diagnosis.
