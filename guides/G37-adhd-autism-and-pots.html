Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • POTS means your heart rate jumps a lot when you stand up and you feel unwell, without your blood pressure dropping. Most people diagnosed are young women Sheldon 2015 (verified against full text) [E]. It is a physical problem, not "just anxiety" Masuki 2007 [P].

  • "Brain fog" is very common in POTS and can look like ADHD. But in one small study it had not been there in childhood, which ADHD requires Raj V 2009 [P]. No study reports how often ADHD and POTS occur together.

  • Hypermobility, autism and POTS overlap more than expected in small studies Csecs 2022 [P]. The popular "triad" of hypermobile EDS, POTS and mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS) is unproven Kucharik 2019 [P].

Evidence grade: PRELIMINARY (P) overall. The definition and who is affected are ESTABLISHED (E) or SUPPORTED (S). Links with ADHD, autism, periods, iron and most treatments are PRELIMINARY (P). The triad and "POTS is just anxiety" are POPULAR BUT UNSUPPORTED (U). Talk to a clinician about: symptoms on standing, when your concentration problems started, and how any ADHD medicine affects your heart rate. Not medical advice.

A few terms first: - POTS (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome): "postural" and "orthostatic" mean on standing up; "tachycardia" means a fast heart rate. - Autonomic nervous system: the automatic wiring that controls heart rate, blood pressure, digestion and sweating. "Dysautonomia" means this system isn't working as it should. - hEDS (hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome): a connective-tissue condition with very flexible joints, diagnosed using formal criteria. - MCAS (mast cell activation syndrome): a proposed condition in which immune cells called mast cells release chemicals such as histamine too easily.

1. What POTS is

The main international heart-rhythm statement defines POTS as frequent symptoms on standing plus a heart-rate rise of at least 30 beats per minute (bpm) when you move from lying down to standing, held for more than 30 seconds. For ages 12 to 19 the threshold is 40 bpm. There must also be no drop in blood pressure (orthostatic hypotension) Sheldon 2015 (verified against full text) [E].

Canadian heart specialists describe POTS as a syndrome, "not a specific disease". In other words, it is a pattern that can have several causes Raj 2020 [E]. Symptoms get worse when you are upright and better when you lie down. The same statement warns that the POTS label is sometimes given wrongly: to people with symptoms but no fast heart rate, a fast heart rate but no symptoms, or a fast heart rate with another clear cause Raj 2020 [E].

2. Who gets it

The heart-rhythm statement gives a prevalence of approximately 0.2%. Most patients first come to a doctor between 15 and 25 years of age, and more than 75% are female Sheldon 2015 (verified against full text) [E]. A US expert meeting described the typical patient as a woman of child-bearing age whose symptoms often start in adolescence. Onset may follow an immune stressor such as a viral infection Vernino 2021 [E].

In an online survey of 4,835 people with a doctor's diagnosis of POTS, 94% were female and 93% white. About half developed symptoms in adolescence, most commonly at age 14. The median wait for a diagnosis was 24 months, and misdiagnosis was frequent Shaw 2019 [S]. People chose to take part in this survey, so it may not represent everyone with POTS.

Problems with heart-rate and blood-pressure control are also part of long COVID. They might affect one-third of highly symptomatic patients, and many severely affected people meet the criteria for POTS Fedorowski 2024 [S].

3. It is not "just anxiety"

Many women with POTS are told their symptoms are anxiety. In a small lab study, researchers made blood pool in the legs, as it does when you stand. Heart rate rose 39 bpm in people with POTS and 19 bpm in controls. A fake (sham) version produced no rise. Heart-rate responses to a mental stress task were the same in both groups. People with POTS did report more anxiety, but their anxiety scores were not related to how much their heart rate rose Masuki 2007 [P].

In 21 people with POTS, lifetime depression and anxiety disorders, including panic disorder, were no more common than in the general population Raj V 2009 [P]. Another small study found that worry in POTS centres on body sensations, not on social fears or past trauma. People with POTS were also less accurate at sensing their own heartbeat Owens 2017 [P].

The research suggests worry follows the symptoms rather than causing them.

4. Brain fog versus ADHD

Trouble concentrating is nearly universal in POTS: 94% of the 4,835 survey respondents reported it Shaw 2019 [S]. In a social-media survey, 132 of 138 people with POTS reported "brain fog". They described it as feeling forgetful and cloudy, with difficulty focusing, thinking and communicating. Common triggers were fatigue (91%), lack of sleep (90%), prolonged standing (87%), dehydration (86%) and feeling faint (85%). The fog persisted after lying down Ross 2013 [P].

Lab testing backs this up. In 87 people with POTS and 39 matched controls, those with POTS had worse attention and executive function (planning and self-control skills) while standing Miller 2020a [P].

So is POTS brain fog actually ADHD? One small study compared 21 people with POTS, 18 with ADHD and 20 controls. The POTS group scored higher than controls on ADHD inattention scales, but these symptoms were not present in childhood Raj V 2009 [P]. ADHD is a lifelong pattern that starts in childhood. New concentration problems that arrive with POTS therefore point to POTS rather than to ADHD, though this rests on one small study.

No study we could find reports how often ADHD and POTS occur together. There is no cohort, registry or meta-analysis on ADHD in people with POTS, or POTS in people with ADHD. Both can still happen in the same person, so fog may have more than one source.

5. Autism, hypermobility and autonomic symptoms

Some overlaps are real, but they are partial and come from small or selected samples.

  • Hypermobility and neurodivergence. In 109 neurodivergent adults, 51% had generalised joint hypermobility, compared with 20% in the general population (odds ratio 4.51, with greater odds in females). The number of hypermobile joints helped explain links to dizziness on standing and to pain Csecs 2022 [P].
  • Autism in autonomic clinics. Among 28 autistic people referred to two UK autonomic centres, 20 had a diagnosed autonomic condition, most often POTS. Sixteen of those 20 had hEDS Owens 2021 [P]. These people had been referred because of symptoms, so the study can't tell us how common POTS is in autistic people generally.
  • Hypermobility in POTS. Using the 2017 criteria, 28 of 91 people with POTS (31%) met criteria for hEDS. A further 24% had generalised joint hypermobility without meeting full hEDS criteria Miller 2020b [P].

The "triad" with MCAS is unproven. A structured review found only 4 to 9 original research articles, and none that studied hEDS, POTS and MCAS together. The authors concluded that no evidence-based shared mechanism has been shown between any two of these conditions, "much less all three". They found that the supposed link rests on overlapping vague symptoms Kucharik 2019 [P]. A 2025 systematic review found no study that diagnosed a mast cell disorder in people with POTS or EDS using pre-set diagnostic criteria Farley 2025 [P]. In one clinic, 44 of 69 people with POTS had extra symptoms such as migraine, allergy, skin or gut problems. Of these 44, 29 had at least one lab result hinting at mast cell activation, but the authors note that single abnormal results are not definitive Kohno 2021 [P]. No study has looked at ADHD together with all three conditions.

6. Autonomic function in ADHD and autism is not the same as POTS

You may read that ADHD or autism "is dysautonomia". The research shows small average differences, not POTS.

  • In ADHD, 13 studies (869 people with ADHD, 909 controls) found slightly lower heart rate variability (HRV, a measure of how flexibly the heart rate adjusts) after a task. The effect was small (Hedges' g = 0.20) and varied a lot between studies Robe 2019 [P].
  • A review of 55 studies found that differences in ADHD more often pointed to under-arousal than over-arousal, but almost half of the findings were null Bellato 2020 [P].
  • In autism, 34 studies found lower baseline HRV and lower HRV reactivity under social stress Cheng 2020 [P].

None of these studies diagnosed POTS. A small average difference across a group says nothing about whether you have POTS.

7. Periods and pregnancy

Many women notice POTS changing across the menstrual cycle. The studies are small, and they point to the period itself rather than the days before it.

  • In a questionnaire study, women with POTS (65) felt more lightheaded than controls (95) in every phase of the cycle. Both groups felt most lightheaded during their period. Women with POTS also reported more gynaecological conditions Peggs 2012 [P].
  • In 10 women with POTS, standing tolerance was poorer just after the period started (early follicular phase) than in the second half of the cycle (mid-luteal phase) Fu 2010 [P].
  • Another 10 women with POTS showed no difference between these phases in heart rate or near-fainting, though blood pressure was higher in the mid-luteal phase Stickford 2015 [P].

Pregnancy. A systematic review of 11 articles rated the evidence as poor. It concluded that POTS should not be a reason to avoid pregnancy. Symptoms varied during pregnancy and after birth, and POTS did not appear to raise the rate of complications Morgan 2018 [P]. For ADHD medicines in pregnancy, see G26.

8. Iron and blood volume

In a small study, 15 people with POTS had larger shortfalls in plasma volume, red-cell volume and total blood volume than 14 controls. The hormones that normally correct this did not rise as expected Raj 2005 [P]. This is one reason fluids and salt are suggested.

In a retrospective study of 24 teenagers with POTS (aged 12 to 18, 71% female), 50% had low iron stores (ferritin 25 μg/L or less). That compares with 14% in US children and teenagers generally Jarjour 2013 [P]. This was one small group of teenagers. No study has tested whether treating low iron improves POTS. For more on ferritin, see G06.

9. What helps

All POTS treatments rest on a thin evidence base. The heart-rhythm statement puts non-drug measures first. It rates a structured, progressive exercise programme as one that "can be effective" (Class IIa). Extra water and salt, and several medicines including low-dose propranolol, are weaker (Class IIb) options Sheldon 2015 (verified against full text) [E]. A US expert meeting noted that no medicines are FDA-approved for POTS and that the evidence for many medicines used "is not robust" Vernino 2021 [E].

  • Overall. A review of 28 studies, mostly case series, found that treatments which increase blood volume, tighten blood vessels, control heart rate or improve exercise tolerance each helped 51% to 72% of people. The authors called the evidence "extremely limited" Wells 2018 [S].
  • Exercise. In 19 patients (18 women), both 4 weeks of propranolol and 3 months of exercise training lowered standing heart rate, but only training improved quality of life Fu 2011 [P].
  • Propranolol. In a short trial of 54 patients, low-dose propranolol eased heart rate and symptoms better than placebo. A higher dose slowed the heart more, but people felt worse Raj 2009 [P].
  • Ivabradine. In 22 patients with one subtype (hyperadrenergic POTS; 95.5% women), ivabradine lowered heart rate and improved physical and social functioning Taub 2021 [P].

The longer view. Of 172 people diagnosed with POTS as teenagers who answered a follow-up survey about 5.4 years later, 19% reported complete resolution and 51% persistent but improved symptoms. Their mental health scores were normal, though their physical health scores were below average Bhatia 2016 [P]. Only 34% of those invited replied, so this may not represent everyone.

10. ADHD medication when you have POTS

This is a decision to make with your prescriber and, ideally, whoever looks after your POTS. This guide doesn't recommend for or against any medicine. The evidence below is for that conversation; the full summary for clinicians is in G38.

  • On average, ADHD medicines raise pulse by a few bpm in adults. Stimulants are no worse on this than the non-stimulants atomoxetine and viloxazine Farhat 2025 [S].
  • In people with POTS, a single dose of atomoxetine raised standing heart rate and worsened symptoms compared with placebo Green 2013 [P]. The heart-rhythm statement advises against drugs that block norepinephrine reuptake in POTS Sheldon 2015 (verified against full text) [E].
  • No trial has tested amphetamines or lisdexamfetamine in POTS. No study has followed people with both ADHD and POTS on medication over time.

Practical tip: what to track. If you and your prescriber decide to try or change a medicine, a simple log helps. Note the date, dose time, your symptoms on standing (dizziness, racing heart, fog), and any heart-rate readings your clinician asks you to take, such as lying and standing. Bring the log to your follow-up.

11. Questions to bring to your appointment

Practical tip. You could ask: - "Do my heart-rate readings meet the criteria for POTS, or could something else explain them?" - "My concentration problems started when / before my dizziness started. Could this be POTS, ADHD, or both?" - "Should my iron (ferritin) be checked?" - "Is a graded exercise programme suitable for me, and where do I start if standing is hard?" - "How might my ADHD medicine affect my heart rate, and what should I monitor?"

What you can do

  • Practical tip: Keep a two-week symptom diary that notes position (lying, sitting, standing), time of day, cycle day, sleep and fluids. Bring it to appointments.
  • Practical tip: Write down when your attention problems started, with examples from school reports if you have them. This helps a clinician tell lifelong ADHD apart from newer POTS-related fog.
  • Practical tip: Ask before changing salt intake or starting supplements, including iron. They aren't right for everyone.
  • Practical tip: If exercise feels impossible upright, ask about programmes that start lying down or seated.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Dizziness, racing heart or fog that comes on when standing and eases when lying down.
  • Whether your attention problems date from childhood or began with your POTS symptoms.
  • Iron stores, especially if your periods are heavy.
  • How your symptoms change across your menstrual cycle, or if you are planning a pregnancy.
  • Any ADHD medicine you take or are considering, and how it affects your standing heart rate (see G38).

What we don't know yet

  • How often ADHD and POTS occur together. No study reports this in either direction. The only controlled data are 21 people with POTS whose inattention was not present in childhood Raj V 2009 [P].
  • How common POTS is in autistic people. The only data come from a referral series Owens 2021 [P] and a neurodivergence and hypermobility study Csecs 2022 [P]. There is no population estimate.
  • Women with both ADHD and POTS. No study looks at them specifically, even though POTS clinics are mostly women.
  • ADHD medicines in POTS. There are no trials of amphetamines or lisdexamfetamine and no long-term studies.
  • Iron. No trial tests whether iron treatment helps POTS.
  • The premenstrual days and PMDD. No study looks at the late-luteal phase specifically, or at PMDD and POTS together.
  • Mast cells. No study has tested the MCAS link using strict diagnostic criteria Farley 2025 [P].
  • Treatment. Trials are tiny; the largest randomised trials here have 54 people. No trial of compression garments or salt alone met our inclusion standard.

Popular claims checked

  • pots-is-just-anxiety: "POTS is just anxiety." Verdict: false. The racing heart is a physical response; worry tends to follow the symptoms.
  • pots-brain-fog-is-adhd: "POTS brain fog is really undiagnosed ADHD." Verdict: unsupported. Fog is common in POTS, but in one study it was not present in childhood. No study reports co-occurrence rates.
  • pots-adhd-heds-mcas-one-syndrome: "ADHD, POTS, hEDS and MCAS are one syndrome." Verdict: unsupported. Partial overlaps exist; a shared syndrome and a mast-cell link have not been shown.
  • pots-mostly-womens-condition: "POTS is mostly a women's condition." Verdict: mostly true. More than 75% of patients are female; men get it too.
  • pots-worse-before-period: "POTS gets worse before your period." Verdict: partly true. Small studies point to the period itself, not the days before.
  • pots-low-ferritin-causes-pots: "Low ferritin causes POTS, and iron fixes it." Verdict: unsupported. Low iron stores were common in one small teen study; treatment is untested.
  • pots-salt-and-water-cure: "Salt and water cure POTS." Verdict: overstated. They are a reasonable first step but a weak recommendation; exercise has better support.
  • pots-stimulants-dangerous: "Stimulants are dangerous if you have POTS." Verdict: overstated. The evidence is thin in both directions; it's an individual decision with your doctor.
