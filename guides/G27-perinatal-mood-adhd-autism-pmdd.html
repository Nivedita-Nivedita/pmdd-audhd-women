After the birth: perinatal mood when you have ADHD, autism or a PMDD history

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • If you have had PMDD or another premenstrual disorder, you are much more likely to be diagnosed with depression around pregnancy. In Swedish registers the link ran both ways.

  • Women with ADHD and autistic women also report or are diagnosed with more depression and anxiety around birth. Autistic mothers also often feel misunderstood by professionals.

  • These are group-level associations. They cannot tell you your own chance, and depression is not inevitable. A plan made in advance (who checks your mood, who you tell, what happens if it drops) is worth having.

  • Postpartum psychosis is rare but is a medical emergency: get same-day urgent help (jump to safety).

Evidence grade: premenstrual disorder link S (Supported); ADHD and autism findings mostly P (Preliminary). Headline grade P. · Talk to a clinician about: mood screening during pregnancy and after birth, and your history of PMDD, depression or anxiety.

Not medical advice. Crisis support →

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

A short glossary (definitions, not evidence claims):

  • Perinatal: during pregnancy and the first year after birth. Postpartum: after birth.
  • PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder): a severe, cyclical mood condition in the days before a period. Premenstrual disorders is the wider group, including PMDD and severe PMS.
  • Odds ratio (OR), hazard ratio (HR), prevalence ratio (PR): different ways of comparing two groups. For each, 1.00 means no difference. They are not interchangeable, so we name the measure each time.
  • Association: two things occurring together more often than chance in a group. It does not show cause, and it does not predict what will happen to any one person.

1. Premenstrual disorders and perinatal depression: a two-way link

This is the strongest finding in this guide.

In Swedish national registers covering 1,803,309 pregnancies, 84,949 women diagnosed with perinatal depression were compared with 849,482 matched women (Yang 2024) [S]:

  • A premenstrual disorder had been recorded before pregnancy in 2.9% of women with perinatal depression vs 0.6% of the comparison group (OR 4.76, 95% CI 4.52,5.01).
  • In the other direction, women with perinatal depression were later more likely to be diagnosed with a premenstrual disorder (HR 1.81, 1.74,1.88).
  • Comparing sisters weakened but did not remove these links. The authors read this as shared underlying factors, not one condition causing the other.

(Yang 2024) [S]

What these numbers mean for you. An odds ratio of 4.76 describes how much more common a premenstrual disorder history was among women with perinatal depression than among comparison women. It does not mean that a woman with PMDD has a 4.76-times chance, or any particular chance, of perinatal depression. In this study, a premenstrual disorder had been recorded in only 2.9% of women with perinatal depression.

Two reviews point the same way. Across 19 observational studies, women with a history of PMS before pregnancy had more than double the odds of postpartum depression (odds ratio 2.20, 95% CI 1.81-2.68), though the evidence was low quality (Cao 2020) [S]. In a review of seven studies on PMDD specifically, six found a positive link with postpartum depression and one did not (Pereira 2021) [P]. Its authors recommend routinely asking about PMDD in perinatal care (Pereira 2021) [P].

PMDD also matters for safety. A meta-analysis found higher odds of suicide attempt (odds ratio 6.97, 95% CI 2.98-16.29) and suicidal thoughts (odds ratio 3.95, 2.97-5.24) in women with PMDD (Prasad 2021) [S]. For the PMDD background, see G05.

2. ADHD: more postpartum depression and anxiety

Swedish registers. Among 773,047 women giving birth, 3,515 had ADHD (Andersson 2023) [S]. In the year after delivery, 16.76% of women with ADHD were diagnosed with depression (prevalence ratio 5.09, 4.68-5.54) and 24.92% with anxiety (PR 5.41, 5.06-5.78), beyond other risk factors (Andersson 2023) [S]. The authors note surveillance bias is possible: women already in contact with mental health services may simply be more likely to be diagnosed. Also note that 16.76% means most women with ADHD in this study did not receive a depression diagnosis that year.

A systematic review of ten studies. Perinatal depression in women with ADHD ranged from 16.76% to 57.6%, with higher odds than in women without ADHD (odds ratios 1.8-2.63) (Pierotti 2025) [P]. Most studies were of medium quality (Pierotti 2025) [P].

Couples in Canada. In 2,544 couples followed to 24 months after birth, mothers with possible ADHD (self-reported) had higher odds of depression (adjusted odds ratio 1.70, 1.13-2.56) and anxiety (adjusted odds ratio 1.74, 1.30-2.34) (Johnson 2025) [P]. Each parent's ADHD symptoms were also linked to the other's mental health (Johnson 2025) [P].

Undiagnosed ADHD after birth. In Denmark, new ADHD diagnoses in mothers rose above pre-pregnancy levels 2 to 5 years after birth (incidence rate ratio 1.24) (Madsen 2025) [S]. Among mothers diagnosed after birth, 53.9% had first had psychiatric contact or other psychotropic prescriptions (Madsen 2025) [S]. An expert review concludes that undiagnosed women have increased vulnerability to postpartum depression (Kooij 2025) [P]. If postpartum depression or anxiety only partly responds to treatment, ADHD may be worth asking about. See G29.

3. Autistic mothers: more stress, depression and anxiety, and feeling misunderstood

In an online survey of 355 autistic and 132 non-autistic mothers (all with at least one autistic child), autistic mothers were more likely to report depression before or after birth, difficulty multitasking and coping with domestic responsibilities, anxiety, isolation and feeling misunderstood by professionals (Pohl 2020) [P]. They also reported acting in their child's best interest (Pohl 2020) [P].

A study following parents from pregnancy to 6 months after birth found autistic parents had higher stress, depression and anxiety scores throughout (Hampton 2022) [P]. No clear differences were found in life satisfaction, parenting confidence or most parenting styles (Hampton 2022) [P]. The published summary does not give the sample size.

The message from both: higher distress, not worse parenting.

4. Does continuing stimulants affect postpartum mood?

Some women worry that staying on ADHD medication in pregnancy will harm their mood afterwards; others worry that stopping will. One large study looked at this (Xu 2026) [S]. Among 3,676 US women with ADHD who were taking stimulants regularly before pregnancy:

  • 41.4% stopped in pregnancy.
  • 16.2% overall had a new postpartum mood or anxiety diagnosis.
  • Continuers looked worse at first (19.3% vs 12.8%), but this disappeared after adjustment. No clear association was found between continuing and postpartum mental health admissions (risk ratio 1.01, 0.59-1.71) or new mood or anxiety diagnoses (risk ratio 1.30, 0.97-1.74).
  • Pre-existing mental health diagnoses were the strongest predictor of admissions (risk ratio 2.60).

(Xu 2026) [S]

So in this study, no clear association was found between the medication decision and postpartum mood after adjustment, while a past mental health diagnosis stood out as a predictor. The confidence interval for new diagnoses (0.97-1.74) does not rule out a modest increase, and one observational study cannot settle the question. The decision is one to make with your prescriber and obstetric team. G26 covers the pregnancy evidence.

5. Sleep loss and executive load: what we don't know yet

Many women with ADHD describe the newborn months as the point where their coping systems collapsed: broken sleep, constant small decisions and no routine. We did not find an eligible study testing this in our search (last updated October 2026). The postpartum sleep studies we found do not report results separately for women with ADHD, and we did not find a study measuring executive-function load in new mothers with ADHD or autism. A review of ADHD in pregnancy and early parenthood notes that data on early parent-child interaction are lacking (Kittel-Schneider 2021) [P].

Practical tip: even without studies, it is reasonable to plan for this. Agree in advance who covers which night feeds, write down routines before the birth, and decide which tasks can simply be dropped for three months.

6. What guidelines recommend: screening and referral

These are clinical recommendations from the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), not research findings. They apply to all women, not specifically to women with ADHD, autism or PMDD.

  • Ask about mood at the start and after birth. NICE recommends that at the first contact in pregnancy (the booking visit) and in the early weeks after birth, clinicians consider asking two short questions about feeling down or hopeless and about losing interest or pleasure, plus two short anxiety questions (the GAD-2) (NICE CG192 [Guideline]). It recommends considering these questions again at later contacts during pregnancy and the first year after birth (NICE CG192 [Guideline]).
  • Ask about severe mental illness. At the first contact, NICE recommends asking about any past or present severe mental illness, past specialist or inpatient treatment, and severe perinatal mental illness in a mother, sister or daughter (NICE CG192 [Guideline]).
  • Refer when needed. Women who have, or have had, severe mental illness should be referred to a specialist (preferably perinatal) mental health service (NICE CG192 [Guideline]).
  • Postpartum psychosis. Clinicians should watch for symptoms in the first 2 weeks after birth in women with a personal history of severe mental illness or a family history of severe perinatal mental illness, and a woman with sudden symptoms should be assessed within 4 hours of referral (NICE CG192 [Guideline]).

Practical tip: if a PMDD history, ADHD or autism isn't asked about, you can raise it yourself at the booking visit.

7. A perinatal plan (one-page handout)

Practical tip: fill this in during pregnancy and share it with your midwife, GP or obstetric team. These are prompts, not evidence claims.

My perinatal mood plan

My history (tick any): PMDD or severe PMS ☐ · depression ☐ · anxiety ☐ · ADHD ☐ · autism ☐ · past postpartum depression ☐ · other: ____

Screening: who will ask about my mood, and when? (for example, each antenatal visit, and at set points after birth)

Early signs for me: (for example, not sleeping when the baby sleeps, stopping eating, shutting down, intrusive frightening thoughts, rage)

Who I will tell: partner / friend / family member: _ · professional: _

What I need professionals to know (especially if autistic): how I communicate best (written, plain, in advance), sensory needs on the ward, what "fine" might hide.

Medication: my ADHD medication plan and review date, agreed with my prescriber: ____

If things get bad: my GP / midwife / perinatal team number: _ · crisis line: _ · emergency number. Signs of postpartum psychosis (confusion, hallucinations, strange beliefs, feeling very "high", rapidly changing mood) need same-day emergency help.

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. Tell your midwife or doctor about any PMDD or premenstrual mood history. It is the clearest risk flag in this guide, and it may not be asked about.
  2. Name your ADHD or autism in your notes, with what helps you communicate.
  3. Make the plan above before the birth, when you have more capacity.
  4. Ask someone you trust to watch for your early signs. People often notice changes in us before we do.
  5. Plan the nights. Protecting some sleep is a practical goal, even though we did not find a study of it in ADHD.
  6. If you track your cycle, restart tracking once periods return; for some women, premenstrual mood returns with them. See G04.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Your history of PMDD, premenstrual disorders, depression or anxiety, and how often your mood will be checked.
  • How your ADHD or autism may affect what you report, and the best way to ask you.
  • Your medication plan for pregnancy, after birth and feeding (G26).
  • Whether ADHD might be part of the picture if postpartum depression or anxiety only partly improves.
  • Any thoughts of harming yourself or your baby. These can be told to a clinician, and help is available.

What we don't know yet

  • Sleep loss and executive load in new mothers with ADHD or autism. We did not find an eligible study in our search (last updated October 2026).
  • Whether treatment works differently. We did not find a dedicated trial of postpartum depression treatment in women with ADHD, autism or a PMDD history. That is not the same as saying no trial included such women; most trials do not report results for them separately.
  • Prospectively confirmed PMDD. The perinatal studies mostly used recorded diagnoses or recall, not daily cycle tracking.
  • Autistic mothers' outcomes in registry data. The autism evidence here is from surveys, including one where every mother had an autistic child.
  • Whether better screening changes outcomes for these groups. We did not find an eligible study in our search.
  • Individual prediction. The studies here describe groups. None gives a tool for predicting which individual woman will become unwell.

Safety and crisis resources

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

ADHD on its own is associated with suicidal thoughts (odds ratio 3.53) and attempts (odds ratio 2.37) (Septier 2019) [E], and PMDD is too (Prasad 2021) [S].

  • Thoughts of suicide or of harming yourself or your baby: get help now. Don't wait for them to pass, or for your period or the next appointment. See crisis help.
  • Postpartum psychosis is a medical emergency. Symptoms usually start suddenly within the first 2 weeks after birth and can include hallucinations, strange or fearful beliefs, feeling very "high" or overactive, confusion, or rapidly changing mood (NHS: Postpartum psychosis [Reference]). It can get worse quickly. Get an urgent same-day assessment, and call your local emergency number if anyone may be in immediate danger (NHS: Postpartum psychosis [Reference]). If you were told you are at high risk and have a care plan, use the crisis contact in it (NHS: Postpartum psychosis [Reference]).
  • Other warning signs: contact your midwife, GP or perinatal team the same day if your mood drops sharply, you can't sleep even when the baby sleeps, or you have thoughts that frighten you.

Popular claims checked

  • PMDD seriously raises suicide risk. Verdict: mostly true (supported). PMDD is linked to substantially higher risk of suicidal thoughts and attempts (Prasad 2021) [S].
  • Women with ADHD are often treated for anxiety or depression for years before anyone spots the ADHD. Verdict: mostly true (supported). After birth, over half of Danish mothers newly diagnosed with ADHD had first had psychiatric contact or other psychotropic prescriptions (Madsen 2025) [S].
  • "If you had PMDD, you will get postpartum depression." Verdict: overstated. A premenstrual disorder history is clearly associated with perinatal depression at group level (Yang 2024) [S], but an association is not a prediction for any individual, and one of seven studies did not find a positive link (Pereira 2021) [P].
  • "Staying on stimulants in pregnancy makes postpartum depression worse." Verdict: mostly true. No clear association after adjustment in 3,676 women, though the interval for new diagnoses does not rule out a modest increase (Xu 2026) [S].
