For clinicians: autism and ADHD in women with eating disorders

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Autistic traits are elevated in anorexia nervosa (AN) (pooled g = 0.88), and 29% of AN patients studied scored above the ADOS cut-off (a screen-positive, not a diagnosis). The association is real but non-specific in registry data, and traits are elevated mainly during acute illness in the one prospective twin study.

  • ADHD is associated with eating disorders in both directions (pooled odds ratio 3.82 for any ED in ADHD), most consistently via impulsivity and binge-type eating. In women in a representative cross-sectional US survey, ADHD onset was reported before ED onset.

  • Screen for both conditions and offer adjustments and assessment without waiting for recovery or weight restoration. Anchor autism assessment in developmental history from before ED onset, and interpret trait scores measured in the acute, underweight phase cautiously. We did not find a trial of adapted ED treatment for autistic or ADHD patients in our search (last updated October 2026).

Evidence grade: SUPPORTED (S). AN–autism association and ADHD–ED association: S (meta-analyses, registry, twin cohort). Self-report AQ data: P. Adapted treatment: not established (gap). Headline grade: SUPPORTED (S), with the treatment section moved to "What we don't know yet".

Talk to a clinician about… (patient-facing prompts) see the section near the end.

Not medical advice.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait for it to pass. See crisis support.

Tags: [E] established · [S] supported · [P] preliminary · [U] no direct evidence. Practice point paragraphs are clinical suggestions, not graded evidence.

1. Autism and anorexia: pooled traits and ADOS screen rates

  • Pooled traits and ADOS. Meta-analysis of 22 studies (1,172 AN patients, 2,747 controls): autistic traits higher in AN than controls (g = 0.88), modestly correlated with ED severity (r = 0.28); 29% scored above the ADOS cut-off Inal-Kaleli 2024 [S].
  • Self-report. Meta-analysis of seven studies: higher AQ scores in AN than controls; the authors note the cause of the traits is unclear and call for robust clinical measures Westwood 2015 [P].

Practice point. An ADOS score above cut-off is a screen-positive, not a diagnosis. Whether trait elevation reflects stable autism or the illness phase is addressed in section 2.

2. Registry and twin evidence: non-specific and phase-dependent

  • Non-specific. Danish nationwide registers (everyone born 1981-2008 plus parents and siblings): autism comorbidity was increased in AN and autism aggregated in relatives, but no clear difference was found between this increase and that for comorbid and familial depression or any psychiatric disorder Koch 2015 [S].
  • Phase-dependent. In 5,987 Swedish twins (52.4% female), children later diagnosed with AN did not have elevated autistic traits at age 9; at 18, restricted/repetitive traits were markedly elevated only in those with acute AN Dinkler 2020 [S]. The authors discuss two explanations: a female phenotype that masks traits in childhood, or an acute-illness effect Dinkler 2020 [S].

Practice point. Both explanations are open. Starvation-related rigidity and true autism can coexist; neither should be assumed.

3. ADHD and disordered eating

  • Bidirectional association. Meta-analysis of 17 studies: ADHD associated with any ED (pooled odds ratio 3.82, 2.34-6.24; AN 4.28, BN 5.71, BED 4.13) Nazar 2016 [E]. Stronger when ADHD was diagnosed by clinical interview (odds ratio 5.89) than self-report (2.23); people with EDs had higher odds of ADHD (odds ratio 2.57), and 5.77 in binge-eating cohorts Nazar 2016 [E].
  • Which features. Systematic review of 75 studies: moderate-strength evidence for an ADHD–disordered eating association, especially overeating; impulsivity consistently linked to overeating and bulimia; limited evidence linking hyperactivity to restrictive eating in males but not females Kaisari 2017 [S].
  • Women, and order of onset. In the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication (a nationally representative cross-sectional survey, with onset ages reported retrospectively), binge eating and BED were associated with ADHD in both sexes; in women, BN and lifetime AN were also associated, and ADHD invariably began before the ED Brewerton 2016 [S].
  • Adult services. Among 5,282,877 insured US adults, eating disorders were associated with an ADHD diagnosis (odds ratio 5.192) Chung 2019 [S].

Practice point. Binge-type presentations are where the ADHD signal is strongest; do not exclude ADHD in restrictive presentations in women, given Brewerton's AN finding.

4. Assessment timing and adapted treatment

Timing is supported indirectly: autistic traits were elevated mainly in acute AN in the one prospective study Dinkler 2020 [S]. This argues for careful interpretation of state-dependent measures, not for withholding support or assessment until recovery. Adapted treatment: we did not find an eligible trial in our search, so it is covered under "What we don't know yet".

What to do in practice

  1. Screen routinely for both. ADHD and autistic traits are common in ED populations Nazar 2016 [E] Inal-Kaleli 2024 [S].
  2. Take a developmental history before illness onset. Childhood traits were not elevated in pre-AN children in the twin cohort Dinkler 2020 [S]; informant history from before ED onset helps separate trait from state. Practice point.
  3. Interpret acute-phase trait measures cautiously, but don't make recovery a precondition. Support, adjustments and autism assessment need not wait for weight restoration. Give most weight to the developmental history, treat AQ/ADOS results during acute AN as provisional, and repeat them later if the picture is unclear Dinkler 2020 [S] Westwood 2015 [P].
  4. Don't treat "anorexia = female autism" as a working model. The registry association is non-specific Koch 2015 [S].
  5. Use interview-based ADHD assessment where possible; associations were stronger with clinical-interview diagnosis than self-report Nazar 2016 [E].
  6. Ask about ADHD symptom onset relative to the ED, particularly in BN and BED Brewerton 2016 [S].
  7. Adapt delivery pragmatically while evidence is lacking. Practice point: sensory-aware meal settings, explicit structure, written plans and reminders. These are clinical suggestions, not tested interventions.
  8. Ask about suicidality and self-harm as part of routine ED risk assessment. Practice point.

Talk to a clinician about…

Prompts you can give patients: - "I've always found social situations and change hard, even before the eating disorder. Could I be assessed for autism?" - "My eating feels impulsive and I've struggled with focus since childhood. Could ADHD be part of this?" - "Can we start looking at autism now, using my history from before the eating disorder, and recheck any questionnaire scores later?"

What we don't know yet

  • Adapted ED treatment for autistic or ADHD patients. We did not find an eligible trial or controlled study in our search.
  • Whether ADHD medication changes ED outcomes. Not searched for this guide; any medication decision in an ED needs the prescriber and ED team to weigh harms.
  • How to time and interpret standardised autism measures during and after acute illness; Dinkler 2020 shows the phase effect but doesn't define a timepoint.
  • Women-specific ADHD–ED data beyond Brewerton 2016; hyperactivity–restriction links were found in males only Kaisari 2017 [S].
  • Validity of ADOS and AQ cut-offs in acutely underweight patients.
  • Autism in BN and BED: the evidence we found is mostly about AN.

Popular claims checked

  • You may have heard: "Anorexia is the female form of autism." What the research supports: anorexia and autism are associated, but the link is not specific enough to make one a form of the other. The association is real but non-specific in registry data Koch 2015 [S], and traits may partly reflect acute illness Dinkler 2020 [S].
  • You may have heard: "A high AQ in an anorexia patient means she's autistic." What the research supports: a high AQ score in anorexia does not by itself establish autism. AQ is self-report and its cause in AN is unclear Westwood 2015 [P]; scores can be state-dependent Dinkler 2020 [S].
  • You may have heard: "ADHD only matters for binge eating." What the research supports: ADHD is linked most strongly with binge-type eating, but also with anorexia. The link is strongest for binge-type eating, but ADHD was also associated with AN in pooled data and in women Nazar 2016 [E] Brewerton 2016 [S].
