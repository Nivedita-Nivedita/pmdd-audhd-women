ADHD after 60: late diagnosis, memory worries and dementia

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • ADHD doesn't stop at 60. A meta-analysis and a population study estimate that about 2.18% to 2.8% of older adults meet ADHD criteria on rating scales or a diagnostic interview, depending on the method, but far fewer (about 0.23% in the meta-analysis) have a clinical diagnosis, and fewer still are treated.

  • Lifelong ADHD can look like early memory decline, and doctors can't always tell them apart. In one study, no clear difference in overall memory or thinking was found in older adults with ADHD once depression was taken into account.

  • Adults with an ADHD diagnosis were more likely to develop dementia later in one large study. That is an association, not proof of cause, and it does not predict any one person's risk. A review of menopause hormone therapy did not find a clear association with dementia (certainty varies); HRT should not be started to prevent dementia.

Evidence grade: SUPPORTED (S) for prevalence, under-diagnosis and the dementia association · PRELIMINARY (P) for telling ADHD apart from mild cognitive impairment · none of the studies we found reports older women separately. Headline grade: PRELIMINARY (P).

Talk to a clinician about… a lifelong pattern of attention problems, as well as any recent change in memory, and whether depression or sleep is playing a part.

Not medical advice. Crisis support →

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

Before you read: none of the studies in this guide report results for older women separately. Everything below is about older adults in general.

Tags: [E] established · [S] supported · [P] preliminary · [U] no direct evidence. Practical tip paragraphs are general guidance, not graded evidence.

1. How common is ADHD after 60?

  • The first population study. In the Longitudinal Aging Study Amsterdam, 1,494 older adults were screened and 231 had a diagnostic interview Michielsen 2012 [S]. The weighted prevalence was 2.8% for full ("syndromatic") ADHD and 4.2% for ADHD symptoms Michielsen 2012 [S]. Adults aged 60 to 70 reported more symptoms than those aged 71 to 94 Michielsen 2012 [S].
  • A meta-analysis. Across 20 studies (32 datasets), pooled ADHD prevalence in older adults was 2.18% using validated rating scales, but only 0.23% using clinical diagnosis and 0.09% using treatment rates Dobrosavljevic 2020 [S]. The authors highlight under-diagnosis and under-treatment in older adults Dobrosavljevic 2020 [S].
  • Global context. Adult ADHD prevalence decreases with age, but it does not disappear Song 2021 [S].

What this means for you: if you recognise a lifelong pattern, it is not too late to ask. The gap between 2.18% with symptoms and 0.23% diagnosed suggests most older adults with ADHD symptoms have never been assessed Dobrosavljevic 2020 [S].

2. ADHD or mild cognitive impairment?

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is a noticeable decline in memory or thinking that is greater than expected for age but doesn't stop someone managing daily life. It can sometimes be an early stage of dementia.

  • They can look alike. A narrative review notes that ADHD symptoms such as trouble sustaining attention, disorganisation and forgetfulness can closely resemble MCI, particularly over age 50 Callahan 2017 [P]. Both share sleep problems, depression and anxiety Callahan 2017 [P]. The review says it is unclear whether ADHD is linked to early dementia or is sometimes misdiagnosed as MCI Callahan 2017 [P].
  • ADHD didn't look like global decline. In 231 adults aged 60 to 94, ADHD was linked only to lower attention and working memory, and even this was no longer clear after adjusting for depression Semeijn 2015 [P]. No clear differences were found in memory, processing speed or executive function Semeijn 2015 [P]. This is one cross-sectional study.

The key difference to look for is history. Practical tip: ask yourself, and tell the clinician, whether these problems have been there for as long as you can remember or are a change from how you used to be. Bring examples from across your life to any memory appointment.

3. Dementia risk: association, family links and the stimulant subgroup

  • An association. In 109,218 Israelis born 1933-1952 and followed from 2003 to 2020, adult ADHD was associated with a higher risk of dementia (adjusted hazard ratio 2.77, 95% CI 2.11-3.63) Levine 2023 [S]. The result held in most sensitivity analyses, with only mild evidence of reverse causation (early dementia being mistaken for ADHD) Levine 2023 [S]. A hazard ratio above 1 means new dementia occurred at a higher rate in the ADHD group over the follow-up. The abstract we hold does not give absolute risks.
  • The stimulant subgroup. No clear increase in dementia risk was found among people who had received stimulant medication Levine 2023 [S]. This is an observational subgroup finding. It does not show that stimulants prevent dementia, or that untreated ADHD causes it, because people who are treated may differ in many ways from those who aren't.
  • Family links. In Swedish registers linking relatives of 2,132,929 people, parents of people with ADHD had a higher risk of Alzheimer's disease (hazard ratio 1.55, 95% CI 1.26-1.89), with weaker links in grandparents and aunts and uncles Zhang 2021 [S]. The authors suggest shared family risk Zhang 2021 [S]. This is relevant if you were recognised after your child or grandchild was diagnosed.

What this means for you: having ADHD does not mean you will develop dementia. These studies show that, across large groups, the two are linked. They do not tell you your own risk, and they don't show that ADHD causes dementia.

4. HRT is not a dementia-prevention strategy

  • A meta-analysis of ten studies (one randomised trial and nine observational studies) with 1,016,055 participants did not find a clear association between menopause hormone therapy and MCI or dementia Melville 2025 [S]. It also did not find clear differences by timing, duration or type Melville 2025 [S].
  • The certainty of the evidence ranged from moderate to very low Melville 2025 [S]. Not finding a clear association does not prove risk is unchanged for every type of HRT, age or group. The authors conclude that hormone therapy should be prescribed for its other benefits and risks, not for dementia prevention Melville 2025 [S].
  • Starting HRT later in life. UK guidance notes that dementia risk might increase with combined HRT if it is started at 65 or over, and is unlikely to increase with oestrogen-only HRT (NICE NG23 [Guideline]). The same guidance lists other risks to weigh, including blood clots with oral HRT and stroke with oral-oestrogen combined HRT, which rises with age at first starting (NICE NG23 [Guideline]).

We did not find an eligible study of hormone therapy for ADHD symptoms in older women in our search (last updated October 2026); see G30 for midlife.

5. Questions for a memory-clinic appointment

This section is practical guidance rather than graded evidence. See the handout below.

What you can do

  • Write a lifelong timeline. Practical tip: note school reports, work patterns, lost keys and missed deadlines across decades. This helps a clinician separate a lifelong pattern from a recent change.
  • Bring someone who knows you. Practical tip: a sibling, partner or old friend can describe what you were like years ago.
  • Mention mood and sleep. In one cross-sectional study, the small attention differences were no longer clear after adjusting for depression Semeijn 2015 [P], and sleep, depression and anxiety overlap between ADHD and MCI Callahan 2017 [P].
  • Mention family history. Practical tip: tell the clinician if your children or grandchildren have ADHD, and if relatives had dementia.
  • Don't start or stop HRT to protect your memory. Discuss hormone therapy for its other benefits and risks Melville 2025 [S], especially if you are considering starting it at 65 or over (NICE NG23) [Guideline].

One-page handout: ADHD, memory and the over-60s

Questions to ask at a memory or GP appointment (Practical tips) 1. "Could my lifelong attention problems be ADHD, rather than, or as well as, a new memory problem?" 2. "Can we look at my history from childhood, not just the last year?" 3. "Have depression, anxiety and sleep been checked as part of this?" 4. "Is my test pattern one of attention and working memory, or of wider decline?" 5. "If I have ADHD, what treatment options are suitable at my age, given my heart health and other medicines?" 6. "What follow-up do you suggest so we can see whether anything changes over time?"

Point Evidence
ADHD persists into older age; most are undiagnosed 2.18% on scales vs 0.23% diagnosed Dobrosavljevic 2020 [S]
ADHD can mimic MCI Callahan 2017 [P]
ADHD alone did not mean global cognitive decline; check depression Semeijn 2015 [P]
ADHD is associated with later dementia (association, not cause) Hazard ratio 2.77 Levine 2023 [S]
HRT: no clear association with dementia found; certainty moderate to very low; not for dementia prevention Melville 2025 [S]; NICE NG23 [Guideline]

Talk to a clinician about…

  • A full ADHD assessment that includes your childhood history, even if you are over 60.
  • Whether memory tests should be interpreted alongside possible ADHD.
  • Low mood, anxiety and sleep problems.
  • The safety of ADHD medication alongside your heart health and other medicines.
  • Hormone therapy, for its own benefits and risks rather than for memory.

What we don't know yet

  • Older women specifically. None of the older-adult ADHD studies we found reports women separately.
  • First diagnosis after 60 in women. We did not find an eligible study in our search (last updated October 2026).
  • How to tell ADHD from MCI reliably. The evidence is a narrative review and one cross-sectional study; there is no validated method.
  • Whether treating ADHD changes dementia risk. The stimulant finding is observational only.
  • Absolute dementia risk for an individual with ADHD; the abstracts give relative risks.
  • ADHD medication safety and benefit in people over 60. We did not find a dedicated trial in this age group in our search. That does not mean no trial included older adults.
  • HRT and dementia by regimen and age. The meta-analysis did not find a clear association overall; guidance flags possible risk with combined HRT started at 65 or over.

Popular claims checked

  • You may have heard: "ADHD can start in adulthood." What the research supports: in a long follow-up study, apparent adult-onset ADHD was not found apart from a complex psychiatric history. In 239 people without childhood ADHD, assessed 8 times from a mean age of 9.89 to 24.40 years, there was no evidence for adult-onset ADHD independent of a complex psychiatric history Sibley 2018 [S]. For older adults this matters because ADHD and early cognitive decline can look alike Callahan 2017 [P], so a new problem late in life should be assessed on its own terms.
  • You may have heard: "ADHD causes dementia." What the research supports: ADHD has been linked with later dementia, but a cause has not been shown. There is an association in one large cohort that held in most sensitivity analyses, not proof of cause Levine 2023 [S], and shared family risk is one suggested explanation Zhang 2021 [S].
  • You may have heard: "ADHD meds protect against dementia." What the research supports: protection has not been shown. There was no clear rise in risk among stimulant users, which is not the same as protection Levine 2023 [S].
  • You may have heard: "HRT protects your brain from dementia." What the research supports: HRT has not been shown to lower dementia risk. A meta-analysis did not find a clear association, with certainty from moderate to very low, and its authors conclude HRT should not be prescribed to prevent dementia Melville 2025 [S].
