Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • ADHD doesn't stop at 60. Studies estimate that 2.18% to 2.8% of older adults have ADHD, depending on the method, but far fewer are ever diagnosed or treated.

  • Lifelong ADHD can look like early memory decline, and doctors can't always tell them apart. In one study, older adults with ADHD did not have worse memory or thinking overall once depression was taken into account.

  • Adults with an ADHD diagnosis were more likely to develop dementia later in one large study. That is an association, not proof of cause. Menopause hormone therapy neither raised nor lowered dementia risk.

Evidence grade: SUPPORTED (S) for prevalence, under-diagnosis and the dementia association · PRELIMINARY (P) for telling ADHD apart from mild cognitive impairment · no study reports older women separately. Headline grade: PRELIMINARY (P).

Talk to a clinician about… a lifelong pattern of attention problems, as well as any recent change in memory, and whether depression or sleep is playing a part.

Not medical advice. Crisis support →

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

Before you read: none of the studies in this guide report results for older women separately. Everything below is about older adults in general.

Tags: [E] established · [S] supported · [P] preliminary · [U] popular but unsupported. Practical tip paragraphs are general guidance, not graded evidence.

1. How common is ADHD after 60?

  • The first population study. In the Longitudinal Aging Study Amsterdam, 1,494 older adults were screened and 231 had a diagnostic interview Michielsen 2012 [S]. The weighted prevalence was 2.8% for full ("syndromatic") ADHD and 4.2% for ADHD symptoms Michielsen 2012 [S]. Adults aged 60 to 70 reported more symptoms than those aged 71 to 94 Michielsen 2012 [S].
  • A meta-analysis. Across 20 studies (32 datasets), pooled ADHD prevalence in older adults was 2.18% using validated rating scales, but only 0.23% using clinical diagnosis and 0.09% using treatment rates Dobrosavljevic 2020 [S]. The authors highlight under-diagnosis and under-treatment in older adults Dobrosavljevic 2020 [S].
  • Global context. Adult ADHD prevalence decreases with age, but it does not disappear Song 2021 [S].

What this means for you: if you recognise a lifelong pattern, it is not too late to ask. The gap between 2.18% with symptoms and 0.23% diagnosed suggests most older adults with ADHD symptoms have never been assessed Dobrosavljevic 2020 [S].

2. ADHD or mild cognitive impairment?

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is a noticeable decline in memory or thinking that is greater than expected for age but doesn't stop someone managing daily life. It can sometimes be an early stage of dementia.

  • They can look alike. A narrative review notes that ADHD symptoms such as trouble sustaining attention, disorganisation and forgetfulness can closely resemble MCI, particularly over age 50 Callahan 2017 [P]. Both share sleep problems, depression and anxiety Callahan 2017 [P]. The review says it is unclear whether ADHD is linked to early dementia or is sometimes misdiagnosed as MCI Callahan 2017 [P].
  • ADHD didn't look like global decline. In 231 adults aged 60 to 94, ADHD was linked only to lower attention and working memory, and even this disappeared after adjusting for depression Semeijn 2015 [P]. Memory, processing speed and executive function did not differ Semeijn 2015 [P].

The key difference to look for is history. Practical tip: ask yourself, and tell the clinician, whether these problems have been there for as long as you can remember or are a change from how you used to be. Bring examples from across your life to any memory appointment.

3. Dementia risk: association, family links and the stimulant subgroup

  • An association. In 109,218 Israelis born 1933-1952 and followed from 2003 to 2020, adult ADHD was associated with a higher risk of dementia (adjusted HR 2.77, 95% CI 2.11-3.63) Levine 2023 [S]. The result held in most sensitivity analyses, with only mild evidence of reverse causation (early dementia being mistaken for ADHD) Levine 2023 [S]. A hazard ratio above 1 means new dementia occurred at a higher rate in the ADHD group over the follow-up. The abstract we hold does not give absolute risks.
  • The stimulant subgroup. There was no clear increase in dementia risk among people who had received stimulant medication Levine 2023 [S]. This is an observational finding. It does not show that stimulants prevent dementia, because people who are treated may differ in many ways from those who aren't.
  • Family links. In Swedish registers linking relatives of 2,132,929 people, parents of people with ADHD had a higher risk of Alzheimer's disease (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.26-1.89), with weaker links in grandparents and aunts and uncles Zhang 2021 [S]. The authors suggest shared family risk Zhang 2021 [S]. This is relevant if you were recognised after your child or grandchild was diagnosed.

What this means for you: having ADHD does not mean you will develop dementia. These studies show that, across large groups, the two are linked. They do not tell you your own risk, and they don't show that ADHD causes dementia.

4. HRT is not a dementia-prevention strategy

  • A meta-analysis of ten studies (one randomised trial and nine observational studies) with 1,016,055 participants found no significant link between menopause hormone therapy and MCI or dementia Melville 2025 [S]. Timing, duration and type made no difference Melville 2025 [S].
  • The authors conclude that hormone therapy should be prescribed for its other benefits and risks, not for dementia prevention Melville 2025 [S]. The certainty of the evidence ranged from moderate to very low Melville 2025 [S].

No study in our library tests hormone therapy for ADHD symptoms in older women (see G30 for midlife).

5. Questions for a memory-clinic appointment

This section is practical guidance rather than graded evidence. See the handout below.

What you can do

  • Write a lifelong timeline. Practical tip: note school reports, work patterns, lost keys and missed deadlines across decades. This helps a clinician separate a lifelong pattern from a recent change.
  • Bring someone who knows you. Practical tip: a sibling, partner or old friend can describe what you were like years ago.
  • Mention mood and sleep. Depression explained the small attention differences in one study Semeijn 2015 [P], and sleep, depression and anxiety overlap between ADHD and MCI Callahan 2017 [P].
  • Mention family history. Practical tip: tell the clinician if your children or grandchildren have ADHD, and if relatives had dementia.
  • Don't start or stop HRT to protect your memory. Discuss hormone therapy for its other benefits and risks Melville 2025 [S].

One-page handout: ADHD, memory and the over-60s

Questions to ask at a memory or GP appointment (Practical tips) 1. "Could my lifelong attention problems be ADHD, rather than, or as well as, a new memory problem?" 2. "Can we look at my history from childhood, not just the last year?" 3. "Have depression, anxiety and sleep been checked as part of this?" 4. "Is my test pattern one of attention and working memory, or of wider decline?" 5. "If I have ADHD, what treatment options are suitable at my age, given my heart health and other medicines?" 6. "What follow-up do you suggest so we can see whether anything changes over time?"

Point Evidence
ADHD persists into older age; most are undiagnosed 2.18% on scales vs 0.23% diagnosed Dobrosavljevic 2020 [S]
ADHD can mimic MCI Callahan 2017 [P]
ADHD alone did not mean global cognitive decline; check depression Semeijn 2015 [P]
ADHD is associated with later dementia HR 2.77 Levine 2023 [S]
HRT does not change dementia risk Melville 2025 [S]

Talk to a clinician about…

  • A full ADHD assessment that includes your childhood history, even if you are over 60.
  • Whether memory tests should be interpreted alongside possible ADHD.
  • Low mood, anxiety and sleep problems.
  • The safety of ADHD medication alongside your heart health and other medicines.
  • Hormone therapy, for its own benefits and risks rather than for memory.

What we don't know yet

  • Older women specifically. No older-adult ADHD study in our library reports women separately.
  • First diagnosis after 60 in women. No study of late-life first diagnosis in women was found.
  • How to tell ADHD from MCI reliably. The evidence is a narrative review and one cross-sectional study; there is no validated method.
  • Whether treating ADHD changes dementia risk. The stimulant finding is observational only.
  • Absolute dementia risk for an individual with ADHD; the abstracts give relative risks.
  • ADHD medication safety and benefit in people over 60. No trial in this age group is in our library.

Popular claims checked

  • adhd-can-start-in-adulthood: "ADHD can start in adulthood." Verdict: overstated. The ledger's better wording is "late recognition", not "late onset". For older adults this matters because ADHD and early cognitive decline can look alike Callahan 2017 [P], so a new problem late in life should be assessed on its own terms.
  • "ADHD causes dementia." (Not yet in the claims ledger.) Verdict: overstated. There is a robust association in one large cohort, not proof of cause Levine 2023 [S], and shared family risk is one suggested explanation Zhang 2021 [S].
  • "ADHD meds protect against dementia." (Not yet in the claims ledger.) Verdict: unsupported. There was no clear rise in risk among stimulant users, which is not the same as protection Levine 2023 [S].
  • "HRT protects your brain from dementia." (Not yet in the claims ledger.) Verdict: unsupported. No association either way Melville 2025 [S].
