Guides › G16

Students

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short - Your difficulties with planning, time and starting work are real, well-documented features of ADHD. They often don't show up on tests, and "just plan better" misses the point. - The mechanisms are fairly well understood: a fast internal clock, a pull towards near rewards, a wandering mind, a late body clock. Each suggests a workaround. - The workarounds themselves are mostly untested. The treatments with the strongest trial evidence for core ADHD symptoms are medications.

Evidence grade: Preliminary (P). The mechanisms are mostly Supported (S) or Established (E). Hyperfocus and mind-wandering are Preliminary (P). Almost none of the specific strategies in this guide has been tested in a trial. They are labelled "Practical tip": they are not evidence.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

How to read this guide

Research claims have a source and a grade: [E] established, [S] supported, [P] preliminary, [U] popular but unsupported. Practical tip marks ideas that follow from the research or from practice but haven't been tested. Try them as experiments. Keep what works for you.

"Executive function" (EF) means the mental skills that let you plan, start, switch, hold things in mind and manage time.

1. Why "just plan better" fails

Planning advice assumes the problem is knowledge. It usually isn't.

EF tests and EF in daily life are different things. Across 20 studies, only 24% of correlations between EF tests and everyday EF rating scales were significant (median r=.19) Toplak 2012 [E]. In adults followed up from childhood, self-reported EF problems predicted impairment at work and in daily life better than EF tests did Barkley 2011 [S].

This matters if you are bright. High-IQ adults with ADHD scored worse than high-IQ controls on several EF tests, even though their scores were in the average range Antshel 2010 [S]. In 157 adults with ADHD and IQ of 120 or more, 73% were significantly impaired on at least 5 of 8 EF measures, which combined test scores and self-report Brown 2009 [S]. That study had no control group.

So you may know exactly what a good plan looks like and still be unable to run it.

Practical tip. Instead of making a better plan, change the setup around you: make time visible, make outputs small, and add outside structure. The sections below suggest how.

2. Time: an accelerated internal clock

Across 55 studies, people with ADHD had more trouble judging very short durations, more variable estimates of durations lasting several seconds, and errors that suggest an internal clock that runs fast Marx 2022 [E]. A review of neurocognitive and imaging studies found timing problems were consistent in ADHD, and that methylphenidate reduced most of them Noreika 2013 [S]. These are lab tasks, mostly in young people. Whether they explain everyday lateness is not proven.

Practical tip: make time visible. - Use a timer you can see (a physical one, not a phone you'll pick up). - Before a task, write down how long you think it will take. Afterwards, write down how long it took. After a few weeks, you'll have your own correction factor. - Put fixed anchors in the day: a 10 a.m. check-in with a lab mate, a 5 p.m. walk.

3. Mind-wandering and cognitive disengagement

One hypothesis is that in ADHD, the brain's "default mode" (the network active when you daydream) doesn't switch off properly during tasks, leading to excessive mind-wandering Bozhilova 2018 [P]. This is a proposal, not settled science. A questionnaire of excessive mind-wandering separated adults with and without ADHD well, and mind-wandering added to impairment beyond core ADHD symptoms Mowlem 2016 [S].

A related idea is "cognitive disengagement syndrome" (formerly "sluggish cognitive tempo"): daydreaming, mental fogginess and slowness. An international work group concluded it is a distinct syndrome, but whether it is a separate disorder or part of others remains unclear Becker 2023 [S].

Practical tip: catch the drift. - Keep a "parking" notepad beside you. When an unrelated thought arrives, write it down and return to the task. - Work in short blocks with a defined output ("write the methods paragraph"), not open time ("work on chapter 2"). - Reading papers: read with a question in mind and write one sentence per section. Passive reading invites drift.

4. Hyperfocus: use it, protect against the crash

In two online samples (251 and 372 adults), people with more ADHD symptoms reported more hyperfocus, including at school, in hobbies and during screen time Hupfeld 2018 [P]. This is self-report, and there is no agreed definition of hyperfocus yet. The "crash" afterwards (exhaustion, missed meals, lost sleep) is widely described, but we found no study of it.

Practical tip: use it. - When hyperfocus arrives on the right task, protect it: silence notifications and clear the next hour if you can.

Practical tip: protect against the crash. - Set an alarm to stop for water and food. - Set a hard stop before bedtime. - Don't plan important meetings for the morning after a long session.

5. Delay discounting: shrink the distance to rewards

Across 21 studies (N=3,913), people with ADHD discounted delayed rewards more steeply than controls (d=0.43, a medium effect) Jackson 2016 [E]. One influential model proposes two routes to ADHD: problems with executive control, and an aversion to waiting for rewards Sonuga-Barke 2003 [S]. A PhD is built on rewards that are years away, which may be a poor fit.

Practical tip: bring rewards closer. - Turn every big goal into a list of small, finishable outputs. Tick them off where you can see them. - Make progress social: share a weekly "what I did" note with a peer or your supervisor. - Pair dull tasks with something pleasant (a particular café, a playlist) that you only use for that task. - Set your own interim deadlines with someone who will notice if you miss them.

6. Sleep timing and scheduling deep work

In adults with ADHD and trouble falling asleep, melatonin (the body's night-time signal) started later in the evening than in controls Van Veen 2010 [S]. A systematic review found consistent evidence for later chronotype ("night owl" body clocks) in ADHD Coogan 2017 [S]. Adults with ADHD reported worse sleep on 7 of 9 subjective measures Díaz-Román 2018 [S].

Shifting the clock is possible but not simple. In a small trial in adults with ADHD and a delayed body clock, low-dose melatonin (0.5 mg) moved the clock earlier by 1 h 28 min and reduced self-reported ADHD symptoms by 14%; the effects disappeared 2 weeks after stopping van Andel 2021 [P]. In the same trial, actual sleep times didn't move, and the authors concluded behavioural coaching is needed van Andel 2022 [P]. Doses here describe the study. They are not advice. Talk to a clinician before using melatonin.

Practical tip: work with your clock. - Find your best two or three hours. Track your focus for a week if you're not sure. - Put deep work (writing, analysis) in that window. Put email, admin and meetings outside it. - Ask your supervisor if regular meetings can be later in the day (see G17).

7. What has trial evidence

A large analysis of 113 trials in 14,887 adults (45.6% female) found that only stimulants and atomoxetine reduced core ADHD symptoms at 12 weeks on both self-rated and clinician-rated scales Ostinelli 2025 [E]. CBT, mindfulness, psychoeducation, cognitive remediation and brain stimulation (tDCS) helped only on clinician ratings, and evidence beyond 12 weeks is lacking Ostinelli 2025 [E]. Medication may help emotional dysregulation less than it helps core symptoms Lenzi 2018 [S].

For the PhD environment itself, a review of doctoral researchers' mental health found the best-established protective factors were social support, seeing the PhD as a process, a positive relationship with the supervisor, and self-care Hazell 2020 [S]. That review did not look at ADHD.

What you can do

  1. Stop trying to out-plan ADHD. Change the setup instead: visible time, short outputs, outside structure. (Practical tip)
  2. Learn your own time correction factor by logging estimates against reality. (Practical tip)
  3. Guard your best hours for deep work. (Practical tip)
  4. Build in people: a peer, a writing group, a supervisor check-in. (Practical tip)
  5. If your difficulties are long-standing, consider an assessment and discuss treatment options. Medication has the strongest trial evidence for core symptoms. (see section 7)
  6. Ask about adjustments (see G14). (Practical tip)

Talk to a clinician about…

  • An ADHD assessment, if you don't have a diagnosis.
  • Treatment options, including medication, and what trials show for adults.
  • A late body clock or trouble falling asleep, and whether melatonin or light timing could help.
  • Whether a stimulant affects your sleep.
  • Low mood, anxiety or exhaustion that doesn't lift.

What we don't know yet

  • Whether any of the practical tips work. We found no trials of time-visibility tools, body doubling, reward pairing or task-splitting in adults with ADHD.
  • The hyperfocus "crash". It is often described but has not been studied.
  • Whether lab timing problems cause real-world lateness. This is inferred.
  • Coaching for adults with ADHD. Our library has no trial of ADHD coaching.
  • PhD students with ADHD. The PhD mental-health studies cited did not report neurodivergence.
  • Long-term treatment effects. Evidence beyond 12 weeks is lacking.

Popular claims checked

  • hyperfocus-is-a-superpower: "Hyperfocus is an ADHD superpower." Verdict: overstated (evidence grade: preliminary).
  • interest-based-nervous-system: "ADHD is an 'interest-based nervous system'." Verdict: overstated (popular but unsupported).
  • time-blindness-explains-lateness: "'Time blindness' explains why you're always late." Verdict: partly true (supported).
  • melatonin-treats-adhd: "Melatonin treats ADHD." Verdict: overstated (supported).
  • adhd-takes-twice-the-cognitive-energy: "With ADHD it takes twice the cognitive energy to produce the same output." Verdict: overstated (preliminary).
  • high-iq-masks-adhd-on-tests: "A high IQ masks ADHD, so it doesn't show up on testing." Verdict: partly true (preliminary).
