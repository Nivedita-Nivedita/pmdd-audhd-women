Living with PMDD: relationships and getting taken seriously

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Women with PMDD describe it as taking over their lives, feeling like "two different people", and strain on their relationships. Preliminary (P): these are interview studies, but they agree with each other.

  • Many report years of being dismissed or misdiagnosed. In one Australian survey, people saw 5.1 clinicians on average before getting a PMDD diagnosis. Preliminary (P), self-selected samples.

  • Two months of daily ratings, a clinician who knows PMDD, and therapy that includes your partner are the practical levers with some evidence behind them. PMDD is linked to a higher risk of suicidal thoughts. If you have them, get help now rather than waiting for your period; a safety plan helps you reach help sooner.

Evidence grade: headline P (Preliminary). The suicide-risk findings are Supported (S). · Talk to a clinician about: your daily ratings, treatment options (G39), and any suicidal thoughts that come with your cycle.

Not medical advice. Crisis resources: see the box below.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait for your period to start or for the feelings to ease. See help.

This guide is about living with PMDD: how it feels, how to get care, and how to bring the people close to you along. For treatments, see G39. For tracking, see G04. For how PMDD overlaps with ADHD, see G05.

A note on the evidence. Most studies here are interviews or online surveys of people who chose to take part. That kind of research is good at describing experiences. It can't tell us how common those experiences are across everyone with PMDD.

1. What people with PMDD report

Life-controlling symptoms. A review that pooled 17 interview studies (479 women) found women described premenstrual disorders as intense, life-changing and life-controlling (Brown 2024) [P]. Women carried responsibility for understanding and managing their condition, and for advocating for themselves in healthcare (Brown 2024) [P].

"Two selves." A review of 19 qualitative studies identified a "dual identity" as a theme, alongside impact on relationships, lack of understanding or support, and difficulty reaching a diagnosis (Haidarieh-Zadeh 2026) [P]. Women reported a lack of understanding from family, friends and medical practitioners (Haidarieh-Zadeh 2026) [P].

"A broken woman" and "the lost decades". In interviews with 17 UK women with specialist-diagnosed PMDD, the themes were a broken woman, misdiagnosis and the lost decades, a life transformed, and negotiating the aftermath (Osborn 2020) [P].

If this sounds like you, you are not imagining it, and you are not alone. That isn't a research finding, but the studies above do show these experiences are widely shared.

2. The diagnostic odyssey

Many clinicians, many labels. In an Australian survey of 267 people with PMDD, participants had seen an average of 5.1 healthcare providers seeking a diagnosis, and judged 51% of them to have poor PMDD awareness (Border 2026) [P]. 54% reported medical gaslighting and 56% misdiagnosis (Border 2026) [P]. Only 19% had been diagnosed using methods consistent with DSM recommendations (Border 2026) [P]. "Medical gaslighting" here means being made to doubt your own account of your symptoms.

The same story in nine countries. Interviews with 27 women from nine countries produced four themes: medical gaslighting and the importance of diagnosis, the diagnostic odyssey, access to healthcare, and self-advocacy (Mosalisa 2026) [P]. Negative experiences delayed diagnosis and fostered mistrust, and they were similar across countries (Mosalisa 2026) [P].

Why it happens. A scoping review grouped the barriers as individual (stigma, medical mistrust, shame, side effects), medical (limited provider knowledge, not following guidelines, negative interactions), cultural and structural (fragmented care, limited access) (Nayak 2025) [P]. Clinical guidance also names poor provider knowledge and cost as barriers (Wagner-Schuman 2023) [S].

These are self-selected samples, so they show that dismissal is a common experience, not how often it happens overall. Not every disagreement is dismissal, either: a clinician may reasonably ask for daily ratings, or consider PME or another diagnosis. If you feel unheard, it's reasonable to ask what they would need to see, to ask for their reasoning to be recorded, and to seek a second opinion or a referral (see section 4).

Diagnosis can help. Participants in the Australian study described diagnosis as bringing better coping and self-compassion (Border 2026) [P]. UK women described it as transforming their lives (Osborn 2020) [P].

3. Which clinicians tend to know PMDD, and what good care looks like

Different strengths. In a global survey of 2,512 patients, psychotherapists were rated highest on interpersonal factors, gynaecologists and psychiatrists highest on PMDD awareness and knowledge, and gynaecologists were the most likely to ask patients to track symptoms daily (Hantsoo 2022) [P]. The authors called for more PMDD training (Hantsoo 2022) [P]. Individual clinicians vary, so treat these as tendencies, not rules.

What good care looks like. The International Society for Premenstrual Disorders (ISPMD) set auditable standards: diagnosis should rest on an accurate history and two months of symptom diaries showing symptoms relieved by menstruation (Ismaili 2016) [S]. For treatment, the top standards were SSRIs as first line, an evidence-based approach, and proper explanation of SSRI side effects (Ismaili 2016) [S].

Practical tip: you can use these standards as a checklist. Good care asks for daily ratings before diagnosing, explains the treatment options and their side effects, and asks about safety.

For clinicians reading this. The gap between these standards and reported practice is large: only 19% of Australian respondents had a DSM-consistent diagnostic process (Border 2026) [P]. The C-PASS scores DSM-5 PMDD from two or more months of DRSP ratings and, in 200 women, agreed with expert clinical diagnosis with an estimated 98% correct classification (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S].

4. Getting taken seriously: bring your daily ratings

Why daily ratings carry weight. The DRSP daily diary gives reliable and valid ratings of PMDD symptoms and impairment (Endicott 2005) [E]. Remembering symptoms afterwards poorly predicted whether daily ratings would confirm PMDD (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S]. A chart turns "it's bad before my period" into something a clinician can score. You don't need a finished chart to book an appointment: a clinician can assess you and discuss care while you keep tracking. G04 explains how to track, including ADHD lines.

Appointment checklist (Practical tip, not evidence)

Before the appointment:

  • [ ] Your daily ratings, ideally two or more full cycles, with period start dates marked. Bring whatever you have; don't put off an appointment to finish the chart.
  • [ ] A one-page summary on top: your worst days, how long they last, and when they clear.
  • [ ] A list of everything you take: medicines and doses, ADHD medication, contraception, supplements, alcohol or cannabis.
  • [ ] What you've already tried for this, and what happened.
  • [ ] Two or three examples of impact: missed work, conflict at home, things you avoid.
  • [ ] Any suicidal thoughts, and when in the cycle they happen.
  • [ ] Your main question, written down. For example: "Could this be PMDD, PME of my ADHD or mood, or both?"
  • [ ] If it helps, someone to come with you.

During the appointment:

  • [ ] Lead with the chart and your main question.
  • [ ] Ask: "What would you need to see to diagnose or rule out PMDD?"
  • [ ] Ask about the options in G39 and their side effects.
  • [ ] If you feel dismissed, ask for the reason to be written in your notes, or for a referral to a gynaecologist or psychiatrist.
  • [ ] Agree a plan and a date to review it.

After the appointment:

  • [ ] Keep tracking, especially if you start a treatment.
  • [ ] Write down what was agreed while you still remember it.

Practical tip: if you have ADHD, put the appointment, the checklist and the chart in one place the day before. Book the appointment for your better week if you can, so you're not trying to advocate on your hardest days.

5. Partners and family

What the research shows. In a randomised trial of 83 women, both one-to-one and couple CBT reduced premenstrual symptoms, emotional reactivity and distress compared with a waiting list (Ussher 2017) [P]. Couple CBT gave more active coping, and partner understanding and relationship improvement were reported most in the couple group (Ussher 2017) [P]. It was one small trial against a waiting list. Treatment may help relationships too: in the 2023 Cochrane review of drospirenone-containing pills, women's ratings of how much symptoms interfered with social activities and relationships may improve, from low-quality evidence (Ma 2023) [S]. These pills aren't suitable for everyone; see G39 for safety checks. Internet CBT reduced how much PMDD interfered with everyday life in 174 women (Weise 2019) [P].

Explaining luteal changes (Practical tip, not evidence)

You decide what to share, with whom and when. These are ideas to agree together, not rules for either of you.

  • Pick a good week to talk. Explain PMDD when you're feeling well, not in the middle of a hard day.
  • Show the chart. A partner can see the pattern for themselves: the same days, every month.
  • Name the "two selves" without blaming either one. Something like "In my luteal week, my brain reacts more strongly to normal hormone changes. It's real and it's treatable. If something I say or do on those days hurts you, I still want us to talk about it."
  • Agree a plan in advance, together. What helps (space, practical help, fewer plans) and what doesn't. A shared calendar of predicted hard days can help if you both want one. Either of you can change the plan.
  • Hold off on big decisions or arguments on predicted hard days if you both agree to. It's an option, not a rule, and it shouldn't be used to dismiss what you say on those days.
  • Partners need support too. Couple CBT is one option to ask about.
  • PMDD explains symptoms, not harm. PMDD can make irritability and conflict worse, but it doesn't make threatening, controlling or violent behaviour by anyone acceptable, and it isn't a reason for someone to dismiss your views or control your choices. If you feel unsafe in a relationship, a cycle plan isn't the answer: see G35 and help.

For emotion regulation and relationships in ADHD more generally, see G08.

6. Safety: suicidal thoughts, asking directly and crisis planning

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait for your period to start or for the feelings to ease. See help.

If you are having thoughts of suicide now, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line now, or tell someone you trust and ask them to help you get support. Don't wait for your period to start or for the thoughts to ease (help).

Suicidal thoughts are common in PMDD. In a global online sample of 599 people with PMDD diagnosed using two months of daily ratings, lifetime rates were 72% for active suicidal thoughts, 49% for planning, 34% for an attempt and 51% for non-suicidal self-injury (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022) [P]. That sample was recruited online, so the true rates across everyone with PMDD may differ. A meta-analysis of 10 studies found women with PMDD had higher odds of suicide attempt (OR 6.97) and suicidal thoughts (OR 3.95) (Prasad 2021) [S]. In Swedish registers, women with diagnosed premenstrual disorders had a higher rate of suicide (HR 1.92), and higher still if diagnosed before age 25 (HR 3.84) (Opatowski 2024) [S].

Asking directly (Practical tip). Clinicians, partners and friends can ask plainly: "Are you having thoughts of suicide?" If you are the one struggling, you can say it plainly too: "On days 24 to 28 I get thoughts of ending my life." Clear words get a clear response.

A cycle-aware crisis plan (Practical tip). Write it while you're well, and share it with someone you trust. Its job is to help you get help sooner, not to wait things out:

  • Your local emergency number and a local crisis line, at the top (help).
  • People you will contact, and how.
  • Early warning signs that you're heading into a bad patch.
  • The days your chart says are hardest, so you and others can check in more then.
  • How to make your surroundings safer on those days.
  • Things that help you get through the next hour while you reach out.

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. Get help now if you're in crisis (help). Don't wait for your period or for a chart.
  2. Track daily for two cycles (G04), and book an appointment without waiting to finish.
  3. Use the appointment checklist in section 4.
  4. Look for a clinician who knows PMDD. Gynaecologists and psychiatrists were rated highest on PMDD knowledge in one large survey.
  5. Read the treatment ladder (G39) so you know what to ask about.
  6. Bring your partner in if and when you choose: share the chart, agree a plan together, and ask about couple CBT.
  7. Make a crisis plan while you're well, and tell one person where it is.
  8. Find your people. Peer groups can offer support. They are not a substitute for care.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Whether your daily ratings confirm PMDD, PME, or both.
  • Treatment options and side effects (G39).
  • A referral to a gynaecologist or psychiatrist if you feel stuck.
  • CBT, including online or couple formats.
  • Suicidal thoughts, especially if they come with your cycle, and a safety plan.
  • How PMDD and any ADHD or autism interact for you.

What we don't know yet

  • How common dismissal is overall. All the care-experience studies used self-selected samples, which may over-represent people with bad experiences.
  • What improves care. We did not find a study in our search (last updated October 2026) testing an intervention, such as clinician training, to reduce the diagnostic odyssey.
  • Partners' own experiences. Beyond one small couple-CBT trial, we did not find a study of partners or families of people with PMDD in our search.
  • Couple CBT against an active treatment. The only trial used a waiting list.
  • Women with ADHD or autism. We did not find a study of PMDD care experiences or relationships in these groups.
  • Whether asking about suicide, or crisis planning, changes outcomes in PMDD specifically. We did not find a PMDD-specific study in our search.
