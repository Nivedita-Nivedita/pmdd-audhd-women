PMDD treatments: what the evidence supports

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Strongest evidence: SSRIs (a type of antidepressant). Many randomised trials in PMS and PMDD, a 2024 Cochrane review and guideline support. Established (E). Both daily and second-half-of-cycle (luteal-only) schedules beat placebo; daily dosing showed a larger benefit on average, from a comparison between groups of trials. Supported (S). Which schedule suits you is decided with a prescriber.

  • Moderate evidence: one kind of combined pill (drospirenone 24/4), mostly in diagnosed PMDD, and medicines that switch off the ovaries (GnRH agonists), studied in PMS. Both come with more trade-offs and safety checks. Supported (S). A 2026 network meta-analysis found both SSRIs and combined pills effective, but only 5 trials entered its comparison, so it does not settle which works better.

  • Limited or early evidence: talking therapy (mostly PMS trials, one online-CBT trial in diagnosed PMDD), ovary removal (small uncontrolled series), and calcium, vitamin B6 and vitex. These supplements have some evidence for premenstrual symptoms (PMS), but evidence specifically in prospectively confirmed PMDD is insufficient to establish benefit. Preliminary (P) for PMS; Unknown (U) for confirmed PMDD.

  • No eligible trial found: cannabis and other substances, and any PMDD treatment in women with ADHD or autism, in our search (last updated October 2026).

Evidence grade: SSRIs E (schedule comparison S); drospirenone pill and GnRH agonists S; CBT and surgery P; calcium, B6 and vitex P in PMS, U in confirmed PMDD. Headline grade P (Preliminary), the weakest essential claim. · Talk to a clinician about: which options fit your symptoms, your daily ratings, and how any option fits with your other medicines.

Not medical advice. Nothing here is a recommendation to start, stop or change a medicine. Crisis resources: see the box below.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait for your period to start or for the feelings to ease. See help.

This guide groups PMDD treatments by how much research supports them, not by how well they will work for you. Use it to prepare for a conversation with a clinician.

How we grouped the evidence. Each treatment sits in a tier (strongest, moderate, limited or early, or no eligible trial found) according to the amount and quality of research in diagnosed PMDD, or in PMS where we say so. We anchored the tiers to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists' 2023 guideline ACOG Clinical Practice Guideline No. 7, 2023 [Guideline] and the 2024 Cochrane review of SSRIs (Jespersen 2024) [E]. A tier is a summary of the research, not a personal recommendation, and options within a tier are not ranked against each other.

Evidence tier is not the same as treatment order. A strong tier means the research is solid. It does not mean everyone should start there, or must try each option in turn. Clinical guidance does suggest a usual order: international standards name SSRIs as first line (Ismaili 2016) [S], and one clinical review places the pill second and GnRH analogues with add-back after that (Wagner-Schuman 2023) [S]. The ACOG 2023 guideline covers medicines (hormonal and non-hormonal), psychological counselling, complementary treatments, exercise and nutrition, self-help and surgery, and notes that many patients may benefit from combining several approaches ACOG Clinical Practice Guideline No. 7, 2023 [Guideline]. In practice, the right first step also depends on your symptoms, whether you want contraception, your other health conditions and medicines, what you have tried, and what you prefer. For what PMDD is and how it overlaps with ADHD, see G05. For relationships and getting taken seriously, see G41.

Who was studied. Many treatment studies enrolled women with PMS, or with premenstrual disorders (PMDs) in general, rather than PMDD confirmed by daily ratings. Throughout this guide we say which group each study included, because a result in PMS may not apply to PMDD.

A quick glossary (definitions, not evidence claims):

  • Luteal phase: the second half of the cycle, from ovulation to your period.
  • SSRI: selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor, a common class of antidepressant.
  • COC: combined oral contraceptive, the pill with both an oestrogen and a progestogen. Drospirenone is one kind of progestogen. 24/4 means 24 hormone pills, then 4 inactive pills.
  • GnRH agonist: a medicine, usually an injection, that switches off the ovarian cycle and causes a temporary, reversible menopause. Add-back means giving low-dose hormones alongside it to reduce menopause-like side effects.
  • Oophorectomy: surgical removal of the ovaries. Hysterectomy: removal of the womb.
  • Placebo: a dummy treatment. In PMDD trials, many people improve on placebo, so a treatment has to beat that.
  • SMD / MD: standardised mean difference / mean difference, ways of measuring how much better a treatment did than its comparison. For SMD, a rough rule of thumb is 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.
  • OR (odds ratio): compares the odds of an outcome in two groups. It is not the same as a risk ratio, and the two shouldn't be merged into one "times more likely" figure.
  • NNT / NNH: number needed to treat (or harm): how many people need the treatment for one extra person to benefit (or have a side effect) compared with placebo.
  • Network meta-analysis: a review that compares treatments indirectly, through trials that each tested one of them against placebo or another treatment.
  • PMS, PMDs and PMDD: PMS is the broader, milder category. Premenstrual disorders (PMDs) is an umbrella term that includes PMS and PMDD. PMDD is the severe, mood-centred form, confirmed by daily ratings. Many supplement trials enrolled women with PMS, so their results may not apply to PMDD.

1. Start with a diagnosis: daily ratings

Every option in this guide assumes the diagnosis is right. Daily ratings, kept as you go (prospectively) across at least two symptomatic cycles, establish the cyclical pattern and support confirming PMDD. The DRSP chart and the C-PASS scoring system are standard tools for this, not the only accepted route. A clinician can assess you, make a provisional diagnosis and start care before two cycles are complete. If you are in crisis, get help now: urgent help never waits for a chart. International expert standards say diagnosis should rest on an accurate history and two months of symptom diaries showing premenstrual symptoms that are relieved by menstruation (Ismaili 2016) [S]. An expert consensus also says management rests on careful diagnosis (Nevatte 2013) [S]. The DRSP daily diary gives reliable, valid ratings of PMDD symptoms and can show change with treatment (Endicott 2005) [E]. Remembering symptoms afterwards is a poor guide to whether daily ratings will confirm PMDD (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S].

This matters for treatment. If your symptoms are there all month and only get worse before your period, that is premenstrual exacerbation (PME), not PMDD, and the evidence below may not apply. G04 explains how to track, and G05 §5 explains PMDD vs PME.

Practical tip: keep your daily chart going once you start a treatment. It is the easiest way for you and your clinician to see whether something is working.

2. Strongest evidence: SSRIs

How well they work. The 2024 Cochrane review of 34 randomised trials, which combined women with PMS and PMDD, found that SSRIs probably reduce overall self-rated premenstrual symptoms (SMD −0.57; moderate-certainty evidence) (Jespersen 2024) [E]. The authors ask readers to interpret this with caution: study methods were poorly reported, publication bias was suspected for one outcome, and 68% of the included studies were funded by drug companies (Jespersen 2024) [E]. An earlier, 2013 version of the review also found SSRIs beat placebo (Marjoribanks 2013) [E]; we keep it here only as history, because the 2024 update replaces it. International standards name SSRIs as the first-line treatment (Ismaili 2016) [S]. They are not only for mood: in a randomised trial of 320 women with confirmed PMDD, daily fluoxetine beat placebo for breast tenderness, bloating and headache (Steiner 2001) [S].

Daily or luteal-only. Both schedules have evidence. In the 2024 Cochrane review (PMS and PMDD), SSRIs beat placebo when taken continuously (SMD −0.69) and when taken in the luteal phase only (SMD −0.39), both with moderate-certainty evidence (Jespersen 2024) [S]. The review concluded that SSRIs are probably more effective when taken continuously (Jespersen 2024) [S]. That conclusion comes from comparing two subgroups of placebo-controlled trials (P = 0.03 for the difference). It describes an average across trials, not what will suit you, and it is weaker than trials that randomise people to one schedule or the other. The 2013 version had found no clear difference between schedules (Marjoribanks 2013) [S]; the updated analysis supersedes it. In a three-arm randomised trial in women with severe PMS or PMDD that did compare schedules directly, sertraline taken only premenstrually did not differ from sertraline taken every day, and both beat placebo (Freeman 2004) [S]. Women who also had symptoms after their period ended stayed more symptomatic on either schedule (Freeman 2004) [S].

Symptom-onset dosing (limited evidence). This is the shortest schedule: start when symptoms begin and stop in the first days of the period. In a randomised trial of 252 women with PMDD, it did not beat placebo on the main outcome (P = .06) (Yonkers 2015) [P]. It did beat placebo on depression, total DRSP and anger/irritability scores, with response in 67.0% vs 52.4% (Yonkers 2015) [P]. The authors concluded that women "may or may not benefit", depending on the scale (Yonkers 2015) [P].

How fast. In the three-arm trial, improvement came swiftly, in the first month (Freeman 2004) [S]. In a randomised trial of 281 women with PMDD, luteal-only sertraline beat placebo by the first cycle (responders 50% vs 26%) (Halbreich 2002) [S]. One honest caveat: placebo improved gradually and reached a similar level by the third month (Freeman 2004) [S].

Side effects and stopping. The 2024 Cochrane review found SSRIs probably increase side effects. The most common were nausea (odds ratio 3.30), weakness or low energy (odds ratio 3.28) and sleepiness or poorer concentration (odds ratio 3.26, low-certainty evidence); others included insomnia, sexual problems or lower libido, dizziness, sweating, dry mouth, tremor, diarrhoea and constipation (Jespersen 2024) [E]. NHS information lists similar common antidepressant side effects, says sexual problems can sometimes carry on after stopping, and says that, uncommonly, some people have suicidal thoughts or an urge to self-harm when starting an antidepressant: speak to a doctor straight away if that happens NHS: antidepressants [Reference]. It also advises talking to your doctor before stopping, so you can agree a plan NHS: antidepressants [Reference]. In the luteal-only trial, about 8% on sertraline and less than 1% on placebo stopped because of side effects (Halbreich 2002) [S]. With symptom-onset dosing, stopping abruptly each month caused no discontinuation symptoms in that trial (Yonkers 2015) [P]. Expert standards include proper explanation of SSRI side effects as a mark of good care (Ismaili 2016) [S].

Practical tip: the evidence on schedules is a reason to ask about options, not to change your own schedule. Scheduling is decided with your prescriber. Questions worth asking include "Given my chart, would daily or luteal-only dosing suit me better?" and "What should I expect in the first cycle?"

3. Moderate evidence: combined pills, especially drospirenone 24/4

The best-studied pill. The 2023 Cochrane review (5 trials, 858 women analysed, most diagnosed with PMDD) found that combined pills containing drospirenone and ethinylestradiol may improve overall premenstrual symptoms compared with placebo (SMD −0.41; low-quality evidence) (Ma 2023) [S]. They may also improve productivity, social activities and relationships, and the authors describe the effects as possibly small to moderate (Ma 2023) [S]. An earlier, 2012 version of the review reached a similar conclusion (Lopez 2012) [S]; we keep it only as history, because the 2023 update replaces it. In the larger of the PMDD placebo-controlled trials (450 women with PMDD), response was 48% vs 36% on placebo, a number needed to treat of 8 (Yonkers 2005) [S]. A smaller crossover trial of 64 women with PMDD also found benefit (61.7% vs 31.8% improved on a clinician rating) (Pearlstein 2005) [S].

The caveats. The placebo also had a significant effect, and it is not known whether the pill works after three cycles, helps less severe symptoms or beats pills with a different progestogen (Ma 2023) [S]. The evidence was very low to moderate quality (Ma 2023) [S]. The pill may lead to more side effects overall (odds ratio 2.31) and more people stopping because of side effects (odds ratio 3.41); it probably causes more breast pain, and may cause more nausea, bleeding between periods and period problems (Ma 2023) [S].

Safety checks before a combined pill. Combined pills are not safe for everyone. NHS information says you may not be able to take one if you are 35 or over and smoke, or are living with obesity. It may not be suitable if you have ever had a blood clot (or a close family member had one under 45), a stroke or narrowed arteries, heart disease or high blood pressure, migraine with aura, breast cancer, or certain liver, gallbladder or diabetes complications NHS: who can take the combined pill [Reference]. The risk of a blood clot in a leg or lung is very small, affecting up to 1 in 1,000 people using combined hormonal contraception, and a doctor, nurse or pharmacist checks your risk factors before prescribing NHS: side effects and risks of the combined pill [Reference]. The NHS also lists mood changes, headaches, nausea and sore breasts among possible side effects NHS: side effects and risks of the combined pill [Reference]. None of the trials in the Cochrane review reported a rare serious harm such as a blood clot (Ma 2023) [S]; small, short trials cannot rule such harms out, which is why we take this safety information from official sources.

Overall symptoms more than mood. In a meta-analysis of 9 randomised trials in PMS and PMDD, combined pills beat placebo for overall premenstrual symptoms (SMD 0.41) but not for premenstrual depressive symptoms specifically (SMD 0.22), and no pill beat any other (de Wit 2021) [S].

Continuous (no-break) regimens (limited evidence). In a randomised trial in women with PMDD, a continuous levonorgestrel/ethinyl estradiol pill beat placebo on some measures but not others (52% vs 40% responded) (Halbreich 2012) [P]. Across four studies of this pill in PMDD and PMS, results were inconsistent and the placebo effect was large (Freeman 2012) [P].

SSRI or pill? What the 2026 network meta-analysis adds. We did not find a trial comparing them head to head in our search (last updated October 2026). A 2026 systematic review with network meta-analysis compared them indirectly in PMDD: it included 16 studies, but only 5 trials entered the network (Berni 2026) [P]. It concluded that both SSRIs and combined pills are effective, naming continuous paroxetine and the drospirenone 24/4 pill as the most effective options within their own classes, and the authors call for direct head-to-head trials (Berni 2026) [P]. The abstract gives no effect sizes, and a network built on 5 trials cannot establish a reliable ordering of individual medicines, so we have not changed the tiers on the strength of it. Clinical guidance places SSRIs first and the pill second (Wagner-Schuman 2023) [S].

The counterweight: the pill and mood. In a national registry cohort of 1,061,997 Danish women aged 15–34, combined pill users were more likely than non-users to start an antidepressant (RR 1.23), and the risk was higher in teenagers (RR 1.8) (Skovlund 2016) [S]. This is an association, not proof of cause. In a Swedish registry cohort, young women with ADHD using the combined pill had a higher depression risk than pill users without ADHD (aHR 6.10) (Lundin 2023) [S]. Neither study was about PMDD treatment. G23 covers contraception and mood in more detail.

Practical tip: if you try a pill, keep tracking daily, and tell your prescriber about migraines with aura, smoking, clots in your family and your blood pressure. Your chart can show whether mood is getting better or worse, which a monthly impression can miss.

4. Limited or early evidence: psychological therapy

CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy) in PMS. A meta-analysis of 9 randomised trials in PMS found low-quality evidence that CBT reduces anxiety (ES −0.58; NNT 5) and depression (ES −0.55; NNT 5) (Busse 2009) [P]. Monitoring alone had limited support, and education appeared ineffective (Busse 2009) [P]. A newer meta-analysis of 20 randomised trials found psychological interventions reduced PMS severity (SMD −1.57 after removing an outlier) and depressive (SMD −1.08), anxiety (SMD −1.03) and anger/irritability symptoms (SMD −0.85) (Liu 2026) [P]. Most of those trials were in PMS rather than confirmed PMDD (Liu 2026) [P].

Internet CBT in diagnosed PMDD. In a randomised trial of 174 women with PMDD, eight weeks of therapist-guided online CBT reduced interference with everyday life, symptom intensity and disability compared with a waiting list, and the effects held at 6 months (Weise 2019) [P]. A waiting list is a weaker comparison than a placebo or another treatment.

Couple CBT in premenstrual disorders. In a randomised trial of 83 women with premenstrual disorders (PMDs), both one-to-one and couple CBT reduced premenstrual symptoms and distress compared with a waiting list, and couple CBT gave more active coping (Ussher 2017) [P]. G41 covers partners in more detail.

The evidence here is consistent in direction but small, mostly of low quality and mostly in PMS. CBT can sit alongside a medicine; it is not only for people who can't take one.

5. Moderate evidence: GnRH agonists, with limited evidence on add-back

They work, but not long term on their own. A 2025 Cochrane review of randomised trials in PMS found that GnRH agonists without add-back improved global premenstrual symptoms compared with placebo (SMD −1.23; high certainty) (Naheed 2025) [S]. More people stopped because of side effects (RR 4.24) (Naheed 2025) [S]. The authors say menopausal side effects rule out long-term use without add-back, and that these medicines can give a short-term break (Naheed 2025) [S]. We place them in the moderate tier, not the strongest, because the trials are few and short and the harms limit how they can be used.

Add-back is less certain. The same review found only low-certainty evidence on add-back (1 trial, 31 women), and oestrogen plus cyclical progestogen add-back may worsen symptoms (SMD 0.90) (Naheed 2025) [P]. An older meta-analysis of trials in PMS found add-back did not reduce the benefit (SMD 0.12) (Wyatt 2004) [S]. The two reviews used different trial sets, and the newer one is more cautious. Long-term add-back data are lacking (Naheed 2025) [P].

Where it fits. Clinical guidance suggests GnRH analogues with stable hormone add-back when SSRIs and the pill have not helped in PMDD, and names poor provider knowledge and cost as barriers (Wagner-Schuman 2023) [S].

Harms. Patient drug information for leuprolide, one GnRH agonist, lists hot flashes and sweating, and new or worsening depression, among its side effects, and says it may decrease bone density, which can increase the chance of broken bones MedlinePlus: leuprolide injection [Reference]. That leaflet covers leuprolide's licensed uses, such as endometriosis, not PMDD specifically. These menopause-like effects and bone loss are why add-back and specialist monitoring are discussed.

6. Limited evidence: removing the ovaries (with or without the womb)

The evidence is small and uncontrolled. In a case series of 14 women with severe, treatment-resistant PMS confirmed by daily charting, hysterectomy with oophorectomy and continuous oestrogen ended the cyclical pattern at 6 months (Casper 1990) [P]. The authors said surgery is indicated only for a small, selected group (Casper 1990) [P]. In a look-back interview study of 47 women with severe PMS who had the same surgery, 96% were satisfied or very satisfied and 93.6% reported complete resolution of cyclical symptoms (Cronje 2004) [P]. 93.6% continued HRT, and there was no comparison group (Cronje 2004) [P]. Experts list oophorectomy among the options that act by suppressing ovulation (Nevatte 2013) [S].

Hysterectomy alone is not a PMDD treatment. In a secondary analysis of a randomised trial in women treated for heavy periods (not diagnosed with PMS or PMDD), premenstrual symptoms fell about equally after hysterectomy with the ovaries kept or a hormonal coil (Leminen 2012) [P].

Harms. If your ovaries are removed, menopause starts immediately after the operation, whatever your age (surgical menopause), and HRT is usually offered for menopausal symptoms NHS: hysterectomy considerations [Reference]. The ovaries' hormones help protect against weak bones (osteoporosis) NHS: hysterectomy considerations [Reference].

Practical tip: surgery can't be undone and brings on menopause. Questions worth asking include whether a trial of a GnRH agonist could show first how you respond to having no cycle, and what hormone replacement afterwards would involve. G30 covers menopause and HRT.

7. Limited evidence: supplements and lifestyle, studied in PMS rather than PMDD

Every study in this section was done in PMS, in women with milder premenstrual symptoms, or in mixed PMS and PMDD groups. We did not find one that used PMDD confirmed by daily ratings in our search (last updated October 2026). Some of these options have evidence for premenstrual symptoms (PMS), but evidence specifically in prospectively confirmed PMDD is insufficient to establish benefit [U].

  • Calcium. In a multicentre randomised trial of 466 analysed women with moderate-to-severe PMS, daily calcium cut luteal symptom scores by 48% vs 30% on placebo by the third cycle (Thys-Jacobs 1998) [P].
  • Vitamin B6. A systematic review of 9 trials (940 patients with PMS) found B6 improved overall premenstrual symptoms (OR 2.32) and premenstrual depression (OR 1.69), but most trials were of low quality (Wyatt 1999) [P]. A systematic review of 31 randomised nutrition trials in PMS found B6, calcium and zinc consistently improved psychological PMS symptoms, though only 1 trial had low risk of bias (Robinson 2024) [P].
  • Calcium and B6 in PMDD. Calcium and vitamin B6 have some evidence for premenstrual symptoms (PMS), but evidence specifically in prospectively confirmed PMDD is insufficient to establish benefit [U].
  • Vitex (chasteberry). A meta-analysis of trials in PMS showed a large pooled effect (Hedges g −1.21), but most trials were at high risk of bias, results varied hugely (I² 91%) and publication bias was likely; the authors say the effect is "at best" an overestimate (Verkaik 2017) [P]. In a meta-analysis of the 3 best-reported trials (520 women with PMS), vitex users were 2.57 times more likely than those on placebo to have PMS remission (Csupor 2019) [P]. A systematic review of 8 trials in PMS or PMDD was positive but found most trials were in PMS, with three different products and varying measures (Cerqueira 2017) [P].
  • Magnesium. In a crossover randomised trial of 44 women with mild premenstrual symptoms, magnesium alone did not help; magnesium plus B6 slightly reduced anxiety-type symptoms, an effect the authors called "small" (De Souza 2000) [P]. The nutrition review in PMS found insufficient evidence for magnesium (Robinson 2024) [P]. A meta-analysis of observational studies found women with PMS do not reliably have lower blood magnesium (Moslehi 2019) [P].
  • Exercise. In a meta-analysis of randomised trials in PMS, exercise for at least 8 weeks reduced symptoms (SMD −1.08), but 87% of trials were at high risk of bias and the authors say it "may be" effective (Pearce 2020) [P].

Experts name CBT, calcium and vitex as warranting further study in premenstrual disorders (Nevatte 2013) [S].

Safety: vitamin B6. A small trial that reports no harms doesn't make a supplement risk-free. NHS advice says that taking large amounts of vitamin B6 as a supplement can cause loss of feeling in the arms and legs (peripheral neuropathy). This usually improves after stopping, but it can occasionally be permanent, particularly after large amounts for more than a few months NHS: vitamin B [Reference].

Practical tip: "natural" doesn't mean free of side effects or interactions. Check any supplement with a pharmacist or clinician, especially if you take other medicines.

8. No eligible trial found: cannabis and other substances

In a cross-sectional survey of 634 people, the 167 respondents classed as having PMDD most often used cannabis to manage premenstrual mood (51.5%), and 16.2% used psychedelics (Nazareth 2026) [P]. Use was higher in the luteal phase (Nazareth 2026) [P]. The survey did not measure whether these helped, and we did not find a trial testing them in PMDD in our search (last updated October 2026) (Nazareth 2026) [U].

Practical tip: if you use cannabis, alcohol or other substances to get through the luteal phase, you can tell your clinician without judgement. It tells them how hard those days are, and it matters for how other medicines might interact.

9. No eligible trial found: treatment in ADHD and autism

We did not find a trial of any PMDD treatment in women with ADHD or autism in our search (last updated October 2026). We also did not find data on whether SSRIs, pills or GnRH agonists interact with ADHD medication in PMDD, or work differently in ADHD. The only premenstrual ADHD-medication evidence is a 9-person case series of stimulant dose changes, covered in G05 §7. The pill-and-mood findings in young women with ADHD (§3) are the nearest data, and they come from depression diagnoses, not PMDD.

This doesn't mean these treatments won't help you. It means your clinician will be using judgement rather than ADHD-specific trials.

10. Evidence tiers at a glance

Grades are for the claim as written in the "Population studied" column. The tier shows the amount and quality of research, not a sequence you must follow, and options within a tier are not ranked against each other; options can be combined. Effect sizes are as reported by each study, and they come from different scales, so don't compare them directly. The last column is a Practical tip, not evidence: it lists situations in which an option may be worth raising with a clinician.

11. Safety: PMDD, premenstrual disorders and suicidal thoughts

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait for your period to start or for the feelings to ease. See help.

Studies of PMDD and other premenstrual disorders report higher rates of suicidal thoughts and suicide attempts than comparison groups. A meta-analysis of 10 observational studies found that women classed as having PMDD had higher odds of suicide attempt (OR 6.97) and suicidal thoughts (OR 3.95) than comparison groups, and women with PMS had higher odds of suicidal thoughts (OR 10.06) (Prasad 2021) [S]. In a Swedish national registry cohort, women with clinically diagnosed premenstrual disorders (PMDs, a broader group than PMDD alone) had a higher rate of death by suicide than matched women without them (HR 1.92) (Opatowski 2024) [S]. A 2026 systematic review of 18 studies, synthesised narratively without a pooled estimate, found suicidality-related outcomes associated with PMDD and/or PMDs; reported prevalences ranged widely (for example 26% to 86% for suicidal thoughts), and 70% of the studies had quality concerns (Zhitnik 2026) [S]. That review found no study testing a treatment for suicidality in PMDD (Zhitnik 2026) [S].

These are group-level associations. They are real and should be taken seriously, but they cannot predict any one person's risk, in either direction. If you have suicidal thoughts, at any point in your cycle, get help now: see help.

Practical tip: if suicidal thoughts come with your cycle, say so plainly when you discuss treatment. It changes how urgent the conversation is. G41 §6 covers safety planning.

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. Start daily ratings for at least two cycles to confirm the pattern (G04). You don't have to wait for the chart to see a clinician, and if you're in crisis, get help now.
  2. Bring this guide. Ask which options fit your symptoms and why.
  3. Keep tracking through any treatment. Compare cycles before and after.
  4. Give each option a fair trial, with your clinician. Ask how many cycles to try before judging it.
  5. List everything you take, including ADHD medication, contraception, supplements, alcohol and cannabis.
  6. Don't change, stop or combine medicines on your own. Changes go through your prescriber.
  7. Plan for the hardest days while you're well.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Whether your daily ratings confirm PMDD, PME or both.
  • SSRI schedules: daily, luteal-only or symptom-onset. Both daily and luteal-only have evidence; daily showed a larger benefit on average in the 2024 review. Decide together which suits your chart.
  • Side effects to expect, and what to do if they happen.
  • Whether a combined pill is safe for you (blood-clot risk, migraine with aura, smoking, age, blood pressure), and if so how to monitor your mood on it.
  • CBT, including online or couple formats.
  • If SSRIs and the pill haven't helped: GnRH agonists, add-back, bone health and referral to a specialist.
  • Surgery, only after specialist review, including what menopause and HRT would mean.
  • How any option fits with your ADHD medication and other medicines.
  • Suicidal thoughts, especially if they come with your cycle.

What we don't know yet

  • Daily vs luteal-only SSRIs head to head. The Cochrane finding favouring daily dosing compares subgroups of placebo-controlled trials (Jespersen 2024) [S]; few trials have randomised people between schedules.
  • SSRI vs pill head to head. We did not find a direct trial in our search (last updated October 2026). The only comparison is indirect, through a network of 5 trials, and its authors call for head-to-head trials (Berni 2026) [P].
  • Long-term use. Whether the drospirenone pill works after three cycles is unknown (Ma 2023) [S], and long-term GnRH add-back data are lacking (Naheed 2025) [P]. We did not find a study of bone safety for GnRH agonists specifically in PMDD in our search (last updated October 2026), although reduced bone density is a known effect of these medicines MedlinePlus: leuprolide injection [Reference].
  • Surgery. The evidence is two small, uncontrolled series in severe PMS. We did not find long-term data on surgical menopause in PMDD in our search.
  • Supplements in confirmed PMDD. We did not find a supplement trial that used PMDD confirmed by daily ratings in our search (last updated October 2026).
  • Treating suicidality in PMDD. A 2026 systematic review found no study testing a treatment aimed at suicidality in PMDD (Zhitnik 2026) [S].
  • Cannabis and other substances. We did not find an efficacy or safety trial in our search.
  • ADHD and autism. We did not find a PMDD treatment trial in either group, or data on interactions with ADHD medication, in our search.
  • Who responds to what. Beyond the finding that symptoms after the period predicted a poorer response (Freeman 2004) [S], we did not find data to match a person to a treatment.
