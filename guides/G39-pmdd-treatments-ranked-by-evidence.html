Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • SSRIs (a type of antidepressant) have the strongest evidence for PMDD. Taking one only in the second half of the cycle worked about as well as taking it every day. Established (E).

  • One kind of combined pill (drospirenone 24/4) and medicines that switch off the ovaries (GnRH agonists) also work, with more trade-offs. Supported (S). Talking therapy, surgery and supplements rest on small studies, uncontrolled studies or studies of PMS rather than PMDD. Preliminary (P).

  • No PMDD treatment has been tested in women with ADHD or autism.

Evidence grade: SSRIs E; drospirenone pill and GnRH agonists S; CBT, surgery and supplements P. Headline grade P (Preliminary), the weakest essential claim. · Talk to a clinician about: which rung of the ladder fits your symptoms, your daily ratings, and how any option fits with your other medicines.

Not medical advice. Nothing here is a recommendation to start, stop or change a medicine. Crisis resources: see the box below.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

This guide ranks PMDD treatments by how strong the research is, not by how well they will work for you. Use it to prepare for a conversation with a clinician. For what PMDD is and how it overlaps with ADHD, see G05. For relationships and getting taken seriously, see G41.

A quick glossary (definitions, not evidence claims):

  • Luteal phase: the second half of the cycle, from ovulation to your period.
  • SSRI: selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor, a common class of antidepressant.
  • COC: combined oral contraceptive, the pill with both an oestrogen and a progestogen. Drospirenone is one kind of progestogen. 24/4 means 24 hormone pills, then 4 inactive pills.
  • GnRH agonist: a medicine, usually an injection, that switches off the ovarian cycle and causes a temporary, reversible menopause. Add-back means giving low-dose hormones alongside it to reduce menopause-like side effects.
  • Oophorectomy: surgical removal of the ovaries. Hysterectomy: removal of the womb.
  • Placebo: a dummy treatment. In PMDD trials, many people improve on placebo, so a treatment has to beat that.
  • SMD / MD: standardised mean difference / mean difference, ways of measuring how much better a treatment did than its comparison. For SMD, a rough rule of thumb is 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.
  • NNT / NNH: number needed to treat (or harm): how many people need the treatment for one extra person to benefit (or have a side effect) compared with placebo.
  • PMS vs PMDD: PMS is the broader, milder category. Many supplement trials enrolled women with PMS, so their results may not apply to PMDD.

1. Start with a diagnosis: two months of daily ratings

Every rung of this ladder assumes the diagnosis is right. International expert standards say diagnosis should rest on an accurate history and two months of symptom diaries showing premenstrual symptoms that are relieved by menstruation (Ismaili 2016) [S]. An expert consensus also says management rests on careful diagnosis (Nevatte 2013) [S]. The DRSP daily diary gives reliable, valid ratings of PMDD symptoms and can show change with treatment (Endicott 2005) [E]. Remembering symptoms afterwards is a poor guide to whether daily ratings will confirm PMDD (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S].

This matters for treatment. If your symptoms are there all month and only get worse before your period, that is premenstrual exacerbation (PME), not PMDD, and the evidence below may not apply. G04 explains how to track, and G05 §5 explains PMDD vs PME.

Practical tip: keep your daily chart going once you start a treatment. It is the easiest way for you and your clinician to see whether something is working.

2. Rung 1: SSRIs

How well they work. A Cochrane review of 31 randomised trials found SSRIs reduced premenstrual symptoms more than placebo (Marjoribanks 2013) [E]. At moderate doses, the effect was SMD −0.65 from end scores (low-quality evidence) and SMD −0.36 from change scores (moderate-quality evidence) (Marjoribanks 2013) [E]. International standards name SSRIs as the first-line treatment (Ismaili 2016) [S]. They are not only for mood: in 320 women with confirmed PMDD, daily fluoxetine beat placebo for breast tenderness, bloating and headache (Steiner 2001) [S].

Daily or luteal-only. The Cochrane review found no clear difference between luteal-only and continuous dosing (Marjoribanks 2013) [E]. In a three-arm randomised trial, sertraline taken only premenstrually did not differ from sertraline taken every day, and both beat placebo (Freeman 2004) [S]. Women who also had symptoms after their period ended stayed more symptomatic on either schedule (Freeman 2004) [S].

Symptom-onset dosing. This is the shortest schedule: start when symptoms begin and stop in the first days of the period. In 252 women with PMDD, it did not beat placebo on the main outcome (P = .06) (Yonkers 2015) [P]. It did beat placebo on depression, total DRSP and anger/irritability scores, with response in 67.0% vs 52.4% (Yonkers 2015) [P]. The authors concluded that women "may or may not benefit", depending on the scale (Yonkers 2015) [P].

How fast. In the three-arm trial, improvement came swiftly, in the first month (Freeman 2004) [S]. In 281 women with PMDD, luteal-only sertraline beat placebo by the first cycle (responders 50% vs 26%) (Halbreich 2002) [S]. One honest caveat: placebo improved gradually and reached a similar level by the third month (Freeman 2004) [S].

Side effects and stopping. Side effects were dose-related, most often nausea (NNH 7) and low energy (NNH 9) (Marjoribanks 2013) [E]. In the luteal-only trial, about 8% on sertraline and less than 1% on placebo stopped because of side effects (Halbreich 2002) [S]. With symptom-onset dosing, stopping abruptly each month caused no discontinuation symptoms (Yonkers 2015) [P]. Expert standards include proper explanation of SSRI side effects as a mark of good care (Ismaili 2016) [S].

Practical tip: the evidence on schedules is a reason to ask about options, not to change your own schedule. Questions worth asking include "Would luteal-only dosing suit my chart?" and "What should I expect in the first cycle?"

3. Rung 2: combined pills, especially drospirenone 24/4

The best-studied pill. A Cochrane review found that, in two placebo-controlled trials in women with PMDD, drospirenone 3 mg with ethinyl estradiol 20 μg reduced premenstrual symptoms after three months (MD −7.92; 95% CI −11.16 to −4.67) (Lopez 2012) [S]. It also improved productivity, social activities and relationships (Lopez 2012) [S]. In the larger of those trials (450 women), response was 48% vs 36% on placebo, a number needed to treat of 8 (Yonkers 2005) [S]. A smaller crossover trial of 64 women also found benefit (61.7% vs 31.8% improved on a clinician rating) (Pearlstein 2005) [S].

The caveats. The placebo also had a large effect, and nobody knows whether the pill works beyond three cycles, helps milder symptoms or beats other pills (Lopez 2012) [S]. Nausea, bleeding between periods and breast pain were more common (ORs 3.15, 4.92, 2.67) (Lopez 2012) [S].

Overall symptoms more than mood. Across 9 trials, combined pills beat placebo for overall premenstrual symptoms (SMD 0.41) but not for premenstrual depressive symptoms specifically (SMD 0.22), and no pill beat any other (de Wit 2021) [S].

Continuous (no-break) regimens. A continuous levonorgestrel/ethinyl estradiol pill beat placebo on some measures but not others (52% vs 40% responded) (Halbreich 2012) [P]. Across four studies of this pill, results were inconsistent and the placebo effect was large (Freeman 2012) [P].

SSRI or pill? No trial has compared them head to head. An indirect network meta-analysis concluded that both are effective for PMDD and called for direct trials (Berni 2026) [P]. Clinical guidance places SSRIs first and the pill second (Wagner-Schuman 2023) [S].

The counterweight: the pill and mood. In 1,061,997 Danish women aged 15–34, combined pill users were more likely than non-users to start an antidepressant (RR 1.23), and the risk was higher in teenagers (RR 1.8) (Skovlund 2016) [S]. This is an association, not proof of cause. In Swedish registers, young women with ADHD using the combined pill had a higher depression risk than pill users without ADHD (aHR 6.10) (Lundin 2023) [S]. Neither study was about PMDD treatment. G23 covers contraception and mood in more detail.

Practical tip: if you try a pill, keep tracking daily. Your chart can show whether mood is getting better or worse, which a monthly impression can miss.

4. Psychological therapy

CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy). A meta-analysis of 9 trials found low-quality evidence that CBT reduces anxiety (ES −0.58; NNT 5) and depression (ES −0.55; NNT 5) in PMS (Busse 2009) [P]. Monitoring alone had limited support, and education appeared ineffective (Busse 2009) [P]. A newer meta-analysis of 20 trials found psychological interventions reduced PMS severity (SMD −1.57 after removing an outlier) and depressive (SMD −1.08), anxiety (SMD −1.03) and anger/irritability symptoms (SMD −0.85) (Liu 2026) [P]. Most of those trials were in PMS rather than confirmed PMDD (Liu 2026) [P].

Internet CBT in diagnosed PMDD. In 174 women with PMDD, eight weeks of therapist-guided online CBT reduced interference with everyday life, symptom intensity and disability compared with a waiting list, and the effects held at 6 months (Weise 2019) [P]. A waiting list is a weaker comparison than a placebo or another treatment.

Couple CBT. In 83 women, both one-to-one and couple CBT reduced premenstrual symptoms and distress compared with a waiting list, and couple CBT gave more active coping (Ussher 2017) [P]. G41 covers partners in more detail.

The evidence here is consistent in direction but small and mostly of low quality. CBT can sit alongside a medicine; it is not only for people who can't take one.

5. Rung 3: GnRH agonists with add-back

They work, but not long term on their own. A 2025 Cochrane review found that GnRH agonists without add-back improved global premenstrual symptoms compared with placebo (SMD −1.23; high certainty) (Naheed 2025) [S]. More people stopped because of side effects (RR 4.24) (Naheed 2025) [S]. The authors say menopausal side effects rule out long-term use without add-back, and that these medicines can give a short-term break (Naheed 2025) [S].

Add-back is less certain. The same review found only low-certainty evidence on add-back (1 trial, 31 women), and oestrogen plus cyclical progestogen add-back may worsen symptoms (SMD 0.90) (Naheed 2025) [P]. An older meta-analysis found add-back did not reduce the benefit (SMD 0.12) (Wyatt 2004) [S]. The two reviews used different trial sets, and the newer one is more cautious. Long-term add-back data are lacking (Naheed 2025) [P].

Where it fits. Clinical guidance suggests GnRH analogues with stable hormone add-back when SSRIs and the pill have not helped, and names poor provider knowledge and cost as barriers (Wagner-Schuman 2023) [S].

6. Rung 4: removing the ovaries (with or without the womb)

The evidence is small and uncontrolled. In 14 women with severe, treatment-resistant PMS confirmed by daily charting, hysterectomy with oophorectomy and continuous oestrogen ended the cyclical pattern at 6 months (Casper 1990) [P]. The authors said surgery is indicated only for a small, selected group (Casper 1990) [P]. In a look-back interview study of 47 women who had the same surgery, 96% were satisfied or very satisfied and 93.6% reported complete resolution of cyclical symptoms (Cronje 2004) [P]. 93.6% continued HRT, and there was no comparison group (Cronje 2004) [P]. Experts list oophorectomy among the options that act by suppressing ovulation (Nevatte 2013) [S].

Hysterectomy alone is not a PMDD treatment. In women treated for heavy periods (not diagnosed with PMS), premenstrual symptoms fell about equally after hysterectomy with the ovaries kept or a hormonal coil (Leminen 2012) [P].

Practical tip: surgery is permanent and brings on menopause. Questions worth asking include whether a trial of a GnRH agonist could show first how you respond to having no cycle, and what hormone replacement afterwards would involve. G30 covers menopause and HRT.

7. Supplements and lifestyle: PMS evidence, not PMDD evidence

Every study in this section was done in PMS or mixed groups. None used PMDD confirmed by daily ratings. Treat these as unproven for PMDD.

  • Calcium. In a multicentre trial of 466 analysed women with moderate-to-severe PMS, daily calcium cut luteal symptom scores by 48% vs 30% on placebo by the third cycle (Thys-Jacobs 1998) [P].
  • Vitamin B6. A review of 9 trials (940 patients) found B6 improved overall premenstrual symptoms (OR 2.32) and premenstrual depression (OR 1.69), but most trials were of low quality (Wyatt 1999) [P]. A review of 31 nutrition trials found B6, calcium and zinc consistently improved psychological PMS symptoms, though only 1 trial had low risk of bias (Robinson 2024) [P].
  • Vitex (chasteberry). Pooled trials showed a large effect (Hedges g −1.21), but most were at high risk of bias, results varied hugely (I² 91%) and publication bias was likely; the authors say the effect is "at best" an overestimate (Verkaik 2017) [P]. In the 3 best-reported trials (520 women), vitex users were 2.57 times more likely to have PMS remission (Csupor 2019) [P]. A review of 8 trials was positive but found mostly PMS trials, three different products and varying measures (Cerqueira 2017) [P].
  • Magnesium. In 44 women with mild premenstrual symptoms, magnesium alone did not help; magnesium plus B6 slightly reduced anxiety-type symptoms, an effect the authors called "small" (De Souza 2000) [P]. The nutrition review found insufficient evidence for magnesium (Robinson 2024) [P]. Women with PMS do not reliably have lower blood magnesium (Moslehi 2019) [P].
  • Exercise. Exercise for at least 8 weeks reduced PMS symptoms (SMD −1.08), but 87% of trials were at high risk of bias and the authors say it "may be" effective (Pearce 2020) [P].

Experts name CBT, calcium and vitex as warranting further study (Nevatte 2013) [S].

Practical tip: "natural" doesn't mean free of side effects or interactions. Check any supplement with a pharmacist or clinician, especially if you take other medicines.

8. What people try that's untested: cannabis and other substances

In a survey of 634 people, those with PMDD most often used cannabis to manage premenstrual mood (51.5%), and 16.2% used psychedelics (Nazareth 2026) [P]. Use was higher in the luteal phase (Nazareth 2026) [P]. The survey did not measure whether these helped, and no trial has tested them in PMDD (Nazareth 2026) [P].

Practical tip: if you use cannabis, alcohol or other substances to get through the luteal phase, you can tell your clinician without judgement. It tells them how hard those days are, and it matters for how other medicines might interact.

9. What's untested in ADHD

Our library holds no trial of any PMDD treatment in women with ADHD or autism. We found no data on whether SSRIs, pills or GnRH agonists interact with ADHD medication in PMDD, or work differently in ADHD. The only premenstrual ADHD-medication evidence is a 9-person case series of stimulant dose changes, covered in G05 §7. The pill-and-mood findings in young women with ADHD (§3) are the nearest data, and they come from depression diagnoses, not PMDD.

This doesn't mean these treatments won't help you. It means your clinician will be using judgement rather than ADHD-specific trials.

11. Safety: PMDD and suicidal thoughts

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

A meta-analysis of 10 studies found women with PMDD had higher odds of suicide attempt (OR 6.97) and suicidal thoughts (OR 3.95) (Prasad 2021) [S]. In Swedish registers, women with diagnosed premenstrual disorders had a higher rate of suicide (HR 1.92) (Opatowski 2024) [S].

Practical tip: if suicidal thoughts come with your cycle, say so plainly when you discuss treatment. It changes how urgent the conversation is. G41 §6 covers safety planning.

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. Confirm the pattern first. Two cycles of daily ratings (G04).
  2. Bring this ladder. Ask where you are on it and why.
  3. Keep tracking through any treatment. Compare cycles before and after.
  4. Give each option a fair trial, with your clinician. Ask how many cycles to try before judging it.
  5. List everything you take, including ADHD medication, contraception, supplements, alcohol and cannabis.
  6. Don't change, stop or combine medicines on your own. Changes go through your prescriber.
  7. Plan for the hardest days while you're well.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Whether your daily ratings confirm PMDD, PME or both.
  • SSRI schedules: daily, luteal-only or symptom-onset, and which suits your chart.
  • Side effects to expect, and what to do if they happen.
  • Whether a drospirenone or continuous pill is an option, and how to monitor your mood on it.
  • CBT, including online or couple formats.
  • If the first rungs haven't helped: GnRH agonists, add-back and referral to a specialist.
  • Surgery, only after specialist review, including what menopause and HRT would mean.
  • How any option fits with your ADHD medication and other medicines.
  • Suicidal thoughts, especially if they come with your cycle.

What we don't know yet

  • SSRI vs pill head to head. There is no direct trial; the comparison is indirect (Berni 2026) [P].
  • Long-term use. Whether the drospirenone pill works beyond three cycles is unknown (Lopez 2012) [S], and long-term GnRH add-back data are lacking (Naheed 2025) [P]. Our library has no bone-safety data for GnRH agonists in PMDD.
  • Surgery. The evidence is two small, uncontrolled series. There are no long-term data on surgical menopause in PMDD.
  • Supplements in confirmed PMDD. No supplement trial in our library used PMDD confirmed by daily ratings.
  • Cannabis and other substances. No efficacy or safety trial.
  • ADHD and autism. No PMDD treatment trial in either group, and no data on interactions with ADHD medication.
  • Who responds to what. Beyond the finding that symptoms after the period predicted a poorer response (Freeman 2004) [S], we found no data to match a person to a rung.

Popular claims checked
