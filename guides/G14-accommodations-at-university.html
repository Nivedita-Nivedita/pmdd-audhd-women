Accommodations at university (with or without a diagnosis)

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • You may be able to get some support before you have a diagnosis. Ask early, because assessments can take a long time.

  • The strongest case for an adjustment links it to a known difficulty: time, distant deadlines, a late body clock, or symptoms that change across your cycle.

  • Rules differ by country and university. Check your own institution's policy and your national disability-rights body.

Evidence grade: Supported (S) for the research on the difficulties that adjustments target. Parts on the menstrual cycle are Preliminary (P). We did not find an eligible study in our search (last updated October 2026) testing which adjustments work best for university students with ADHD. Advice about offices, emails and appeals is labelled "Practical tip": it is practice guidance, not evidence.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait to see whether the thoughts pass. See crisis support.

How to read this guide

Research claims have a source and a grade: [E] established, [S] supported, [P] preliminary, [U] no direct evidence. Practical tip marks general advice. It has not been tested in a study and has no grade. This guide does not explain the law. Two different questions are mixed up in most advice about adjustments:

  • Can I get it? That depends on access policy and the law, which differ by country, by university and sometimes by department. Your university's disability policy and your national equality or disability-rights body are the official routes.
  • Will it help? That is a research question. The research below is about the difficulties adjustments aim at, not about whether the adjustments themselves work.

Neither answers the other: an adjustment can be your right without being tested, and a well-supported difficulty doesn't by itself give you a legal entitlement.

1. What disability and accessibility offices typically need

We did not find research on how accessibility offices work in our search. Everything in this section is practice guidance, and it varies a lot by country and institution.

Practical tip: what offices often ask for. - Some proof of a condition or difficulty. This might be a diagnosis letter, a report from a clinician, or sometimes a letter from your GP saying you're waiting for assessment. - A description of how it affects your studies. Be specific: "I lose track of time in timed exams," not "I struggle with exams." - A meeting with an adviser, who writes a support plan (the name differs between places) and shares it with your department.

Practical tip. Ask the office early in term what evidence it accepts. Ask in writing, so you have the answer on record.

2. Interim support while you wait for assessment

Difficulties don't start on the day you're diagnosed. In a US community study of 1,001 adults, those with self-reported ADHD diagnoses were less likely than matched controls to hold a college degree (19% vs 26%) or be employed (52% vs 72%) Biederman 2006 [S]. In a meta-analysis of long-term outcomes, childhood ADHD was linked to failing to complete high school (odds ratio 3.7) Erskine 2016 [E]. These are averages across many people, not predictions about you. They show the stakes are real.

Your test scores may not show the problem either. Across 20 studies, executive-function tests and everyday rating scales agreed poorly (only 24% of correlations significant), suggesting they measure different things Toplak 2012 [E]. So "you did fine on the tests" doesn't mean you're fine in daily life.

Practical tip: what to ask for while waiting. - Ask whether the office can offer interim adjustments based on a GP letter or a referral letter. - Ask your department directly for informal flexibility, such as an agreed extension process. Many departments can do this without the accessibility office. - Keep a short diary of what goes wrong and when. It helps both the office and your assessment.

3. Adjustments that match the evidence

The research below describes difficulties. It does not test the adjustments. Linking an adjustment to a documented difficulty is still the most persuasive way to ask for it.

Time

Across 55 studies, people with ADHD found it harder to judge very short durations, and their time-estimation errors fitted a model of a faster-running internal clock Marx 2022 [E]. That is a model researchers use to explain group averages, not a measurement of any one person's clock. These were lab tasks, mostly with young people. The link to exams and everyday lateness is likely but not proven.

Practical tip: adjustments to ask about. Extra time in exams; a visible clock or timer in the exam room; rest breaks that stop the clock.

Deadlines

On average in case-control studies, people with ADHD valued a reward less the further away it was (d=0.43, a medium effect) Jackson 2016 [E]. A deadline months away may feel unreal until it is very close.

Practical tip: adjustments to ask about. Breaking big projects into staged deadlines; a named contact for extension requests; extensions that don't need fresh evidence every time.

Sleep timing

In adults with ADHD and trouble falling asleep, the body's melatonin signal started later in the evening than in controls Van Veen 2010 [S]. A systematic review found consistent evidence for a later body clock in ADHD Coogan 2017 [S].

Practical tip: adjustments to ask about. Exams scheduled later in the day where possible; recorded lectures; flexibility on early-morning labs or classes.

The menstrual cycle

In 32 regularly cycling young women tracked daily, lower estradiol together with higher progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the next day Roberts 2018 [P]. In an online survey, women with self-reported clinical ADHD screened positive for premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD, a severe cycle-linked mood condition) more often than women without ADHD (31.4% vs 9.8%), but the survey used a one-off questionnaire, not daily tracking Broughton 2025 [P]. Confirming a PMDD diagnosis relies on daily ratings across two or more cycles; memory alone was a poor guide Eisenlohr-Moul 2017 [S].

Practical tip. If you notice a predictable bad week, track it daily for two cycles (see G04). A chart is useful evidence for flexible deadlines around that week, with or without a diagnosis. You don't have to wait for the chart to ask for help or to see a clinician.

Sensory load

In 116 adults with ADHD, sensory over- and under-sensitivity was more common than in norms, and 43% of women vs 22% of men reported atypical sensitivity Bijlenga 2017 [S].

Practical tip: adjustments to ask about. A smaller or quieter exam room; permission to use earplugs; a seat away from doors.

4. Template email

Practical tip. Adapt this. Keep it short. Attach any evidence you have.

Subject: Request for study adjustments: [your name, student number, course]

Dear [Accessibility Office / name],

I am writing to ask about study adjustments. I am [diagnosed with ADHD / autistic / on the waiting list for an ADHD/autism assessment since (month, year)]. I have attached [a diagnosis letter / a GP or referral letter / a symptom diary].

The main ways this affects my studies are:

  • [e.g. I lose track of time in timed exams and often don't finish.]

  • [e.g. I find it very hard to start long assignments until the deadline is close.]

  • [e.g. My sleep runs late, so 9 a.m. exams and labs are especially hard.]

  • [e.g. My symptoms are much worse in one predictable week each month; I have tracked this for two cycles.]

Adjustments that I think would help are:

  • [e.g. extra time and rest breaks in exams]

  • [e.g. staged deadlines, or an agreed extension process]

  • [e.g. later exam slots where possible]

If I don't yet have the evidence you need, could you tell me what interim support is possible while I wait?

Could you also tell me who will be told about my condition, and what information they will see?

Thank you, [Name]

Practical tip: a shorter version. If a full email feels like too much, this is enough to start:

Dear [Accessibility Office], I'm a [course] student ([student number]). I have [ADHD / autism / a pending assessment] and it's affecting [exams / deadlines / attendance]. Could we book a meeting to discuss adjustments, and what evidence do you need? Thank you, [Name]

5. If you're refused

We did not find research on appeals in our search. This section is practice guidance only.

Practical tip. - Ask for the decision and the reasons in writing. - Ask what evidence would change the decision, then try to get it. - Ask whether a partial adjustment, or a time-limited trial of one, is possible. - Use your university's complaints or appeals procedure. Your students' union often has free advice workers who know it well. - For the legal side, contact your national equality or disability-rights body rather than relying on general online advice.

What you can do

  1. Contact the accessibility office early, even before diagnosis. (Practical tip)
  2. Describe specific difficulties and link each one to a specific adjustment. (Practical tip)
  3. If your cycle affects you, track daily for two cycles and bring the chart. (Practical tip)
  4. Keep copies of every email and decision. (Practical tip)

Talk to a clinician about…

  • An assessment for ADHD or autism, and a letter confirming you are on the waiting list.
  • Sleep timing that runs late, and whether it needs treatment in its own right.
  • Symptoms that worsen at the same point in each cycle (bring your daily chart).
  • Low mood or exhaustion that doesn't lift with rest.

What we don't know yet

  • Which adjustments work. We did not find eligible trials of extra exam time, extensions or other university adjustments for students with ADHD or autism in our search (last updated October 2026).
  • How accessibility offices handle undiagnosed students. We did not find research on this. Practice varies widely.
  • Whether cycle-aware scheduling helps. Cycle effects on ADHD symptoms come from small studies, and we did not find a study that tested a cycle-based adjustment.
  • University-specific data on women. The impairment studies above are mixed-sex or not specific to students.

Popular claims checked

  • You may have heard: "An online ADHD or autism quiz can tell you whether you have it." What the research supports: screeners can suggest an assessment is worth pursuing, but an online quiz cannot diagnose ADHD or autism. Evidence: established. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "If you did well at school, you can't have ADHD." What the research supports: doing well at school does not rule out ADHD; it occurs in high-IQ people, whose coping can delay diagnosis. Evidence: supported. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "'Time blindness' explains why you're always late." What the research supports: people with ADHD tend to judge time less accurately than others in lab tests, and the link to everyday lateness is likely but not proven. Evidence: supported. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "If your ADHD, anxiety or depression gets worse before your period, that's PMDD." What the research supports: premenstrual worsening of an existing condition is often premenstrual exacerbation, which differs from PMDD. Evidence: established. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "ADHD is really a body clock disorder." What the research supports: many people with ADHD have a later body clock, with delayed melatonin onset and sleep timing, but that does not make ADHD a body clock disorder. Evidence: preliminary. More in Claim check
