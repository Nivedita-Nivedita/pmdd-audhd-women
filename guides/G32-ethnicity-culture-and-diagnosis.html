Who gets diagnosed? Ethnicity, culture and ADHD/autism in women (incl. India and the South Asian diaspora)

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • In the US, ADHD is diagnosed less often in Black, Hispanic and Asian American children and adults than in white people. In one large study of US schoolchildren this held even after accounting for symptoms. That points to gaps in recognition and access, not to less need.

  • In England, autism is recorded far less often in girls, and less often in pupils who speak English as an additional language. Social disadvantage explained much of the difference between ethnic groups.

  • Indian data on adult women are very thin. Adult figures come mostly from screening questionnaires, and the one clinic study of gender we found included 23 women. We did not find an eligible study of stigma, family or help-seeking in South Asian women, in India or in the diaspora, in our search (last updated October 2026).

Evidence grade: SUPPORTED (S) for US and English diagnosis gaps · PRELIMINARY (P) for Indian adult prevalence and for how women present in Indian clinics · South Asian women's help-seeking: no eligible study identified in our search (last updated October 2026). Headline grade: PRELIMINARY (P).

Talk to a clinician about… whether your assessment considered your culture, language and the possibility that ADHD or autism was missed.

Not medical advice. Crisis support →

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait to see if it passes: crisis support.

Tags: [E] established · [S] supported · [P] preliminary · [U] popular but unsupported. Practical tip paragraphs are general guidance, not graded evidence.

How to read this guide. The studies below measure who gets diagnosed and treated. They describe patterns in health systems and diagnostic practice. They do not describe the people in any group, and they don't show that any community "has less" or "more" ADHD or autism. Group labels (such as "Asian American" or "Black") are the ones the studies used. Each finding comes from a particular country (mostly the US, England and India), a particular measure (recorded diagnoses, insurance or school records, or screening questionnaires) and a particular sample (children, students, insured adults, clinic attenders), so it may not carry over to other places or groups. Our search covered studies that met our selection rules; it is not a systematic review, and relevant studies may exist outside it.

1. US: fewer diagnoses and less treatment in minority children and adults

The US has the most data, mostly from children.

  • Same symptoms, fewer diagnoses. In 4,297 US children surveyed in grades 5, 7 and 10, African-American and Latino children had lower odds of an ADHD diagnosis and of ADHD medication than white children at every wave, after controlling for ADHD symptoms and other factors Coker 2016 [S]. The authors conclude this more likely reflects under-diagnosis and under-treatment of minority children than over-diagnosis of white children Coker 2016 [S].
  • A national birth cohort. In 238,011 US children born 2006-2012, the hazard of an ADHD diagnosis was lower for Asian (HR 0.48), Black (HR 0.83) and Hispanic children (HR 0.77) than for white children Shi 2021 [S]. Asian children had the highest odds of receiving no treatment after diagnosis Shi 2021 [S]. A hazard ratio (HR) below 1 means a lower rate of diagnosis over time than in white children.
  • Adults with the same insurance. Among 5,282,877 insured adults in Northern California (2007-2016), white adults consistently had the highest recorded ADHD prevalence (0.67%-1.42%), compared with Black (0.22%-0.69%), Hispanic (0.25%-0.65%) and Asian American adults (0.11%-0.35%) Chung 2019 [S]. Women had slightly lower odds of diagnosis (odds ratio 0.943) Chung 2019 [S]. This study is useful because everyone had the same insurer, which reduces (but doesn't remove) differences in access.
  • The pattern can reverse with age. In US office-visit data for 2012-2013, ADHD diagnosis was 77% less likely among Black than white adults but 24% more likely among Black than white young people Fairman 2017 [P]. The study summary we checked does not name the effect measure behind these percentages, and this is a survey of visits, not of people, so we grade it preliminary.

What this means: at group level, in these US data, minority ethnic children were less often diagnosed, and recorded adult diagnoses were lower too. This describes health-system patterns, not any one person's chances. Both feed into late diagnosis.

2. Contested prevalence in Black Americans

A meta-analysis challenged the statement in the DSM-5 (the main US diagnostic manual) that ADHD is less common in Black Americans.

  • Across 21 studies with 154,818 Black participants, pooled ADHD prevalence among US Black individuals was 14.54% (95% CI 10.64%-19.56%) Cénat 2021 [P]. The authors argue this contradicts the DSM-5 statement of lower prevalence and call for more assessment and culturally appropriate care Cénat 2021 [P].
  • Read it carefully. Only two of the 21 studies were in adults, and the pooled figure is much higher than other estimates Cénat 2021 [P]. So it is strong evidence against the idea that ADHD is rarer in Black Americans, but weak evidence for any specific figure.

3. England: autism records by ethnicity, language and sex

  • Whole-country school data. Among 7,047,238 pupils in England, 1.76% had an autism record: 2.81% of male and 0.65% of female pupils, a ratio of 4.32 to 1 Roman-Urrestarazu 2021 [S].
  • Ethnicity. Recorded autism was highest in Black pupils (2.11%) and lowest in Roma/Irish Traveller pupils (0.85%) Roman-Urrestarazu 2021 [S]. The ethnicity effect was mostly explained by social disadvantage Roman-Urrestarazu 2021 [S].
  • Language. Pupils who spoke English as an additional language were less likely to have an autism record (adjusted prevalence ratio 0.64) Roman-Urrestarazu 2021 [S]. The authors suggest differences in detection or referral Roman-Urrestarazu 2021 [S].

For South Asian families in the UK: the abstract we reviewed does not report figures for Indian, Pakistani or Bangladeshi pupils, so we can't say how they compare. The language finding is relevant to any family where English isn't the first language at home.

4. India: what the numbers can and can't tell us

Children. India has some good child data. - In a population survey of 3,964 children aged 2 to 9 across five regions of India, 9.2% of children aged 2 to under 6, and 13.6% of those aged 6 to 9, had a neurodevelopmental disorder Arora 2018 [S]. No clear difference was found by gender, rural or urban residence, or religion Arora 2018 [S]. ADHD was assessed in 6 to 9 year olds Arora 2018 [S]. - Across 19 Indian studies, pooled ADHD prevalence was 63.2 per 1000 children, but 75.1 per 1000 in school-based and 18.6 per 1000 in community-based studies Chauhan 2022 [P]. That gap shows how much the study method changes the answer.

Adults. Here the evidence is weak. - A systematic review of 10 Indian studies found adult ADHD prevalence ranging from 5.48% to 25.7% Mishra 2024 [P]. In the same paper, 14% of 1,665 college students in Delhi-NCR (69.1% female) screened positive on the ASRS Mishra 2024 [P]. The ASRS is a screening questionnaire. A positive screen is not a diagnosis.

The global baseline. Worldwide, adjusted to the 2020 population, about 2.58% of adults have persistent ADHD that began in childhood, and 6.76% have ADHD symptoms Song 2021 [S].

How to compare them. The Indian adult figures are mostly screening results in particular groups, such as students. The global figures come from population-based studies. So you can't conclude that adult ADHD is "more common in India". The honest summary is that adult ADHD in India is under-studied, and screening suggests it is not rare Mishra 2024 [P].

5. Women in Indian clinics: more often given a personality-disorder diagnosis

  • In 101 adults seeking treatment at an Indian tertiary (specialist) clinic, only 23 were women Goyal 2025 [P]. The women had higher ASRS scores, more mind-wandering, hyperfocus and mood swings, lower self-esteem and more severe illness Goyal 2025 [P].
  • Women were more likely to be diagnosed with a personality disorder than with ADHD, and showed a trend towards low help-seeking and less typical main complaints Goyal 2025 [P].
  • Similar patterns elsewhere. In Norwegian registers, the extra burden of personality disorders, anxiety and depression linked to ADHD was larger in women than in men Solberg 2017 [S]. In a small Italian clinic sample, autistic women waited longer for referral and were more often misdiagnosed at first Gesi 2021 [P].

What this means: if you have been told you have a personality disorder, anxiety or depression and you also recognise lifelong ADHD or autistic traits, it is reasonable to ask whether both have been considered. This is a question of diagnostic overshadowing (one diagnosis hiding another), not of the first diagnosis being "wrong".

6. What is missing: stigma, family and help-seeking in South Asian women

We did not find an eligible study in our search (last updated October 2026) on the topics below. Studies may exist that fall outside our selection rules (for example, small qualitative studies or work not indexed where we searched): - stigma about ADHD or autism among women in India or the South Asian diaspora; - the role of family in seeking, or not seeking, assessment; - how South Asian women in the UK, US, Canada or elsewhere are diagnosed as adults.

We are not going to fill this gap with generalisations about "South Asian culture". Families and communities differ widely, and we did not find research that could support such generalisations. What the evidence does show is that systems under-recognise ADHD and autism in women Young 2020 [E], in minority ethnic groups in the US Coker 2016 [S], in Asian American children and adults Shi 2021 [S] Chung 2019 [S], and in children who speak an additional language at home in England Roman-Urrestarazu 2021 [S]. The US "Asian" figures group many different communities together, so they can't be read as South Asian figures.

What you can do

  • Treat a missed childhood diagnosis as common, not as your fault. In one US national survey of adults aged 18-44, most of those with ADHD were untreated for it, though many had been treated for something else Kessler 2006 [E].
  • Ask for the assessment in the language you think and feel in. Practical tip: if English isn't your first language, ask whether an interpreter or a bilingual clinician is available. Many people find symptoms easier to describe in their own words.
  • Bring evidence from childhood. Practical tip: school reports, a parent's or sibling's memories, and examples from daily life help, especially if your symptoms were quiet.
  • Name diagnostic overshadowing. Practical tip: "I've been treated for anxiety (or given a personality-disorder diagnosis). Could ADHD or autism also be part of the picture?"
  • In India. Practical tip: if you screen positive on a questionnaire, take the result to a psychiatrist or your doctor as a reason to ask for a full assessment. It is not a diagnosis on its own.
  • Treat online quizzes as a starting point only (see "Popular claims checked").

For clinicians and researchers

  • Women may arrive with less typical main complaints and be given another diagnosis first, so ask about ADHD symptoms directly Goyal 2025 [P].
  • Report ethnicity, language and sex in prevalence and service studies, and break down broad "Asian" categories where samples allow. Practical tip.
  • In India, distinguish screening prevalence from diagnosed prevalence in reporting Mishra 2024 [P].

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Whether your assessment took account of your language, culture and schooling.
  • Whether a personality-disorder, anxiety or depression diagnosis could sit alongside ADHD or autism.
  • How a screening score fits with a full assessment.
  • Your options if ADHD medication is offered, and how follow-up works.

What we don't know yet

  • South Asian women's experience. We did not find an eligible study of stigma, family or help-seeking in Indian or diaspora women in our search (last updated October 2026); see section 6.
  • South Asian diaspora diagnosis rates. We did not find a UK, US or Canadian study of adult South Asian women's diagnosis in our search. The US data we found combine all Asian groups.
  • Diagnosed adult prevalence in India. Adult figures are mostly screening-based; we did not find a population study using diagnostic interviews in Indian adults.
  • Indian women in clinics. The only gender study we found had 23 women.
  • Autism in Indian women. We did not find an eligible adult study in our search.
  • Why gaps exist. The studies show patterns in diagnosis. They mostly cannot separate access, clinician bias, language, cost and stigma.

Popular claims checked

  • "ADHD isn't really a thing in my community." / "Black people have less ADHD." Verdict: mostly true. US data show lower diagnosis even at the same symptom level Coker 2016 [S], and a meta-analysis found ADHD common among Black Americans, though that figure is contested Cénat 2021 [P].
  • "Adult ADHD affects 1 in 4 Indians." Verdict: overstated. 25.7% is the top of a 5.48%-25.7% range of mostly screening studies Mishra 2024 [P].
  • adhd-first-treated-as-anxiety-or-depression: "Women with ADHD are often treated for anxiety or depression for years before anyone spots the ADHD." Verdict: mostly true. In an Indian clinic, women were more often diagnosed with a personality disorder than ADHD Goyal 2025 [P].
  • online-quiz-can-diagnose-adhd-or-autism: "An online ADHD or autism quiz can tell you whether you have it." Verdict: mostly true. The Indian student figure of 14% is an ASRS screen, not a diagnosis Mishra 2024 [P].
