Night owl: delayed sleep timing in ADHD

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • People with ADHD tend to have a later body clock. Across 62 studies, later chronotype and later melatonin timing were consistent findings Coogan 2017 [S].

  • Melatonin can shift sleep timing earlier. It is a sleep-timing tool, not an ADHD treatment Van der Heijden 2007 [S]; van Andel 2021 [P].

  • Morning light and timed melatonin for adults with ADHD have only small or open-label studies behind them Fargason 2017 [P]. "ADHD is a circadian disorder" goes beyond the evidence Bijlenga 2019 [U].

Evidence grade: SUPPORTED (S) for delayed sleep timing in ADHD. PRELIMINARY (P) for body-clock treatments improving ADHD symptoms. POPULAR BUT UNSUPPORTED (U) for "ADHD is a circadian disorder". Talk to a clinician about: trouble falling asleep, melatonin timing, how your ADHD medication affects your sleep, and winter low mood. Not medical advice.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

A few terms first:

  • Circadian rhythm: your roughly 24-hour body clock.
  • Chronotype: whether you're naturally a "lark" (early) or an "owl" (late).
  • DLMO (dim-light melatonin onset): the time in the evening when your body starts making the sleep hormone melatonin. Researchers measure it in saliva under dim light. It is the standard marker of where your body clock is set.

1. Delayed melatonin onset in many adults with ADHD

  • In 40 adults with ADHD, 31 had long-term trouble falling asleep. Those 31 had a later DLMO and later sleep timing than controls, and than adults with ADHD who slept normally Van Veen 2010 [S].
  • A systematic review of 62 studies (4,462 people with ADHD) found consistent evidence for later chronotype and later DLMO or sleep onset in ADHD Coogan 2017 [S].
  • In adults, this pattern of long sleep latency (time to fall asleep), eveningness and late DLMO resembles delayed sleep phase disorder Snitselaar 2017 [S].
  • One study found adults with ADHD had altered body-clock rhythms at the level of behaviour, hormones (cortisol) and clock genes Baird 2012 [P].

Note the wording in the Van Veen study: the delay was in the adults who had sleep-onset insomnia, not in every adult with ADHD.

2. How you feel vs what the machines show

How you rate your own sleep and what a sleep lab measures don't always agree.

  • A meta-analysis of 13 studies in adults with ADHD Díaz-Román 2018 [S]:
  • Adults with ADHD reported worse sleep on 7 of 9 self-rated measures (SMD 0.56–1.55).
  • Actigraphy (a wrist-worn movement sensor) showed longer time to fall asleep (SMD 0.80) and lower sleep efficiency.
  • Polysomnography (an overnight sleep-lab study of brain waves) showed no differences.
  • In children, a meta-analysis of 16 studies (722 with ADHD, 638 controls) found more bedtime resistance, trouble falling asleep, night waking and daytime sleepiness, and objectively longer time to fall asleep Cortese 2009 [E].
  • In adults with ADHD, insomnia is reported in 43–80% Wynchank 2017 [S].

So a "normal" sleep-lab result and real, measurable sleep complaints can sit side by side Díaz-Román 2018 [S].

3. Melatonin: shifts timing, doesn't treat ADHD

In children with ADHD

  • In 105 medication-free children aged 6–12 with ADHD and long-term trouble falling asleep, melatonin moved sleep onset 26.9 minutes earlier and DLMO 44.4 minutes earlier, and increased total sleep. It did not improve behaviour, thinking skills or quality of life Van der Heijden 2007 [S].
  • Across 13 trials (682 children) with neurodevelopmental conditions, melatonin increased total sleep by 48.26 minutes and cut time to fall asleep by 28.97 minutes. There were no medication-related serious side effects. Study differences limited the quality of the evidence Abdelgadir 2018 [S].
  • A youth consensus review puts healthy sleep habits first and behavioural approaches as first-line for insomnia. It notes trials support melatonin for delayed sleep onset Cortese 2013 [S].

In adults with ADHD and delayed sleep phase

  • In a trial of 51 adults, low-dose melatonin (0.5 mg) moved DLMO earlier by 1 h 28 min and reduced self-reported ADHD symptoms by 14%. Melatonin plus bright light did not reduce symptoms. The effects disappeared 2 weeks after stopping van Andel 2021 [P].
  • In a second trial report on sleep (49 adults with ADHD and delayed sleep phase), melatonin with or without light shifted the body clock but did not shift actual sleep times or improve sleep. The authors concluded behavioural coaching is needed van Andel 2022 [P].
  • A review describes afternoon or evening melatonin, morning bright light and sleep hygiene as treatments used for delayed rhythms in adult ADHD Kooij 2013 [S].

The take-home: melatonin can move your clock, but the trial authors concluded that behavioural coaching is needed for sleep times to follow van Andel 2022 [P]. Doses here describe what studies used. They are not a recommendation.

4. Morning light: small and open-label studies

"Open-label" means everyone knew what treatment they were getting. There was no dummy treatment to compare with.

  • In an uncontrolled pilot study in adults with ADHD, morning bright light moved DLMO 31 minutes earlier and mid-sleep 57 minutes earlier. The bigger the shift, the bigger the drop in ADHD symptoms Fargason 2017 [P].
  • In an open trial of 29 adults in autumn and winter, morning light reduced ADHD measures. Improvement tracked a shift towards being more of a morning person, rather than changes in mood Rybak 2006 [P].
  • Light therapy can move rhythms earlier Snitselaar 2017 [S].
  • A 2025 review of sleep and body clocks in autistic and ADHD young people covers light therapy evidence in adults Lok 2025 [S].

Promising, but the light-only studies above had no comparison group. In the adult melatonin trial, adding light to melatonin did not reduce ADHD symptoms van Andel 2021 [P].

5. Stimulants and sleep: mixed in adults

  • In adults, studies that measured sleep systematically during stimulant treatment show mixed effects. Some found sleep quality or structure improved Surman 2022 [P].
  • In children and teenagers (9 studies, 246 young people), stimulants lengthened time to fall asleep (ES 0.54), reduced sleep efficiency (ES −0.32) and shortened total sleep (ES −0.59). The effect on sleep efficiency was smaller in those who had taken medication longer Kidwell 2015 [S].
  • Treatment-emergent insomnia has been reported in 10–45% of adults on various stimulants, and in some on atomoxetine Wynchank 2017 [S].
  • Stimulants may delay body-clock rhythms Snitselaar 2017 [S].

So medication can make sleep worse for some people and better for others.

Practical tip. Talk to your prescriber before changing anything, and bring a sleep diary.

6. Seasonality and late sleep

  • In 2,239 adults in the NESDA cohort study, probable seasonal affective disorder (winter depression) was found in 9.9% of those with clinically significant ADHD symptoms vs 3.3% of those without. Late sleep onset partly explained the link Wynchank 2016 [S].
  • The open light-therapy trial above was run in autumn and winter Rybak 2006 [P].

Practical tip. If your sleep drifts later and your mood drops in winter, mention both to a clinician together.

7. Tools, and when to see a sleep clinician

Practical tip: a sleep diary. For two weeks, write down each day:

  • when you got into bed, and roughly when you fell asleep
  • when you woke, and whether an alarm woke you
  • naps, caffeine, alcohol and screen use late in the evening
  • when you took any medication
  • whether it was a work/study day or a free day

The gap between your free-day and work-day sleep is useful information.

Practical tip: the MCTQ. The Munich ChronoType Questionnaire asks about your sleep timing on work and free days. It is free. Our questionnaires page explains it and its limits. A morningness–eveningness questionnaire (MEQ) measures preference but does not diagnose a sleep disorder.

Practical tip: when to ask for a sleep referral.

  • You can't fall asleep before the early hours, even when you try.
  • Your sleep is very different on free days.
  • You are very sleepy in the day despite enough time in bed.
  • You have restless legs, loud snoring or pauses in breathing.
  • Melatonin or light hasn't helped, or you aren't sure how to time them.

In 200 adults with ADHD, 33.0% had restless legs syndrome, and low iron stores were more common in those with it Lopez 2019 [S]. See guide G06.

What you can do

  1. Keep a two-week sleep diary and fill in the MCTQ.
  2. Notice whether your sleep is later in winter.
  3. Track how sleep changes on days you take, skip or change ADHD medication.
  4. If you try melatonin or morning light, do it with a clinician who can help you time it. Pair it with a steady wake-up time.
  5. Keep "I'm a night owl" and "I have ADHD" as two separate questions. They often go together, but treating one doesn't treat the other.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Trouble falling asleep, and whether a delayed sleep phase could be part of it.
  • Melatonin: whether it suits you, and when to take it.
  • Whether morning light therapy is worth trying.
  • How your ADHD medication timing affects your sleep.
  • Low mood in winter, especially with late sleep.
  • Restless legs and your iron levels.

What we don't know yet

  • Women specifically. No study in our library reports body-clock results separately for women with ADHD.
  • The menstrual cycle. We have no evidence on whether sleep timing in ADHD changes across the cycle.
  • Light therapy. Only open-label or uncontrolled studies exist in adults with ADHD.
  • Chronotherapy for ADHD symptoms. The only adult trial had about 50 people, and the symptom effect faded after stopping.
  • Whether a late body clock causes some ADHD symptoms. This is a hypothesis about a subgroup Bijlenga 2019 [P], not established.
  • Stimulants in adults. Effects on sleep are mixed. Most of the harm signal comes from children.

Popular claims checked

  • melatonin-treats-adhd: "Melatonin treats ADHD." Verdict: overstated. It shifts sleep timing and lengthens sleep. It didn't improve behaviour or thinking in children. A 14% symptom drop in adults faded after stopping.
  • adhd-is-a-circadian-disorder: "ADHD is really a circadian rhythm (body clock) disorder." Verdict: overstated. A delayed clock is common in ADHD, but adult sleep-lab studies show no objective difference. The idea that a delayed clock causes ADHD symptoms is a hypothesis about a subgroup.
