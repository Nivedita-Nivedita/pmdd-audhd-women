Guides › G28

Clinicians

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short - Maternal ADHD is associated with modestly higher preterm birth, preeclampsia and severe maternal and neonatal morbidity in Canadian registry data. In the largest Swedish study, the preterm excess attenuated to non-significance after adjustment for age, education and co-occurring mental disorders. Both are presented below. - Autistic women had higher odds of preterm birth (mostly medically indicated), elective caesarean and preeclampsia in Swedish registers. - How much of the ADHD excess is modifiable, and whether adapted antenatal care changes outcomes, has not been tested.

Evidence grade: S (Supported) for associations; effect of interventions not studied. · Act on: recording ADHD/autism and co-occurring mental disorders at booking, preeclampsia vigilance, and a coordinated medication plan.

Not medical advice. Crisis resources →

Medication exposure is covered in G26. This guide is about the condition.

1. Preterm birth: Swedish and Canadian registries compared

The registries disagree on whether maternal ADHD independently raises preterm birth risk.

Study Population ADHD definition Preterm birth Interpretation
Hesselman 2020 [S] 377,381 nulliparous women, Sweden 2007-2014; 6,327 (1.7%) with ADHD Medication dispensing 7.3% vs 5.8%; aOR 1.17 (95% CI 1.05-1.30), particularly very preterm (<32 weeks); both spontaneous and medically indicated Authors: most of the excess risk is due to modifiable risk factors
Andersson 2025 [S] 1,617,121 pregnancies, Sweden 2006-2020; 17,434 pregnancies in 12,553 women with ADHD Diagnosis 6.6% vs 4.9%; crude OR 1.33 (95% CI 1.25, 1.42); adjOR 1.06 (0.99, 1.13) after adjusting for maternal age, education and comorbid mental disorders Excess attenuated to non-significance
Zipursky 2026 [S] 1,051,033 deliveries, Ontario 2013-2023; 22,995 (2.2%) with preconception ADHD Preconception diagnosis aRR 1.41 Largely unchanged after excluding psychostimulant fills in pregnancy

Reading them together. The direction is consistent: crude preterm rates are higher with maternal ADHD in all three. Whether an independent excess remains depends on adjustment. Andersson 2025, the largest and most recent Swedish study, adjusted for co-occurring mental disorders and found the excess largely explained. Hesselman 2020 defined ADHD by dispensed medication, so ADHD and medication exposure cannot be separated in that cohort. Zipursky 2026 found an association that persisted when stimulant-exposed pregnancies were removed. The library summary does not list Zipursky's covariates, so we cannot say whether co-occurring mental disorders were adjusted for. We do not think either reading should be dropped.

Andersson 2025 also found maternal ADHD remained associated with large-for-gestational-age babies (adjOR 1.16) and planned caesarean section (adjOR 1.16) (Andersson 2025) [S].

2. Severe maternal and neonatal morbidity

In Ontario, compared with deliveries without maternal ADHD (Zipursky 2026) [S]:

  • Severe maternal morbidity: 2.8% vs 2.3% (aRR 1.31, 1.21-1.42).
  • Severe neonatal morbidity: 15.7% vs 12.5% (aRR 1.25, 1.21-1.29).
  • Preeclampsia (1.28), preterm birth (1.41) and small for gestational age (1.21) were also associated; gestational diabetes was not.
  • Findings were largely unchanged after removing people who filled a psychostimulant prescription in pregnancy.

This is one registry; we found no replication of the severe-morbidity outcome elsewhere. Read the relative risks next to the absolute rates above: both outcomes were somewhat more common, and most deliveries in both groups had neither.

3. Autistic women: medically indicated preterm birth, caesarean and preeclampsia

In 2,198 births to autistic women vs 877,742 births in Sweden 2006-2014 (Sundelin 2018) [S]:

  • Preterm birth: OR 1.30 (1.10-1.54), especially medically indicated preterm birth: OR 1.41 (1.08-1.82).
  • Elective caesarean: OR 1.44.
  • Preeclampsia: OR 1.34.
  • Not associated: emergency caesarean, low Apgar score, gestational diabetes, stillbirth.

The excess is concentrated in planned interventions. The study summary does not tell us why (for example, clinical indication, maternal request, or communication around birth planning). That is a question for future research, not something to assume.

4. What is modifiable

Partly a "what we don't know yet".

  • Hesselman's authors attribute most of the excess preterm risk to modifiable risk factors (Hesselman 2020) [S]. The library summary does not specify which factors or their individual contributions.
  • In Andersson 2025, adjusting for comorbid mental disorders (with age and education) removed the significant preterm excess (Andersson 2025) [S]. That points to co-occurring mental health conditions as a target for antenatal attention, though adjustment does not prove that treating them would change outcomes.
  • Medication-specific signals (preeclampsia with stimulants in the first half of pregnancy, adjusted RR 1.29 (95% CI 1.11-1.49); preterm birth with continuation after 20 weeks, adjusted RR 1.30 (1.10-1.55)) are summarised in G26 (Cohen 2017) [S].

We could not quantify, from the library, the separate roles of smoking or substance use in women with ADHD in pregnancy. No intervention study targeting these risks in pregnant women with ADHD was found.

5. Antenatal care adjustments, and what the review literature leaves open

A systematic review of parental ADHD in pregnancy and the postpartum period covers birth outcomes, health behaviour in pregnancy, early parent-child interaction and treatment risks (Kittel-Schneider 2021) [P]. Its abstract describes scope only and notes that data on early parent-child interaction are lacking (Kittel-Schneider 2021) [P].

We found no trial or cohort study of adapted antenatal care for women with ADHD or autism. The practice points below are therefore labelled practice guidance, not evidence.

What to do in practice (one-page handout)

Practice guidance, not graded evidence.

Booking and antenatal care: women with ADHD or autism

Record at booking - ADHD and/or autism diagnosis; current and recent ADHD medication (drug, dose, duration). - Co-occurring depression, anxiety, PMDD/premenstrual disorder, substance use, smoking. - Communication preferences (written summaries, advance notice, sensory needs).

Risk awareness (associations, not causes) - Preterm birth: raised crude rates; independent excess contested between registries (Hesselman 2020; Andersson 2025; Zipursky 2026) [S]. - Preeclampsia: associated with maternal ADHD in Ontario (Zipursky 2026), autism in Sweden (Sundelin 2018) and stimulant use in the first half of pregnancy (Cohen 2017) [S]. - Severe maternal and neonatal morbidity: modestly raised with maternal ADHD in Ontario (Zipursky 2026) [S]. - Autism: more medically indicated preterm birth and elective caesarean (Sundelin 2018) [S].

Coordinate - Shared medication plan with the ADHD prescriber, made with the woman (G26). Avoid unplanned abrupt changes. - Perinatal mood screening plan, with PMDD history flagged (G27).

Adjust the process - Written appointment summaries and reminders; one named contact. - Discuss birth-planning options early and explain reasons for any planned intervention clearly. - Ask about sensory needs for scans, examinations and the ward.

Talk to a clinician about…

For discussion within the care team and with the woman:

  • Her own priorities and history when weighing medication decisions.
  • Which co-occurring conditions are active now, and who is treating them.
  • Whether a referral to perinatal mental health services is warranted.
  • What adjustments to communication and environment would make antenatal care accessible.

What we don't know yet

  • Whether maternal ADHD independently raises preterm birth after full adjustment: registries conflict.
  • Which modifiable factors drive the excess, and their relative contributions.
  • Whether adapted antenatal care improves outcomes for women with ADHD or autism. No study found.
  • Why autistic women have more planned interventions. Not explained by the available summaries.
  • Severe maternal morbidity outside Ontario. Not replicated in the library.
  • Women with both ADHD and autism. Not analysed separately.

Safety and crisis resources

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

Co-occurring mental disorders appear central to the ADHD risk profile in pregnancy (Andersson 2025) [S], and pre-existing mental health diagnoses were the strongest predictor of postpartum psychiatric admission in women with ADHD (RR 2.60) (Xu 2026) [S]. Ask directly about suicidal thoughts during pregnancy and after birth.

Popular claims checked

  • "ADHD makes pregnancy high-risk." (Not yet in the claims ledger.) Verdict: overstated. Associations with some complications are real but modest (Zipursky 2026) [S], and the preterm excess was not significant after adjustment in the largest Swedish study (Andersson 2025) [S].
  • "The pregnancy risks are all from the medication." (Not yet in the claims ledger.) Verdict: unsupported. In Ontario, associations were largely unchanged after removing stimulant-exposed pregnancies (Zipursky 2026) [S].
