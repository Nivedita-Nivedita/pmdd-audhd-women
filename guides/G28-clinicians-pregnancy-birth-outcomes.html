For clinicians: pregnancy and birth outcomes in women with ADHD or autism

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Maternal ADHD is associated with modestly higher preterm birth, preeclampsia and severe maternal and neonatal morbidity in Canadian registry data. In the largest Swedish study, no clear independent preterm association remained after adjustment for age, education and co-occurring mental disorders. Both are presented below.

  • Outcomes associated with the condition (maternal ADHD or autism) are kept separate here from outcomes associated with medication exposure, which are covered in G26. These are population associations, not individual risk predictions.

  • Autistic women had higher odds of preterm birth (mostly medically indicated), elective caesarean and preeclampsia in Swedish registers.

  • How much of the ADHD excess is modifiable, and whether adapted antenatal care changes outcomes, we did not find tested in our search (last updated October 2026).

Evidence grade: S (Supported) for associations; effect of interventions no eligible study found. · Act on: recording ADHD/autism and co-occurring mental disorders at booking, preeclampsia vigilance, and a coordinated medication plan.

Not medical advice. Crisis support →

Medication exposure is covered in G26. This guide is about the condition. Where a study cannot separate the two (for example, when ADHD is defined by dispensed medication), we say so.

Effect measures, named each time: OR odds ratio, aOR/adjOR adjusted odds ratio, aRR adjusted risk ratio, RR risk ratio. They are not interchangeable.

1. Preterm birth: Swedish and Canadian registries compared

The registries disagree on whether maternal ADHD independently raises preterm birth risk.

Study Population ADHD definition Preterm birth Interpretation
Hesselman 2020 [S] 377,381 nulliparous women, Sweden 2007-2014; 6,327 (1.7%) with ADHD Medication dispensing 7.3% vs 5.8%; aOR 1.17 (95% CI 1.05-1.30), particularly very preterm (<32 weeks); both spontaneous and medically indicated Authors: most of the excess risk is due to modifiable risk factors
Andersson 2025 [S] 1,617,121 pregnancies, Sweden 2006-2020; 17,434 pregnancies in 12,553 women with ADHD Diagnosis 6.6% vs 4.9%; crude OR 1.33 (95% CI 1.25, 1.42); adjOR 1.06 (0.99, 1.13) after adjusting for maternal age, education and comorbid mental disorders No clear independent association after adjustment
Zipursky 2026 [S] 1,051,033 deliveries, Ontario 2013-2023; 22,995 (2.2%) with preconception ADHD Preconception diagnosis adjusted risk ratio (aRR) 1.41 Largely unchanged after excluding psychostimulant fills in pregnancy

Reading them together. The direction is consistent: crude preterm rates are higher with maternal ADHD in all three. Whether an independent excess remains depends on adjustment. Andersson 2025, the largest and most recent Swedish study, adjusted for co-occurring mental disorders, after which no clear independent association remained; the interval (0.99-1.13) does not exclude a small excess. Hesselman 2020 defined ADHD by dispensed medication, so ADHD and medication exposure cannot be separated in that cohort. Zipursky 2026 found an association that persisted when stimulant-exposed pregnancies were removed. The abstract does not list Zipursky's covariates, so we cannot say whether co-occurring mental disorders were adjusted for. We do not think either reading should be dropped.

Andersson 2025 also found maternal ADHD remained associated with large-for-gestational-age babies (adjOR 1.16) and planned caesarean section (adjOR 1.16) (Andersson 2025) [S].

2. Severe maternal and neonatal morbidity

In Ontario, compared with deliveries without maternal ADHD (Zipursky 2026) [S]:

  • Severe maternal morbidity: 2.8% vs 2.3% (aRR 1.31, 1.21-1.42).
  • Severe neonatal morbidity: 15.7% vs 12.5% (aRR 1.25, 1.21-1.29).
  • Preeclampsia (aRR 1.28), preterm birth (aRR 1.41) and small for gestational age (aRR 1.21) were also associated; no clear association was found with gestational diabetes.
  • Findings were largely unchanged after removing people who filled a psychostimulant prescription in pregnancy.

This is one registry; we did not find a replication of the severe-morbidity outcome in our search (last updated October 2026). Read the relative risks next to the absolute rates above: both outcomes were somewhat more common, and most deliveries in both groups had neither.

3. Autistic women: medically indicated preterm birth, caesarean and preeclampsia

In 2,198 births to autistic women vs 877,742 births in Sweden 2006-2014 (Sundelin 2018) [S]:

  • Preterm birth: OR 1.30 (1.10-1.54), especially medically indicated preterm birth: OR 1.41 (1.08-1.82).
  • Elective caesarean: OR 1.44.
  • Preeclampsia: OR 1.34.
  • No clear association found: emergency caesarean, low Apgar score, gestational diabetes, stillbirth. This does not prove these risks are unchanged.

The excess is concentrated in planned interventions. The abstract does not tell us why (for example, clinical indication, maternal request, or communication around birth planning). That is a question for future research, not something to assume.

4. What is modifiable, and condition vs medication

Partly a "what we don't know yet".

Associated with the condition (maternal ADHD or autism):

  • Hesselman's authors attribute most of the excess preterm risk to modifiable risk factors (Hesselman 2020) [S]. The abstract does not specify which factors or their individual contributions, and because ADHD was defined by dispensed medication, condition and drug exposure overlap in that cohort.
  • In Andersson 2025, adjusting for comorbid mental disorders (with age and education) removed the significant preterm excess (Andersson 2025) [S]. That points to co-occurring mental health conditions as a target for antenatal attention, though adjustment does not prove that treating them would change outcomes.
  • In Ontario, associations with maternal ADHD were largely unchanged after removing people who filled a psychostimulant prescription in pregnancy (Zipursky 2026) [S].

Associated with medication exposure (compared with unexposed pregnancies; see G26):

  • Preeclampsia with stimulants in the first half of pregnancy, adjusted risk ratio 1.29 (95% CI 1.11-1.49); preterm birth with continuation after 20 weeks vs stopping, adjusted risk ratio 1.30 (1.10-1.55) (Cohen 2017) [S]. Cohen's authors state the absolute increases are small and that women with significant ADHD should not be counselled to stop treatment on the basis of these findings (Cohen 2017) [S]. Details, including malformation and neurodevelopmental data, are in G26.

We could not separate the roles of smoking or substance use in pregnant women with ADHD from the abstracts we reviewed. We did not find an intervention study targeting these risks in pregnant women with ADHD in our search (last updated October 2026).

5. Antenatal care adjustments, and what the review literature leaves open

A systematic review of parental ADHD in pregnancy and the postpartum period covers birth outcomes, health behaviour in pregnancy, early parent-child interaction and treatment risks (Kittel-Schneider 2021) [P]. Its abstract describes scope only and notes that data on early parent-child interaction are lacking (Kittel-Schneider 2021) [P].

We did not find a trial or cohort study of adapted antenatal care for women with ADHD or autism in our search (last updated October 2026). The practice points below are therefore labelled practice guidance, not evidence, except where they cite a guideline.

Guideline recommendations that apply to all women ({CG}):

  • At booking and in the early postnatal period, consider the two depression identification questions and the GAD-2, and repeat at later contacts; use the EPDS or PHQ-9 as part of a full assessment where indicated ({CG}).
  • At first contact, ask about past or present severe mental illness, previous specialist or inpatient care, and severe perinatal mental illness in a first-degree relative; refer women with current or past severe mental illness to a secondary (preferably specialist perinatal) mental health service ({CG}).
  • Be alert for postpartum psychosis in the first 2 weeks after birth in women with a personal or family history of severe mental illness; refer sudden-onset symptoms for assessment within 4 hours ({CG}).
  • Specialist perinatal mental health services can advise on the risks of psychotropic medication during breastfeeding ({CG}).

What to do in practice (one-page handout)

Practice guidance, not graded evidence.

Booking and antenatal care: women with ADHD or autism

Record at booking

  • ADHD and/or autism diagnosis; current and recent ADHD medication (drug, dose, duration).

  • Co-occurring depression, anxiety, PMDD/premenstrual disorder, substance use, smoking.

  • Communication preferences (written summaries, advance notice, sensory needs).

Risk awareness (population associations, not causes, and not individual predictions)

Associated with the condition:

  • Preterm birth: raised crude rates with maternal ADHD; independent excess contested between registries (Hesselman 2020; Andersson 2025; Zipursky 2026) [S].

  • Preeclampsia: associated with maternal ADHD in Ontario (Zipursky 2026) [S] and with autism in Sweden (Sundelin 2018) [S].

  • Severe maternal and neonatal morbidity: modestly raised with maternal ADHD in Ontario (Zipursky 2026) [S].

  • Autism: more medically indicated preterm birth and elective caesarean (Sundelin 2018) [S].

Associated with medication exposure:

  • Preeclampsia with stimulant use in the first half of pregnancy; preterm birth with continuation after 20 weeks (Cohen 2017) [S]. See G26.

Coordinate

  • Perinatal mood screening at booking and postnatally, with referral of current or past severe mental illness (NICE CG192) [Guideline].

  • Shared medication plan with the ADHD prescriber, made with the woman (G26). Avoid unplanned abrupt changes.

  • Perinatal mood screening plan, with PMDD history flagged (G27).

Adjust the process

  • Written appointment summaries and reminders; one named contact.

  • Discuss birth-planning options early and explain reasons for any planned intervention clearly.

  • Ask about sensory needs for scans, examinations and the ward.

Talk to a clinician about…

For discussion within the care team and with the woman:

  • Her own priorities and history when weighing medication decisions.
  • Which co-occurring conditions are active now, and who is treating them.
  • Whether a referral to perinatal mental health services is warranted.
  • What adjustments to communication and environment would make antenatal care accessible.

What we don't know yet

  • Whether maternal ADHD independently raises preterm birth after full adjustment: registries conflict.
  • Which modifiable factors drive the excess, and their relative contributions.
  • Whether adapted antenatal care improves outcomes for women with ADHD or autism. We did not find an eligible study in our search (last updated October 2026).
  • Why autistic women have more planned interventions. Not explained by the abstracts we reviewed.
  • Severe maternal morbidity outside Ontario. We did not find a replication in our search.
  • Women with both ADHD and autism. Not analysed separately in the studies we found.
  • Individual risk. None of these studies offers a validated way to predict an individual woman's risk.

Safety and crisis resources

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

Co-occurring mental disorders appear central to the ADHD risk profile in pregnancy (Andersson 2025) [S], and pre-existing mental health diagnoses were the strongest predictor of postpartum psychiatric admission in women with ADHD who had been taking stimulants (risk ratio 2.60) (Xu 2026) [S]. Ask directly about suicidal thoughts during pregnancy and after birth, and arrange help appropriate to the level of risk rather than waiting for a routine review (NICE CG192 [Guideline]).

Postpartum psychosis is a psychiatric emergency. NICE recommends assessment by a secondary (preferably specialist perinatal) mental health service within 4 hours of referral for sudden-onset symptoms (NICE CG192 [Guideline]). Patient information describes sudden onset, usually within 2 weeks of birth, with hallucinations, delusions, mania, low or rapidly changing mood, or confusion (NHS: Postpartum psychosis [Reference]). Crisis routes for patients: help page.

Popular claims checked

  • You may have heard: "ADHD makes pregnancy high-risk." What the research supports: ADHD is linked with modestly higher rates of some pregnancy complications, not with a uniformly high-risk pregnancy. Associations with some complications are real but modest (Zipursky 2026) [S], and no clear independent preterm association remained after adjustment in the largest Swedish study (Andersson 2025) [S].
  • You may have heard: "The pregnancy risks are all from the medication." What the research supports: The pregnancy associations with ADHD do not appear to come mainly from medication. In Ontario, associations were largely unchanged after removing stimulant-exposed pregnancies (Zipursky 2026) [S].
