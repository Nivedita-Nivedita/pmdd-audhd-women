Guides › G01

Women, Students

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short - Many women with ADHD are recognised late. Experts point to quieter, more inward symptoms, coping strategies that hide them, and diagnosis that has tended to follow disruptive behaviour. - A proper adult assessment is a clinician's interview about your whole life, including childhood, ideally with someone who knew you then. It is more than a questionnaire. - Screening tools screen; they don't diagnose. A low score does not rule ADHD out, especially if you have spent years compensating.

Evidence grade: SUPPORTED (S). The assessment process rests on expert consensus (E). The reasons women are missed rest on reviews and large studies, mostly of children (S).

Talk to a clinician about… see the section near the end.

Not medical advice.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

Tags: [E] established · [S] supported · [P] preliminary · [U] popular but unsupported. Practical tip and Process note paragraphs are general guidance, not research findings, so they carry no grade.

1. Why women are often recognised late

A UK expert consensus on girls and women named four main barriers to recognising ADHD: differences in how symptoms show up, gender bias, other conditions that draw attention instead, and compensatory strategies that mask symptoms Young 2020 [E]. ("Compensatory strategies" means the workarounds you build, such as lists, over-preparing or working late, that keep things on track at a cost.) The same experts recommend that clinicians look for a subtler, more internalised presentation, and keep looking across a woman's life Young 2020 [E]. "Internalised" means the difficulty is mostly felt inside, as worry, low mood or a racing mind, rather than seen as disruptive behaviour.

A major review found that girls meet ADHD criteria at just under half the rate of boys, but the ratio approaches parity by adulthood Hinshaw 2021 [S]. Girls and women showed more inattention and internalising problems, and were often overlooked because their symptoms were less overt and they used compensatory strategies Hinshaw 2021 [S].

Who gets referred matters too. In 19,804 Swedish twins rated by their parents at age 9, hyperactivity, impulsivity and conduct problems predicted a clinical ADHD diagnosis (conduct problems: odds ratio 1.43) and medication (odds ratio 2.20) more strongly in girls than in boys Mowlem 2018 [S]. The authors suggest that girls with ADHD may be more easily missed unless they also have prominent disruptive behaviour Mowlem 2018 [S]. This was a study of children, so it tells us about childhood referral, not about adult clinics.

A review in The Lancet Psychiatry concluded that females are diagnosed with ADHD less often and later than males, and are less often prescribed medication Martin 2024 [S]. It weighs several explanations: whether the criteria suit girls, diagnostic thresholds, "diagnostic overshadowing" (another condition getting the attention), and how girls present and compensate Martin 2024 [S].

Doing well at school does not settle the question. A review of 41 articles concluded that satisfactory academic achievement should not rule out ADHD in girls and women Quinn 2014 [P].

One caution. ADHD in women is not a different disorder. A review of 73 studies concluded that many apparent gender differences in adult ADHD may partly come from how studies were done, or from social and cultural influences Williamson 2015 [S]. The clearer differences are in who gets noticed and referred.

Adults in general are missed too. A European consensus of 63 experts found adult ADHD is under-diagnosed and under-treated Kooij 2018 [E]. In a US survey of adults aged 18 to 44, most with ADHD had not been treated for it, though many had been treated for other mental health or substance problems Kessler 2006 [S].

2. "Late recognition", not "adult onset"

You may have seen headlines saying ADHD can start in adulthood. Two cohort studies did find many adults with ADHD who had not met criteria as children. In a New Zealand birth cohort followed to age 38, 90% of adult cases had no history of childhood ADHD Moffitt 2015 [P]. In a British twin cohort, 67.5% of 166 young adults with ADHD had not met criteria at any childhood assessment Agnew-Blais 2016 [P].

One team then tested this more thoroughly. They followed 239 people without childhood ADHD and assessed them 8 times, from a mean age of 9.89 to 24.40 years Sibley 2018 [S]. Approximately 95% of those who first screened positive for "late-onset" ADHD were excluded once everything was considered Sibley 2018 [S]. Among those with impairing late symptoms, the most common reason was that the symptoms occurred only with heavy substance use Sibley 2018 [S]. The team found no evidence of ADHD starting in adulthood independent of a complex psychiatric history Sibley 2018 [S].

What this means for you: - "Late recognition" is the more accurate phrase. Your clinician will look for childhood signs, even if nobody named them then. - A careful assessment checks other explanations. That isn't doubt; it is good practice. - The study was not about women specifically, so we don't know if the pattern holds for women who compensated well as girls.

3. What an adult assessment involves

Experts agree that adult ADHD can be diagnosed with validated screening and diagnostic instruments, and that effective treatments exist Kooij 2018 [E]. A major overview concluded that ADHD diagnosis is reliable and valid when standard criteria are used Faraone 2024 [E].

Process note (this describes standard criteria and tools, as summarised in our questionnaire guide; it is not a graded research claim). An ADHD diagnosis needs a clinician's assessment. Under DSM-5, the diagnostic manual most clinicians use: - some symptoms were present before age 12; - they cause impairment in two or more settings, such as work, study, home or relationships; and - other explanations have been ruled out.

Process note. A good assessment usually includes: - A structured interview. The DIVA-5 is widely used in Europe and in many Canadian and UK clinics. It goes through all 18 DSM-5 symptoms, in childhood and in adulthood. It then asks about impairment in five areas: education or work, relationships, family, leisure, and self-confidence. It gives example behaviours for each item, which helps if you have learned to hide things. How long it takes varies; we could not verify a typical time. We also could not confirm a published validation of the English-language DIVA-5, although validations exist in other languages. - A childhood history. School reports help if you have them. - An informant. Ideally this is someone who knew you as a child or knows you well now, such as a parent, sibling or partner. - Rating scales, which you and an informant fill in.

Why the interview matters more than a test score: lab tests of "executive function" (planning, attention, self-control) and everyday rating scales often disagree. Across 20 studies, only 68 of 286 correlations between the two were significant, with a median r of .19 Toplak 2012 [E]. The authors concluded that they measure different things. Tests capture cognitive efficiency; ratings capture success in pursuing goals in everyday life Toplak 2012 [E]. In a group followed from childhood hyperactivity to a mean age of 27, self-reported executive problems predicted impairment in daily life and work better than tests did Barkley 2011 [S]. Most adults whose ADHD persisted were impaired on the ratings, but few were impaired on the tests Barkley 2011 [S].

So "your test results were normal" does not by itself answer the question.

4. Screening questionnaires: what they can and can't tell you

Screening tools screen. They don't diagnose. A high score means "worth assessing". A low score does not rule ADHD out, and that matters most for women who have spent years compensating. The reason is the one experts give for missed diagnoses in women: coping strategies can hide symptoms Young 2020 [E]. What we don't know is how often a screener misses ADHD in women who mask. No study in our library has measured this.

Process note: two screeners you will meet. - ASRS v1.1 (Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale). This is an 18-item checklist of current symptoms, and 6 of the questions form a short screener. The 6-item screener is free to use. Its wording follows DSM-IV, the older edition of the manual, which our questionnaire review notes is weighted towards visible hyperactivity. Women who mask may under-report on it. - WURS-25 (Wender Utah Rating Scale). This asks you to rate yourself as a child ("As a child I was…"). It is widely reproduced, and the Canadian ADHD Resource Alliance (CADDRA) hosts a free copy. Caveats, all hedged: it is often said that the WURS was developed in men. The original paper's abstract does not report the sex of participants, so we could not verify this. Its items may lean towards conduct and temper, so inattentive girls may score low. We have not checked the item wording against the full paper. It relies on your memory, so use it alongside an informant and school records.

The original validation papers for the ASRS and WURS are not yet in our evidence library. For that reason we don't quote their accuracy figures here.

Other questionnaires describe your profile. For example, a scale on excessive mind wandering picked out adults with ADHD with about .9 sensitivity and specificity in two adult ADHD studies, and mind wandering added to impairment beyond the core symptoms Mowlem 2016 [S]. Our assessment-pack guide (G02) lists what is worth filling in before your appointment.

Practical tip. When you fill in a screener, rate yourself as you are without your workarounds: without the lists, the late nights and the bursts of last-minute focus. Write a note of the workarounds next to your answers.

5. Common misdiagnoses and overlaps

ADHD rarely travels alone. In a combined analysis of large studies of adults (550,748 with ADHD, 14,546,814 without), the pooled odds ratios were 5.0 for anxiety, 4.5 for major depression, 8.7 for bipolar disorder and 4.6 for substance use disorders Hartman 2023 [E]. Sex did not change these associations Hartman 2023 [E]. In Norwegian registries covering 40,103 adults with ADHD, the extra rates of anxiety, depression, bipolar and personality disorders linked to ADHD were larger in women than in men Solberg 2017 [S].

So anxiety or depression may be noticed first. Reviews note that these conditions can lead to ADHD being missed or misdiagnosed in women Quinn 2014 [P]. In qualitative studies, adults described ADHD symptoms that overlap with other conditions, which may contribute to misdiagnosis and delay Ginapp 2022 [P]. No study we have gives a precise misdiagnosis rate for women.

Overlaps your clinician should consider: - Bipolar disorder and borderline personality disorder (BPD). ADHD co-occurs in about 20% of adults with either Asherson 2014 [S]. Bipolar disorder comes in episodes, while ADHD and BPD are long-standing and trait-like Asherson 2014 [S]. Several features point to BPD rather than ADHD: frantic efforts to avoid abandonment, self-harm and suicidal behaviour, chronic emptiness, and stress-related paranoia or dissociation Asherson 2014 [S]. Impulsivity and emotional ups and downs occur in both ADHD and BPD Ditrich 2021 [S]. - Autism. ADHD is the most common co-occurring diagnosis in autistic people, at a pooled 28% Lai 2019 [E]. About 21% of children and adolescents with ADHD meet the threshold for autism Hollingdale 2019 [E]. A clinical review of autism first diagnosed in adulthood said possible misdiagnosis, especially in women, should be explored Lai 2015 [S]. - Premenstrual worsening. Around 60% of women with mood disorders report that symptoms get worse before their period. This is called premenstrual exacerbation, or PME Kuehner 2021 [S]. PME is different from PMDD, and daily tracking is how they are told apart (see G04).

Having one of these doesn't rule out ADHD, and having ADHD doesn't rule them out.

6. Waiting lists, private vs public routes, and what to do while you wait

What we don't know yet. Our evidence library has no studies on waiting times for adult ADHD assessment. It also has none comparing the quality or outcomes of private and public assessments. We can't tell you which route is better, so we won't guess.

Practical tip: choosing a route. Whichever route you take, ask: - whether the assessor is qualified to diagnose adult ADHD where you live; - whether they use a structured interview such as the DIVA-5; - whether they will speak to an informant; - whether they will consider overlapping conditions such as anxiety, mood disorders, autism and PME; and - whether a private diagnosis will be accepted by your GP or public service for prescribing or shared care.

Practical tip: use the waiting time. - Start a daily symptom diary now if your symptoms shift with your cycle. Diagnosing PMDD needs daily ratings over at least two cycles. In 200 women, retrospective reports of premenstrual worsening poorly predicted the diagnosis that daily ratings gave Eisenlohr-Moul 2017 [E]. Two cycles take about two months, so start early. - Gather childhood evidence: school reports, and a parent or sibling willing to answer questions. - Write your impact summary (see G02). - Keep getting help for low mood, anxiety or sleep; they don't need to wait for the ADHD assessment.

7. After the assessment: whatever the outcome

If you are diagnosed. You may feel relief, grief, or both. In a synthesis of 8 qualitative studies of women diagnosed as adults, one recurring theme was self-acceptance after diagnosis Attoe 2023 [P].

On treatment, a large analysis of 113 trials in adults (45.6% of them women) found that only stimulants and atomoxetine reduced core symptoms at 12 weeks on both self-rated and clinician-rated scales Ostinelli 2025 [E]. Psychological approaches such as CBT and psychoeducation helped on clinician ratings only, and longer-term evidence is lacking Ostinelli 2025 [E]. Talk to your clinician about what suits you.

If you are not diagnosed. That is useful information too. Ask what does explain your difficulties. In the careful follow-up study described above, most apparent late-onset cases had other explanations Sibley 2018 [S]. One possibility to ask about is cognitive disengagement syndrome (formerly "sluggish cognitive tempo"): daydreaming, mental fog and slowness. An international work group concluded it is a distinct syndrome, though its exact nature is unclear Becker 2023 [S]. Ask about autism, mood, anxiety and sleep too.

Practical tip. Whatever the outcome, ask for a written report explaining the reasoning. If something important was missed, such as childhood evidence or an informant, ask for it to be considered, or seek a second opinion.

What you can do

Practical tips 1. Book a GP or family-doctor appointment and say plainly: "I'd like to be referred for an adult ADHD assessment. Here is a one-page summary of how it affects me." 2. Fill in a screener, such as the ASRS, to bring along. Treat it as a conversation starter, not a verdict. 3. Ask a parent, sibling or old friend whether they would talk to the assessor or fill in a childhood form. 4. Find your school reports. Look for comments like "daydreams", "could try harder" or "disorganised", not only comments about behaviour. 5. Start daily mood and symptom tracking if things get worse before your period. 6. Write down your workarounds and what they cost you in hours, sleep and energy.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Whether your history fits ADHD, and what else could explain it.
  • How anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, BPD or autism have been considered.
  • Whether your symptoms change with your menstrual cycle, and whether daily tracking would help.
  • If you are diagnosed: treatment options, including the evidence on medication and on psychological support.
  • If you are not diagnosed: what the assessor thinks is going on, and what support is available.
  • Any thoughts of self-harm or suicide. Raise these directly; they matter more than any assessment timetable.

What we don't know yet

  • How often screeners miss ADHD in women who mask. No study in our library measured screener accuracy in women, in high-IQ adults, or in people who compensate heavily. We could not find a study validating screener cut-offs in gifted adults.
  • How well the DIVA-5 performs in women. We could not confirm a published English-language validation.
  • Waiting lists, private vs public routes. Our library has no evidence on wait times, or on how the quality of the two routes compares.
  • Whether "late recognition" looks the same in women. The most careful study of late onset was not about women specifically Sibley 2018 [S].
  • Masking in ADHD. We found no validated ADHD-specific masking measure (see G03).
  • Exact misdiagnosis rates. No study we hold gives a rate of ADHD being misdiagnosed as anxiety, depression or BPD in women.

Popular claims checked

  • girls-with-adhd-get-missed: "Girls with ADHD get missed because they're quiet and daydreamy." Mostly true (supported). Girls tend to be diagnosed later and less often, partly because diagnosis tends to follow disruptive behaviour.
  • adhd-can-start-in-adulthood: "ADHD can start in adulthood." Overstated (supported). "Late recognition" is more accurate than "late onset".
  • online-quiz-can-diagnose-adhd-or-autism: "An online quiz can tell you whether you have it." False (established). Screeners can show an assessment is worth pursuing. A low score doesn't rule ADHD out.
  • adhd-first-treated-as-anxiety-or-depression: "Women are treated for anxiety or depression for years before anyone spots the ADHD." Mostly true (supported). These conditions are common with ADHD and can be noticed first, but no study gives a precise misdiagnosis rate.
  • adhd-in-women-is-a-different-disorder: "ADHD in women is a completely different disorder." Overstated (supported). It is the same condition, more often inattentive and internalised, and so easier to miss.
  • good-grades-rule-out-adhd: "If you did well at school, you can't have ADHD." False (supported).
