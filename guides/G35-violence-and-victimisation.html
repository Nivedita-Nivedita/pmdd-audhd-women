Safety: violence and victimisation in women with ADHD or autism

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Women who had ADHD as girls, and autistic people, report much higher rates of partner violence and sexual violence than others. The studies are small or self-selected, so exact figures are uncertain, but the pattern is consistent.

  • Violence is always the responsibility of the person who commits it. Higher rates in neurodivergent women are a reason for better protection and support, not a reason to blame anyone.

  • You deserve to be safe. Help is available, whether or not you have a diagnosis, and whether or not you are ready to leave.

Evidence grade: SUPPORTED (S) that autistic people experience high rates of victimisation, and for partner violence in young women with childhood ADHD (one prospective cohort) · PRELIMINARY (P) for the "nine in ten" figure and for ADHD and partner violence in general. Headline grade: PRELIMINARY (P).

Talk to a clinician about… anything that makes you feel unsafe now, and support for the effects of past experiences.

Not medical advice. Crisis support → · Abuse and sexual-assault services →

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait for things to settle: see crisis support.

If you are in danger now, contact your local emergency services (911 in Canada and the US, 999 in the UK, 112 in India). For support with domestic violence, abuse or sexual assault, see the checked services for Canada, the US, the UK and India below. You don't need to have reported anything, or to have decided what to do, to talk to them.

Content note. This page discusses partner violence and sexual violence in general terms. It does not describe any assault. You can skip straight to "Safety planning and services" or the list of services at any point.

Tags: [E] established · [S] supported · [P] preliminary · [U] popular but unsupported. Practical tip paragraphs are general guidance, not graded evidence.

1. ADHD and partner violence against young women

"Intimate partner violence" (IPV) means harm by a current or former partner. It can be physical, sexual, emotional or financial.

  • The clearest evidence. A US study followed 114 women who had ADHD as girls and 79 women who didn't, up to ages 17 to 24 Guendelman 2015 [S]. Physical partner violence had been experienced by 30.7% of women with childhood ADHD compared with 6.3% of the comparison group Guendelman 2015 [S].
  • Persistence mattered. Rates were highest when ADHD persisted into adulthood (37.3%), compared with ADHD that faded (19.0%) and never having ADHD (5.9%) Guendelman 2015 [S].
  • School was part of the pathway. Lower school achievement in adolescence partly explained the link Guendelman 2015 [S].
  • The wider picture. A major review lists intimate partner violence among the heightened risks faced by girls and women with ADHD Hinshaw 2021 [E].

This is one US cohort of 193 women. We did not find a large register study of partner violence against women with ADHD in our search (last updated October 2026). The finding is important, but it needs confirming.

2. Autistic people: pooled victimisation rates

  • A meta-analysis. Across 34 studies, 44% of autistic people had experienced some form of victimisation Trundle 2022 [S]. This included 47% for bullying, 40% for sexual victimisation and 16% for child abuse Trundle 2022 [S]. The pooled figure for multiple forms of victimisation was 84% Trundle 2022 [S].
  • The studies varied a lot, so these are averages across very different groups Trundle 2022 [S]. The abstract we checked does not give figures for women separately.

3. The "nine in ten" figure: where it comes from and how to read it

You may have seen the claim that nine in ten autistic women have experienced sexual violence. It comes from one French study.

  • What it found. In an online survey of 225 autistic women, 88.4% reported sexual victimisation on a standardised questionnaire, and 68.9% when asked an open question Cazalis 2022 [P]. Of 199 women who had been victimised, 135 were first assaulted at age 18 or younger Cazalis 2022 [P].
  • Where the authors put responsibility. The authors argue that prevention must target offenders and culture, not victims Cazalis 2022 [P].
  • How to read it. The women chose to take part in an online community survey. People with experiences of violence may be more likely to answer such a survey, so the figure can't be read as the rate for all autistic women. The pooled meta-analytic figure for sexual victimisation is 40% Trundle 2022 [S].

The accurate sentence: "Autistic people experience high rates of sexual victimisation: 40% across 34 studies, and higher in one self-selected survey of autistic women." Both figures are far too high. Neither should be used to suggest that violence is inevitable.

4. ADHD and partner violence: a small literature focused on perpetration

  • A systematic review found only seven studies (four case-control and three cohort) on ADHD and adult domestic or partner violence Buitelaar 2016 [P]. Most were about people who commit violence, and were not broken down by sex Buitelaar 2016 [P].
  • Three case-control studies found positive links, but two did not properly account for conduct disorder or antisocial personality disorder Buitelaar 2016 [P].

This review tells us little about women who are harmed. It mainly shows how thin the evidence is.

5. Safety planning and services

We did not find an eligible study testing safety-planning approaches for neurodivergent women (search last updated October 2026). The steps below are general good practice, labelled as practical tips. Expert guidance for autistic adolescents with gender dysphoria lists victimisation as an area clinicians should address Strang 2016 [P]; we found no equivalent guidance for adult women with ADHD or autism.

What you can do

  • Trust your sense that something is wrong. Practical tip: you don't need to be certain, or to have "proof", to ask for support.
  • Make a simple safety plan. Practical tip: write down, somewhere safe, who you could call, where you could go, and what you'd take (ID, medication, phone charger, important documents). ADHD can make planning under stress harder, so doing it in advance helps.
  • Use reminders that don't reveal too much. Practical tip: if someone checks your phone, store contacts under a neutral name. See "Using your phone or computer safely" below before clearing history.
  • Ask services for adjustments. Practical tip: you can ask for written information, a quieter space, extra time, or a support person, if those help you.
  • Look after the after-effects. Practical tip: if you notice anxiety, sleep problems, low mood or feeling numb after what happened, you can ask for support, however long ago it was.

For clinicians

  • Ask about safety routinely and privately, especially with young women with persistent ADHD and with autistic patients Guendelman 2015 [S] Trundle 2022 [S].
  • Place responsibility on perpetrators in how you talk about risk Cazalis 2022 [P].
  • Adapt communication: plain, direct questions; written follow-up. Practical tip.

Services you can contact (checked 5 October 2026)

We checked each number below on the service's own website or an official government page on 5 October 2026. Numbers and hours change, so check the linked page if a call doesn't connect. You don't need to have reported anything, have a diagnosis, or have decided to leave to contact any of them.

Canada - Immediate danger: call 9-1-1. - Family and partner violence: there is no single national domestic-violence number. The Government of Canada's family violence helplines page lists helplines for each province and territory, and ShelterSafe.ca (Women's Shelters Canada) has a map of women's shelters across the country. - Sexual assault: the Ending Sexual Violence Association of Canada directory lists sexual assault centres and crisis lines by province. - Indigenous Peoples: Hope for Wellness Helpline, 1-855-242-3310, 24/7 (listed by the Government of Canada).

United States - Immediate danger: call 911. - Domestic violence: National Domestic Violence Hotline, 1-800-799-7233 (1-800-799-SAFE), or text START to 88788 (thehotline.org). - Sexual assault: RAINN National Sexual Assault Hotline, 800-656-4673 (800-656-HOPE), or text HOPE to 64673 (rainn.org).

United Kingdom - Immediate danger: call 999. - Domestic abuse: National Domestic Abuse Helpline (run by Refuge), 0808 2000 247, free, 24 hours (nationaldahelpline.org.uk). - Sexual violence, England and Wales: Rape Crisis 24/7 Rape & Sexual Abuse Support Line, 0808 500 2222 (rapecrisis.org.uk). - Scotland: Domestic Abuse and Forced Marriage Helpline, 0800 027 1234 (sdafmh.org.uk); Rape Crisis Scotland helpline, 08088 01 03 02 (rapecrisisscotland.org.uk). - Wales: Live Fear Free helpline, 0808 80 10 800, 24/7, for domestic abuse and sexual violence; calls welcomed in Welsh (gov.wales). - Northern Ireland: Domestic and Sexual Abuse Helpline, 0808 802 1414, 24 hours (nidirect.gov.uk).

India - Emergency: 112 (Emergency Response Support System, for police, fire, health and other emergency help). - Women Helpline: 181 (national women helpline). - National Commission for Women 24×7 helpline: 14490. - All three are listed on the National Commission for Women helplines page; 181 is also listed by the Ministry of Women and Child Development.

Elsewhere: contact your local emergency number, and ask a GP, clinic or local women's organisation for the nearest domestic-violence or sexual-assault service.

Using your phone or computer safely

Practical tip. If someone else can see your phone, computer or accounts: - A "quick exit" button on a website does not erase anything. It only takes you to another page. The site you visited stays in your browsing history unless you delete it. - Clearing history removes the record, but a suddenly empty history can itself be noticed. You may prefer to delete only specific entries, or to use a private (incognito) window, which doesn't save history on that device (it doesn't hide your activity from someone monitoring your network or accounts). - The safest option is a device the other person can't access: a friend's phone, a work or library computer, or a public phone. Some services above, such as the UK National Domestic Abuse Helpline, have advice on checking your devices for tracking. - Watch for shared accounts and location sharing. Call logs, texts, shared cloud accounts and location-sharing apps can show whom you contacted and where you went. - If you think your devices are being monitored, changing settings suddenly can raise suspicion. A service like those above can help you plan what to change and when.

Other kinds of help - A crisis line, if you feel unable to keep yourself safe: see crisis support. - Your GP or primary-care clinician, for health care, records and referral. - Autism or ADHD support organisations, which may know services that offer adjustments.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Feeling unsafe in a relationship, now or in the past.
  • Support after sexual violence, including if it happened a long time ago.
  • Anxiety, low mood, sleep problems or flashbacks.
  • Adjustments that would make health and support services easier for you to use.

What we don't know yet

  • Register-level evidence on victimisation of women with ADHD. We did not find any in our search; the best evidence we found is one cohort of 193 women.
  • Women-specific autistic figures from the pooled meta-analysis.
  • Population-representative rates for sexual violence against autistic women.
  • What prevents victimisation, and what helps afterwards, for neurodivergent women. We did not find an eligible intervention or safety-planning study, so section 5 is practical guidance only.
  • ADHD and partner violence in women beyond the single cohort; the review literature is small and mostly about perpetration.

Popular claims checked

  • "Nine in ten autistic women are victims of sexual violence." Verdict: partly true, overstated as a population figure. It comes from a self-selected survey of 225 women Cazalis 2022 [P]; the pooled figure for sexual victimisation in autistic people is 40% Trundle 2022 [S].
  • "Women with ADHD keep choosing abusive partners." Verdict: misleading and blaming. Young women with persistent childhood ADHD experienced more partner violence Guendelman 2015 [S], but responsibility for violence lies with the person who commits it.
  • "People with ADHD are violent partners." Verdict: overstated. The evidence is seven studies, and some links were partly explained by other behaviour disorders Buitelaar 2016 [P].
