Midlife: perimenopause, HRT and the ADHD or autistic brain

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Whether menopause is harder with ADHD is genuinely contested: one large cohort says yes, one survey says no. Autistic people report more bothersome psychological and physical menopause symptoms in small studies.

  • Perimenopause is a known window of risk for depression, and a history of premenstrual or perimenopausal mood problems is a flag. In one trial, HRT started early in the transition reduced new depressive symptoms.

  • In one long trial of healthy postmenopausal women, oral oestradiol showed no clear effect on memory and thinking. A meta-analysis did not find a clear association between HRT and dementia; certainty varies, and HRT should not be started to prevent dementia. We did not find a trial of HRT for ADHD symptoms.

Evidence grade: HRT trials S (mood) and E (cognition, in the trial population); ADHD and autism in menopause P (Preliminary); HRT for ADHD no eligible trial found. Headline grade P. · Talk to a clinician about: changes in focus, sleep and mood in your 40s, your mood history, and what HRT would be for in your case.

Not medical advice. Crisis support →

This guide extends the perimenopause section of G23.

A short glossary (definitions, not evidence claims):

  • Perimenopause / menopause transition: the years of changing hormones before periods stop. Early transition means cycles are starting to vary; late transition means periods are often missed.
  • HRT / MHT (menopause hormone therapy): oestrogen (oestradiol), usually with a progestogen if you have a womb. Transdermal means through the skin, as a patch or gel.
  • RCT (randomised controlled trial): a study where people are randomly given a treatment or placebo. The strongest way to test whether a treatment works.
  • Executive functions: planning, switching, holding information in mind. Related to ADHD, but not the same thing.

1. Is menopause worse with ADHD? Two studies disagree

Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025 [P] Chapman 2025 [P]
Who 535 women with self-reported ADHD and 4,857 without, from a population-based cohort 656 women aged 45 to 60; 245 with an ADHD diagnosis
Main finding Total perimenopausal symptom scores 18.0 vs 13.0; severe symptoms 54.2% vs 30.1% (prevalence ratio 1.80, 1.64-1.98), across psychological, somatic and urogenital symptoms After correction for multiple tests, no clear difference in menopausal complaints at any stage, with or without medication
Age pattern Gap largest at ages 35-39 (19.0 vs 12.5); authors read this as earlier perimenopause ADHD symptoms and menopausal complaints correlated in everyone, less strongly within the ADHD group

Why might they differ? Possible reasons include how ADHD was defined (self-report vs diagnosis), sample size, and how women with ADHD attribute their symptoms, which Chapman's authors suggest (Chapman 2025) [P]. Neither study followed the same women over time. Both readings rest on real data, so we present them together rather than choosing one.

An expert review argues that hormonal transitions exacerbate ADHD symptoms and mood, and that undiagnosed women have increased vulnerability to cardiovascular disease in perimenopause (Kooij 2025) [P]. That review is expert opinion, not new data.

2. Autistic menopause: more bothersome psychological symptoms

  • A survey of 342 people (242 autistic, 100 non-autistic) found autistic people reported significantly more bothersome psychological and somatic menopause symptoms, with no clear difference in hot flushes and night sweats (Charlton 2025) [P]. Autistic people also reported more bothersome symptoms before menopause, so it is not yet clear how much is due to menopause itself (Charlton 2025) [P].
  • A Dutch study found autistic women reported more menopausal complaints, linked to depression and autistic traits (Groenman 2022) [P]. In non-autistic women in the same study, menopausal complaints were linked to ADHD traits and depression (Groenman 2022) [P].
  • A focus group of 7 autistic women described autism-related difficulties (sensory sensitivity, socialising, communicating needs) worsening during menopause, sometimes so much they could no longer mask (Moseley 2020) [P]. They described meltdowns, anxiety, depression and suicidal feelings, and very little support (Moseley 2020) [P].

These are small or self-selected samples. They are still the best evidence available, and they line up.

3. ADHD does not fade in midlife, especially in women

In 323 adults with ADHD assessed three times from a mean age of 34 to 47, 68.8% had a stable ADHD course, 25.5% unstable and 5.7% remitting (Grevet 2022) [P]. Being a woman, more severe ADHD, more co-occurring conditions and genetic liability to depression predicted a stable course (Grevet 2022) [P].

So if your ADHD feels "worse" in your 40s, one possibility is that it is the same ADHD meeting new demands and a changing body. Another is perimenopause, depression, poor sleep or something else. These can overlap.

Practical tip: it can help to ask "what changed, and when?" rather than "is it ADHD or menopause?". If difficulties with focus and organisation have been lifelong but got harder at a particular point, write down that point and what else changed then: cycle length, sleep, night sweats, mood, work or caring load. If the change came with low mood, loss of interest or hopelessness, mention that first, because depression is treatable and can look like worsening ADHD. Bringing a few weeks of notes usually gets you further than a single description of a bad week.

4. Perimenopausal depression and premenstrual history

A known window of risk. In a cohort of 218 African American and 218 white women aged 35 to 47 followed for 4 years, depressive symptoms were more likely during the transition to menopause and less likely afterwards, after accounting for past depression, severe PMS and other factors (Freeman 2004) [S]. Hormone patterns were consistent with a role for the changing hormonal environment, though an observational cohort cannot show cause (Freeman 2004) [S]. Expert guidelines describe perimenopause as a window of vulnerability for depressive symptoms and major depression, and note that most women with a perimenopausal major depressive episode have had a prior episode (Maki 2018) [S].

Premenstrual history. On prospective daily ratings, 21% of depressed vs 3% of non-depressed perimenopausal women met criteria for premenstrual dysphoria (Richards 2006) [P]. It was not present in every depressed woman, and women's own recall did not predict it (Richards 2006) [P]. Daily tracking is more reliable than memory here, as with PMDD (G04).

Sensitivity to oestrogen dropping. In women with past perimenopausal depression, a blinded switch from oestradiol to placebo raised depression scores (CES-D 2.4 to 8.8; HAM-D 3.0 to 6.6) (Schmidt 2015) [P]. Controls did not change, despite similar hormone levels on placebo (Schmidt 2015) [P]. This was 26 women and 30 controls, so it is a strong design but a small study.

We did not find an eligible study of perimenopausal depression specifically in women with ADHD or autism in our search (last updated October 2026). A premenstrual mood history is relevant because, in survey studies, women with ADHD screened positive for PMDD more often than women without ADHD; a positive screen is not a confirmed PMDD diagnosis (G05). On premenstrual symptoms themselves: in daily diaries from 290 women, no clear effect of late reproductive vs early menopause-transition stage on premenstrual symptom severity was found (Mitchell 2022) [P]. In a cohort of 436 women followed for 5 years, premenstrual syndrome decreased across the transition, but women with PMS at the start were more likely to report hot flushes (odds ratio 2.09), depressed mood (odds ratio 2.34) and poor sleep (odds ratio 1.72) (Freeman 2004b) [S].

5. HRT and mood: a prevention trial and guidelines

Prevention. In 172 perimenopausal and early postmenopausal women aged 45 to 60 who were not depressed at the start, 12 months of transdermal oestradiol plus intermittent micronised progesterone reduced the onset of clinically significant depressive symptoms compared with placebo (17.3% vs 32.3%; odds ratio 2.5, 95% CI 1.1-5.7) (Gordon 2018) [S]. The benefit was seen in the early menopause transition, not in the late transition or after menopause (Gordon 2018) [S].

Treatment. Expert guidelines say antidepressants and psychotherapy are front-line for perimenopausal depression (Maki 2018) [S]. Oestrogen is not approved for perimenopausal depression but has evidence of antidepressant effects in perimenopausal women, particularly those with hot flushes and night sweats; data for oestrogen plus progestogen are sparse (Maki 2018) [S].

None of these trials selected women with ADHD or autism.

What UK guidance says HRT is for, and its risks. These are clinical recommendations, not new research findings (NICE NG23 [Guideline]):

  • Indications. NICE recommends offering HRT for menopause-related hot flushes and night sweats, and considering HRT for depressive symptoms that start around the same time as other menopause symptoms but do not meet criteria for depression. For diagnosed depression, it says to follow the depression guideline alongside the menopause guideline (NICE NG23 [Guideline]).
  • Blood clots. The risk of venous thromboembolism (blood clots) is increased with oral HRT and not increased with transdermal HRT (NICE NG23 [Guideline]).
  • Breast cancer. Combined HRT increases breast cancer risk; the increase rises with duration of use and falls after stopping but persists for at least 10 years (NICE NG23 [Guideline]).
  • Stroke. Combined HRT with oral oestrogen increases stroke risk; with transdermal oestrogen the risk is unlikely to increase (NICE NG23 [Guideline]).
  • Dementia. See section 6.

NICE provides a discussion aid with numbers per 1,000 people; ask your clinician to go through it with your own risk factors.

The medication content in this guide (HRT, and ADHD medication in midlife) is awaiting specialist review. If you are pregnant or planning pregnancy, medication questions are covered separately in G26, whose content is also awaiting specialist review; decisions about a particular medicine are made individually with your prescriber.

Practical tip (for clinicians): in a woman with ADHD or autism presenting with new or worsening low mood in her 40s, it is reasonable to ask about cycle changes and vasomotor symptoms, a history of premenstrual or postnatal mood problems, and current ADHD treatment, alongside a standard depression and suicide-risk assessment. Prospective daily ratings for two cycles can help separate cyclical from persistent symptoms while periods continue. Where HRT is being considered, it helps to agree in advance what it is for and what outcome would count as success, since the trial evidence covers mood prevention and vasomotor-related benefit, not ADHD symptoms.

6. HRT and memory, thinking and dementia: no clear association found, and not a prevention treatment

  • Cognition (ELITE trial). In 567 healthy postmenopausal women, oral oestradiol for a mean 57 months showed no clear effect on verbal memory (difference -0.06, 95% CI -0.22 to 0.09), executive functions or global cognition, whether started within 6 years or 10+ years after menopause (Henderson 2016) [E]. The authors conclude oestradiol neither benefits nor harms these abilities (Henderson 2016) [E]. That conclusion applies to this trial: healthy women, oral oestradiol, these cognitive tests. It does not tell us about other preparations, about women with ADHD, or about people with existing memory problems.
  • Dementia. A meta-analysis of ten studies (one RCT and nine observational; 1,016,055 participants) did not find a clear association between menopause hormone therapy and mild cognitive impairment or dementia, and did not find clear differences by timing, duration or type (Melville 2025) [S]. Certainty ranged from moderate to very low (Melville 2025) [S]. Not finding a clear association is not proof that risk is unchanged for every regimen, age or group. The authors conclude HRT should be prescribed for its other benefits and risks, not for dementia prevention (Melville 2025) [S].
  • Guidance. NICE notes that dementia risk might increase with combined HRT if it is started at 65 or over, and is unlikely to increase with oestrogen-only HRT (NICE NG23 [Guideline]).

So HRT should not be started to prevent dementia or to improve memory. It may still be the right choice for other reasons, such as hot flushes, which is a conversation for you and a clinician.

7. What hasn't been tested: HRT for ADHD symptoms

We did not find an eligible trial of HRT for ADHD symptoms in our search (last updated October 2026). This section is a "what we don't know yet".

Why people expect it might help: in healthy young women, natural oestradiol changes affected working memory, with the direction depending on baseline dopamine (Jacobs 2011) [P]. In a small daily study, lower oestrogen with higher progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the next day (Roberts 2018) [P]. A review of eleven studies called the hormone-ADHD evidence suggestive but small and mixed, and called for menopause research (Osianlis 2025) [P].

Why that isn't enough: the ELITE trial found no clear cognitive effect of oral oestradiol in healthy postmenopausal women (Henderson 2016) [E]. Mechanism studies in young women do not tell us what HRT does to ADHD in midlife. Equally, we did not find an eligible study of whether ADHD medication needs adjusting across the menopause.

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. Keep a simple log for two or three months: focus, sleep, mood, periods, hot flushes and night sweats. Patterns are easier to see on paper.
  2. Track mood daily if you still have periods, to see whether low mood is cyclical (G04).
  3. Note your history: PMDD or severe PMS, postnatal depression, past depression. These are the flags clinicians need.
  4. Separate the questions. "Is this perimenopause?", "Is my ADHD treatment still right?" and "Am I depressed?" may each need an answer.
  5. Be wary of anyone promising HRT will fix ADHD or brain fog. That claim goes beyond the evidence.
  6. If you are autistic, consider asking for appointments in the format that works best for you (for example, written questions in advance).

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Whether your symptoms fit perimenopause, depression, changes in ADHD, or a mix.
  • Your history of premenstrual, postnatal or perimenopausal mood problems.
  • What HRT would be for in your case (for example, hot flushes or sleep), what it is and isn't expected to do for mood and thinking, and your own risks (blood clots, breast cancer, stroke) given the type of HRT and your history.
  • Whether your ADHD medication or other treatment needs reviewing.
  • Sensory and communication needs in menopause care if you are autistic.

What we don't know yet

  • HRT for ADHD symptoms. We did not find an eligible trial in our search (last updated October 2026).
  • Whether menopause worsens ADHD. Two studies conflict; we did not find a study following the same women with ADHD through the transition.
  • Whether perimenopause starts earlier in ADHD. Suggested by one cohort's age pattern, not measured directly.
  • Perimenopausal depression in women with ADHD or autism. We did not find an eligible study in our search.
  • ADHD medication across menopause. We did not find a study of dosing or effectiveness changes.
  • Autistic menopause at scale. All studies so far are small, self-selected or qualitative.
  • Whether HRT's mood benefit differs in women with ADHD, autism or PMDD. We did not find a study that reports this separately.
  • HRT and dementia in specific groups. The meta-analysis did not find a clear association overall, with certainty from moderate to very low; it cannot rule out differences for particular regimens, ages or groups.

Safety and crisis resources

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

Perimenopause is a window of higher depression risk (Maki 2018) [S], and autistic women have described suicidal feelings during menopause (Moseley 2020) [P]. If your mood drops sharply, tell a clinician promptly. If you have thoughts of suicide, get help now; don't wait for them to pass or for a hormone change to settle. See crisis help.

Popular claims checked

  • You may have heard: "Perimenopause makes ADHD much worse." What the research supports: In one large cohort, women with self-reported ADHD more often had severe perimenopausal symptoms, but another survey found no clear difference. Evidence: preliminary. One cohort found more severe perimenopausal symptoms with ADHD (Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025) [P]; a newer survey found no clear difference after correction for multiple tests (Chapman 2025) [P]. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "Estrogen boosts dopamine, so ADHD gets worse whenever estrogen drops." What the research supports: ADHD symptoms may shift with hormone changes, according to small early studies. Evidence: preliminary. Plausible from small studies (Jacobs 2011; Roberts 2018) [P]; we did not find a direct test of the mechanism in ADHD. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "Menopause is always worse for women with ADHD." What the research supports: Some studies find women with ADHD report more severe perimenopausal symptoms, but results are mixed. Evidence: preliminary. The evidence is mixed (Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025; Chapman 2025) [P]. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "PMDD gets worse in perimenopause." What the research supports: On average, premenstrual symptoms did not worsen across the menopause transition, but women with PMS were more likely to report low mood and poor sleep. Evidence: preliminary. Among depressed perimenopausal women, 21% met criteria for premenstrual dysphoria vs 3% of non-depressed women (Richards 2006) [P], and depressive symptoms are more likely during the transition (Freeman 2004) [S], while premenstrual syndrome itself decreased across the transition in one cohort (Freeman 2004b) [S]. That is not the same as PMDD itself getting worse; we did not find a study following confirmed PMDD through the late transition. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "HRT will fix midlife brain fog or ADHD." What the research supports: HRT has not been shown to fix midlife brain fog or ADHD. No clear cognitive benefit in the ELITE trial of healthy postmenopausal women (Henderson 2016) [E]; we did not find a trial of HRT for ADHD.
  • You may have heard: "HRT prevents dementia." What the research supports: HRT has not been shown to prevent dementia, and it should not be started for that reason. A meta-analysis did not find a clear association, with certainty from moderate to very low, and its authors conclude HRT should not be prescribed for dementia prevention (Melville 2025) [S].
