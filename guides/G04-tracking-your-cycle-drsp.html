Tracking your cycle with the DRSP

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Memory, a blood test or a one-off quiz can't confirm PMDD. Prospective daily symptom ratings across at least two symptomatic cycles establish the cyclical pattern and support diagnostic confirmation. Established (E). You can see a clinician while you track: they can assess you and discuss care in the meantime, and urgent help never waits for a chart.

  • The DRSP is the daily chart most often used for this, and the C-PASS is a standard way to score it. They are standard tools, not the only accepted route. Adding a few ADHD lines helps a clinician see whether it's PMDD, your ADHD getting worse before your period, or both.

  • You don't need a perfect record. Sixty seconds a day, at the same time, for two cycles is enough to start a useful conversation.

Evidence grade: E (Established) for the core claim. Why ADHD lines help is Preliminary (P). · Talk to a clinician about: reading your chart with the C-PASS, and what the pattern means for treatment.

Not medical advice. Crisis resources: see the box below.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait to finish your chart, or for your period to start. See help.

1. Why daily, prospective tracking is how PMDD is confirmed

PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder) is a severe mood condition tied to the menstrual cycle. "Prospective" tracking means rating your symptoms each day as you go, rather than looking back.

Diagnosing premenstrual disorders rests on a careful history plus daily symptom monitoring across two menstrual cycles (Yonkers 2018) [E]. International experts have agreed shared rules for how premenstrual disorders should be diagnosed and measured (O'Brien 2011) [E].

Memory is the problem. In 200 women who kept daily ratings for two to four months, what women remembered about premenstrual worsening poorly predicted what their daily ratings showed (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S].

It changes the numbers too. A 2024 meta-analysis of 44 studies estimated PMDD at about 3.2% when it was confirmed (about 1.6% in community-based confirmed samples), but about 7.7% with a one-off "provisional" diagnosis, and estimates varied substantially between studies (Reilly 2024) [S]. Hormone blood tests can't diagnose PMDD either. In PMDD, hormone levels are usually normal; the brain reacts unusually to the normal rise and fall of hormones (Schmidt 1998; Schmidt 2017) [E].

Practical tip: tracking confirms the pattern; it isn't a waiting list for care. A clinician can take your history, consider a provisional diagnosis and talk about support or treatment while you track. If things feel unsafe at any point, get help straight away (help).

2. How the DRSP works

How the chart works (a description, not an evidence claim): the DRSP (Daily Record of Severity of Problems) is a one-page daily chart. Each day you rate how strongly you felt each listed premenstrual symptom, and how much your symptoms got in the way of work, study, relationships and daily life. Over a cycle, the pattern shows whether symptoms build before your period and clear after it.

A standard scoring system for PMDD, the C-PASS, was built to work from two or more months of DRSP daily ratings (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S].

Practical tip: use an official copy of the DRSP or a tracker based on it (see section 7), not a home-made list. The exact items and rating scale matter for scoring, so check them on the copy you use.

3. Adding ADHD lines

If you have ADHD, add a few lines of your own under the DRSP items. Rate them the same way each day.

Why bother? ADHD symptoms may themselves shift across the cycle. In a small study of 32 young women who gave daily saliva samples and ratings for 35 days, lower estradiol together with higher progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the next day, particularly in women high in impulsivity (Roberts 2018) [P]. These women were not selected for having ADHD. A review of 11 studies found only suggestive evidence that ADHD symptoms are linked to sex hormones (Osianlis 2025) [P].

Separately, many people find that a condition they already have gets worse before a period. This is called premenstrual exacerbation, or PME. Around 60% of women with mood disorders report it (Kuehner 2021) [S]. Without ADHD lines on your chart, PME of ADHD can be hard to see.

Practical tip: three lines are plenty. Suggested wording:

  • Concentration: "I couldn't hold my focus on things I needed to do."
  • Overwhelm: "Small tasks or demands felt like too much."
  • Task completion: "I didn't finish what I planned today."

4. Logging medication, contraception, sleep and flow

A clinician will want to know what else was changing. Logging a few extra things takes seconds and avoids guesswork later.

Practical tip: add these to each day:

  • ADHD medication: taken or not, the dose and the time. In a case series of 9 women with ADHD and premenstrual worsening, a prescriber raised the stimulant dose in the premenstrual week (de Jong 2023) [P]. If you and your prescriber ever consider something similar, a daily log is what you'd need.
  • Hormonal contraception: the type, and any missed pills or break weeks. In a Danish registry cohort of over a million women, hormonal contraception was linked to more first use of antidepressants (combined pill RR 1.23) (Skovlund 2016) [S]. This is a link, not proof of cause.
  • Sleep: roughly when you fell asleep and woke up.
  • Flow: none, light, medium or heavy. Heavy periods are a major, under-recognised cause of iron deficiency and anaemia (Munro 2023) [S]. Iron deficiency may cause fatigue and difficulty concentrating (Auerbach 2025) [E], so heavy flow is worth mentioning to your clinician. See G06.

5. Two cycles minimum: making it ADHD-proof

Two cycles is the minimum the C-PASS scoring system works from (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S]. With ADHD, the hard part isn't understanding the chart. It's remembering to fill it in for eight weeks or more.

Practical tips:

  • Anchor it. Set a daily phone alarm at a time you're usually at home, such as when you brush your teeth at night. Label the alarm "60-second chart".
  • Keep it visible. Paper by the bed or the toothbrush, or a tracker app on your home screen.
  • Rate today only. Don't try to remember yesterday's mood after the fact. A blank day is more honest than a guessed one.
  • Missed days are fine. Mark them as missed (leave them blank rather than filling them in later) and carry on. A chart with gaps is far more useful than a chart you abandon. Scoring methods have their own rules for missing days, so ask your clinician how they handle gaps.
  • Mark day 1. Note the first day of each period clearly. Everything is read from there.
  • Don't judge the chart while you're in it. Look at the pattern at the end of each cycle, not every day.

6. What a clinician does with it: C-PASS scoring, PMDD vs PME

A clinician can score your chart with the C-PASS (Carolina Premenstrual Assessment Scoring System). It is a standard tool, not the only way a clinician can read your chart against the diagnostic criteria. It standardises the DSM-5 diagnosis of PMDD from two or more months of daily ratings (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S]. In 200 women, it agreed with expert clinical diagnosis with an estimated 98% correct classification (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S].

Practical tip: your clinician will also look at whether symptoms are there all month and worsen premenstrually (which points towards PME), or appear only before your period and clear afterwards (which points towards PMDD). The difference matters, because treatments that help PMDD show less or no benefit in PME (Kuehner 2021) [S].

Practical tip: the C-PASS tells your clinician whether your ratings meet the PMDD pattern. Deciding between PMDD, PME of ADHD or mood symptoms, or both, still takes a clinician's judgement. Your ADHD lines and extra logs are what make that possible.

7. Free tools

Practical tip: these are free and widely used. We list them as resources, not as research evidence.

  • IAPMD PMDs Symptom Tracker. IAPMD (the International Association for Premenstrual Disorders) offers a free DRSP-based tracker: iapmd.org/drsp. IAPMD also has a free self-screen, which can tell you it's worth tracking but can't diagnose.
  • UNC C-PASS worksheet. The UNC Center for Women's Mood Disorders offers a free C-PASS worksheet (PDF) and Excel macro for clinicians: med.unc.edu/psych/wmd/resources. It is meant for scoring, so share it with your clinician rather than diagnosing yourself.

What you can do

Tracker how-to (step by step)

  1. Get a DRSP-based tracker. Print one, or use the IAPMD tracker.
  2. Add your extra rows. Below the DRSP items, add the three ADHD lines from section 3. Rate them the same way.
  3. Add a log strip. One small row each for medication (dose and time), contraception, sleep times and flow.
  4. Pick your time and alarm. Same time each day, ideally evening, so you're rating the whole day.
  5. Start any day. You don't need to wait for your period. Mark day 1 of each period when it arrives.
  6. Track for at least two full cycles. Three is better if your cycles are irregular or one month was unusual (illness, exams, a big life event). Note unusual events in the margin.
  7. At the end of each cycle, take a photo or export. Keep the originals.
  8. Book the appointment before you finish. Waits can be long, and you can keep tracking while you wait.
  9. Bring the full charts, not just a summary, plus a one-line note of what you're asking: "Could this be PMDD, PME of my ADHD or mood, or both?"

Talk to a clinician about…

  • How they will read your charts (for example, scoring them with the C-PASS), and what support or treatment can start while you track.
  • Whether your pattern looks like PMDD, PME of ADHD, depression or anxiety, or a mix.
  • Any change in how well your ADHD medication seems to work across the cycle, before changing a dose.
  • Heavy periods, and whether to check your iron (ferritin).
  • Mood changes since starting or changing hormonal contraception.
  • Any suicidal thoughts, especially if they come at the same point in each cycle.

Safety. In a meta-analysis of 10 studies, women with PMDD (as defined in the included studies) had higher odds of suicidal thoughts (OR 3.95) and suicide attempts (OR 6.97) than women without premenstrual disturbances (Prasad 2021) [S]. These are group-level associations: they can't predict any one person's risk, which is why it matters to ask directly and get help early. If your chart shows dark thoughts at the same point each cycle, tell a clinician, and plan for those days while you're well. If you're having suicidal thoughts now, get help now: don't wait for your chart or your period. Use the box at the top of this page or help.

What we don't know yet

  • Whether ADHD lines change the diagnosis. Adding ADHD items to the DRSP is a practical suggestion. It hasn't been tested as a method.
  • How women with ADHD use daily trackers. We did not find a study in our search (last updated October 2026) of how complete their charts are, or whether missing days bias the result.
  • The DRSP's exact item count and rating scale. We don't list them here. Its validation study reports that the DRSP gives reliable and valid measures of PMDD symptoms and impairment (Endicott 2005) [E]. Check the copy you use.
  • Whether the C-PASS itself separates PMDD from PME. The 2017 validation paper reports agreement with expert diagnosis of PMDD. Telling PMDD from PME still relies on a clinician.
  • Whether ADHD medication affects premenstrual symptoms. The only study we found is a 9-person case series (de Jong 2023) [P].

Popular claims checked

Claim What the research supports
A hormone blood test (or a one-off quiz) can tell you if you have PMDD Blood tests and one-off quizzes can't confirm PMDD; daily tracking for at least two cycles confirms the pattern, and a clinician can assess you while you track. Evidence: established.
If your ADHD, anxiety or depression gets worse before your period, that's PMDD Premenstrual worsening is often PME, which differs from PMDD; daily tracking helps tell them apart. Evidence: established.
Women with ADHD are 3x more likely to have PMDD Women with ADHD are more likely to screen positive for PMDD; this has never been confirmed with daily tracking. See G05. Evidence: preliminary.
Estrogen boosts dopamine, so ADHD gets worse whenever estrogen drops Early research links ADHD symptom shifts to falling estrogen; the mechanism hasn't been tested in ADHD. Evidence: preliminary.
Your ADHD meds stop working before your period, so increase the dose that week In a 9-person case series, a higher dose in the premenstrual week was followed by improved symptoms; a promising idea, and only with a prescriber. Evidence: preliminary.
