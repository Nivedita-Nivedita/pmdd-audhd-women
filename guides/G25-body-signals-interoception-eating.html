Body signals: interoception, eating & daily function

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • "Interoception" is how you sense signals from inside your body, like hunger, thirst, heartbeat or needing the toilet. Lab accuracy (for example, counting your heartbeat) and how aware of your body you feel are measured differently and don't always agree. A 2025 review and a 2025 adult study lean towards modestly lower interoception in ADHD, but samples are small, older studies disagree, and none measured hunger, thirst or toilet signals.

  • Eating disorders are several times more common with ADHD, especially binge eating and bulimia. In women, ADHD usually started before the eating disorder.

  • Everyday life is where ADHD shows most: questionnaires about daily functioning predict real problems better than lab tests do. Accidents and injuries are more common, but medication is linked to fewer of them.

Evidence grade: ESTABLISHED (E) for the ADHD–eating disorder association · SUPPORTED (S) for the obesity association and daily-life impairment · PRELIMINARY (P) for interoception in ADHD. Headline grade: PRELIMINARY (P). Talk to a clinician about… eating that feels out of control, missing hunger or toilet signals, and everyday tasks that keep slipping. Not medical advice. 1. What interoception is

"Interoception" means sensing what's happening inside your body: your heartbeat, breathing, hunger, fullness, thirst, temperature, needing the toilet, and the bodily side of emotions.

Researchers split it into three parts, because they don't always line up. In 80 healthy adults:

  • Accuracy (how well you actually detect a signal, such as counting your heartbeat),
  • Sensibility (how good you think you are at it), and
  • Awareness (how well your confidence matches your real accuracy)

were distinct and could come apart Garfinkel 2015 [S]. They only matched in the people with the best accuracy Garfinkel 2015 [S]. So "I'm bad at noticing hunger" and "I score badly on a heartbeat test" are different claims.

An expert consensus describes interoception as relevant to anxiety, mood, eating, addiction and physical-symptom conditions, and sets out how to research it better Khalsa 2018 [E]. It does not address ADHD directly.

2. Interoception in ADHD: what was measured, and what was found

Many women with ADHD describe forgetting to eat, drink or go to the toilet until it's urgent. The research asks a narrower question: do adults with ADHD differ on specific lab or questionnaire measures of body-signal sensing? Because the measures differ (section 1), we keep them apart below.

Heartbeat-task accuracy (a lab test). You count your heartbeats over short periods without feeling your pulse, and your count is compared with a recording.

  • In 14 adults with ADHD and 16 without, the ADHD group was less accurate at counting their heartbeat Kutscheidt 2019 [P].
  • In 24 adults with ADHD and 23 without, performance on a heartbeat perception task was strikingly similar in both groups Wiersema 2018 [P].
  • In 30 adults with ADHD and 57 without, the ADHD group had lower heartbeat-counting accuracy and lower confidence in their answers. This held after accounting for age, ethnicity, autistic traits, alexithymia, depression and social functioning Göz Tebrizcik 2025 [P].

Self-reported body awareness (questionnaires). You rate how well you think you notice body signals.

  • In the 2018 study, adults with ADHD and controls scored very similarly on an interoception questionnaire Wiersema 2018 [P].
  • In the 2025 study, adults with ADHD rated their own interoceptive accuracy lower on questionnaires than controls did Göz Tebrizcik 2025 [P].

Putting it together. A 2025 systematic review of 18 studies, which included both heartbeat-tracking tests and self-report questionnaires, found that 3 of the 5 studies comparing people with and without ADHD showed reduced interoception. In 12 general-population studies, lower interoception went with more inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity, emotional dysregulation and executive difficulties. Studies were of moderate quality, with small samples and inadequate reporting Bruton 2025 [P]. The review concluded that people with ADHD may have reduced interoception Bruton 2025 [P].

So the newer evidence leans towards a modest difference, but it rests on a handful of small studies using different measures, and the older 2018 study found no clear difference on either kind of measure. The 2025 adult study's authors urge caution because of its small sample and narrow age range Göz Tebrizcik 2025 [P].

We did not find a study, in our search (last updated October 2026), that tested whether any of these measures explains missing hunger, thirst or toilet signals in daily life, or that reported results for women with ADHD separately.

What this means for you: if you miss body signals, you're not imagining it, and it may relate to ADHD. But a lower score on a heartbeat test is not the same thing as missing hunger cues, and the link hasn't been tested. External reminders work whatever the cause.

3. Alexithymia and the autism overlap

"Alexithymia" means difficulty identifying and describing your own emotions. It's linked to body awareness because emotions are partly felt in the body.

  • About half of autistic people (49.93%) met the questionnaire threshold for alexithymia, compared with 4.89% of non-autistic people Kinnaird 2019 [E].
  • A review argues that emotional-processing difficulties in some autistic people come from co-occurring alexithymia rather than autism itself. Alexithymia is also raised in eating disorders and anxiety Bird 2013 [S].
  • A meta-analysis of five adult studies found no statistically clear difference in heartbeat-sensing accuracy between autistic and non-autistic adults. Findings for self-reported body awareness and other dimensions were mixed Klein 2025 [P].

Because ADHD and autism often occur together (see guide G13), it can be hard to tell which is behind body-signal difficulties. The adult ADHD study above found a difference even after accounting for alexithymia and autistic traits Göz Tebrizcik 2025 [P].

4. Eating: ADHD and binge eating or bulimia

This is the strongest finding in this guide. It is a separate body of evidence from section 2: the eating-disorder studies below did not measure interoception, so they can't show that body-signal differences explain the link.

  • Eating disorders are more common with ADHD. A meta-analysis of 17 studies found ADHD was linked to any eating disorder with a pooled odds ratio of 3.82, including anorexia (4.28), bulimia (5.71) and binge-eating disorder (4.13) Nazar 2016 [E]. The link was stronger when ADHD was diagnosed by clinical interview (5.89) than by self-report (2.23) Nazar 2016 [E]. It works both ways: people with eating disorders had higher odds of ADHD (OR 2.57) Nazar 2016 [E].
  • Overeating and impulsivity. Across 75 studies, there was moderate-strength evidence linking ADHD to disordered eating, especially overeating, with impulsivity consistently linked to overeating and bulimia Kaisari 2017 [S]. How the link works is still unknown.
  • ADHD comes first. In a nationally representative US survey, binge eating was linked to ADHD in both sexes. In women, bulimia and lifetime anorexia were also linked to ADHD, and ADHD always began before the eating disorder Brewerton 2016 [S].

A treatment note. In one trial of 255 adults with moderate to severe binge-eating disorder, the ADHD stimulant lisdexamfetamine at 50 and 70 mg a day, but not 30 mg, reduced binge-eating days compared with placebo McElroy 2015 [S]. Side effects were common: 84.7% on the drug vs 58.7% on placebo McElroy 2015 [S]. This was a trial in binge-eating disorder, not in ADHD. Doses here describe the study only and are not advice.

5. Weight: the obesity association, and medication

A meta-analysis of 42 studies (728,136 people) found that adults with ADHD had higher odds of obesity (OR 1.55) Cortese 2016 [E]. Obesity prevalence was 28.2% in adults with ADHD vs 16.4% without Cortese 2016 [E].

Two details matter:

  • The link was the same in women and men Cortese 2016 [E].
  • People taking ADHD medication were not at higher risk of obesity Cortese 2016 [E]. This is an association, so it doesn't prove medication prevents weight gain.

Weight is an association, not a personal prediction, and it says nothing about your worth.

6. Daily function: executive function in real life

"Executive function" means the mental skills for planning, organising, starting tasks, remembering and managing time. This is where ADHD shows up most in daily life.

  • Lab tests miss it. Across 20 studies, executive-function lab tests and everyday-life questionnaires agreed poorly (median r = .19). They seem to measure different things Toplak 2012 [E].
  • Daily-life ratings predict real outcomes. Hyperactive children followed to about age 27 showed that self-reported executive problems predicted impairment at work and in life better than lab tests did Barkley 2011 [S].
  • Everyday impact. In a US survey, 500 adults with self-reported ADHD diagnoses were less likely to be employed (52% vs 72%) and changed jobs more often than 501 adults without ADHD Biederman 2006 [S].
  • Accidents and injuries. In German insurance data on 17,041 adults with ADHD, women with ADHD had the highest risk of minor accidents and injuries at all ages Libutzki 2022 [S]. In 2,319,450 US patients with ADHD, months on ADHD medication carried a lower risk of emergency visits for car crashes, including 42% lower in women Chang 2017 [S]. A Swedish study found a similar benefit in men, but no clear association in women Chang 2014 [S].
  • Long-term health. In UK primary care data, estimated life expectancy was 8.64 years shorter for women with diagnosed ADHD, which the authors think is likely due to modifiable risk factors and unmet support needs O'Nions 2025 [S]. This is an average, not a forecast for you.
  • Autistic adults too. Autistic adults who reported more ADHD symptoms reported less independence in daily living Yerys 2022 [P].

Self-care is a gap. We did not find an eligible study of everyday self-care tasks, such as eating regularly, drinking water, personal hygiene or medical appointments, in women with ADHD in our search (last updated October 2026; see "What we don't know yet").

What you can do

  • Outsource body signals. Practical tip: set phone reminders to eat, drink and go to the toilet, especially during long focused stretches. Keep water and simple snacks where you work.
  • Make meals low-effort. Practical tip: a few default meals, batch cooking and ready-to-eat options reduce the planning load.
  • Notice binge patterns without blame. Practical tip: note time of day, how long since you last ate, and mood.
  • Use daily-life questionnaires in assessments. If your test scores look "normal", ask for questionnaires about everyday functioning too.
  • Reduce accident risk. Practical tip: take extra care when tired, rushed or distracted, especially when driving or cooking.
  • Build external structure for self-care. Practical tip: checklists by the door, recurring calendar entries for appointments and prescriptions, and one "admin hour" a week.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Eating that feels out of control, binge eating, restriction, or purging. If you have both ADHD and eating difficulties, make sure each clinician knows about both.
  • Regularly missing hunger, thirst or toilet signals.
  • Whether ADHD treatment might help with daily functioning, and how it affects your appetite.
  • Weight or metabolic health, without stigma.
  • Frequent accidents or injuries.

What we don't know yet

  • Whether adults with ADHD have reduced interoception. The evidence is small, moderate quality and partly conflicting, and heartbeat-task and questionnaire results don't always agree. We did not find a study reporting results for women specifically.
  • Whether interoception explains missed hunger, thirst or toilet signals in ADHD. We did not find a study testing this.
  • How ADHD leads to eating disorders. The association is strong, but the mechanism is unknown.
  • Whether treating ADHD reduces eating disorders. The lisdexamfetamine trial was in binge-eating disorder, not ADHD.
  • Self-care in daily life. We did not find an eligible study of everyday self-care tasks in women with ADHD.

(All "we did not find" statements refer to our search, last updated October 2026.) - Why women with ADHD have more minor injuries, and why medication's association with fewer serious accidents was clear in men but not in women in one study.

Popular claims checked

  • adhd-means-poor-interoception: "People with ADHD have poor interoception and can't feel hunger, thirst or the need to pee." Verdict: untested (as stated). Small studies, mostly using a heartbeat-counting task, lean towards modestly lower interoception in ADHD Bruton 2025 [P]; Göz Tebrizcik 2025 [P], but older studies disagreed Kutscheidt 2019 [P]; Wiersema 2018 [P]. None measured hunger, thirst or toilet signals, and "can't feel" is not what was found.
  • adhd-adults-reduced-interoception: "Adults with ADHD show reduced interoception." Verdict: partly true. Newer small studies lean that way, on heartbeat-task accuracy and, in one study, self-rated accuracy Bruton 2025 [P]; Göz Tebrizcik 2025 [P], though an earlier study found no clear difference Wiersema 2018 [P]. We did not find a study linking interoception to noticing burnout.
  • autistic-adults-6x-alexithymia: "Autistic adults are 6x more likely to have alexithymia." Verdict: partly true. The best estimate is 49.93% vs 4.89% Kinnaird 2019 [E]: about half of autistic people compared with about 1 in 20 non-autistic people. The "6x" figure does not match these percentages.
  • time-blindness-explains-lateness: "'Time blindness' explains why you're always late." Verdict: partly true. Across 55 studies, people with ADHD judged short time intervals less accurately in lab tasks Marx 2022 [S], but everyday lateness itself is rarely studied. See guide G16.
