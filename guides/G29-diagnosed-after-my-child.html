Diagnosed after my child: ADHD and autism in mothers

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • ADHD runs strongly in families, so it is common to recognise yourself while your child is being assessed. In Denmark, new ADHD diagnoses in mothers peaked 2 to 5 years after giving birth.

  • Parents' ADHD symptoms have a real but small link with harsher or less consistent parenting, similar in size to other parental mental health conditions. That is not a verdict on you as a parent.

  • In one study, mothers with more ADHD symptoms got less benefit for their child from a parenting programme. Whether treating mothers' ADHD fixes that has not been tested directly.

Evidence grade: heritability E (Established); timing and parenting meta-analysis S (Supported); autistic mothers and the treatment question P (Preliminary). Headline grade P. · Talk to a clinician about: your own assessment, and how your needs fit into your child's support plan.

Not medical advice. Crisis support →

If you are reading this because you filled in your child's questionnaire and kept thinking "this is me", you are not alone and you are not imagining it. This guide explains why, what the timing studies show, and where to go next.

A short glossary (definitions, not evidence claims):

  • Heritability: how much of the variation in a trait across a population is linked to genetic differences. It is not the chance that you will pass something on.
  • Parent training: structured programmes that teach parents specific strategies for managing children's behaviour.
  • Meta-analysis: a study that pools results from many studies.

1. Heritability: why it runs in families

Family, twin and adoption studies show ADHD runs in families, with heritability of 74% (Faraone 2018) [E]. About a third of that heritability comes from many common genetic variants, each with a small effect (Faraone 2018) [E]. There is no single "ADHD gene".

Autism and ADHD also cluster together in families. In Swedish registers covering 1,899,654 people, people with autism had much higher odds of ADHD (OR 22.33) (Ghirardi 2017) [E]. The overlap was stronger in identical twins (OR 17.77) than in non-identical twins (4.33) or full siblings (4.59), consistent with shared genes (Ghirardi 2017) [E]. So a mother of an autistic child might recognise ADHD traits in herself, or the other way round.

What this means in plain terms: when a child is diagnosed, it is common for a parent to have the same or a related condition. It does not mean you "caused" anything, and it does not mean every child of a parent with ADHD will have it.

2. Timing: new diagnoses in mothers peak 2 to 5 years after birth

A Danish study followed 363,904 mothers through 524,936 births (Madsen 2025) [S]:

  • New ADHD diagnoses fell during pregnancy (IRR 0.28).
  • They stayed below pre-pregnancy levels up to 2 years after birth (IRR 0.82).
  • They rose above them 2 to 5 years after birth (IRR 1.24).
  • Among mothers diagnosed after birth, 53.9% had first had psychiatric contact or other psychotropic prescriptions.

(Madsen 2025) [S]

The authors suggest the postpartum years may be a window when symptoms get worse (Madsen 2025) [S].

What this study does not show. It does not test whether a child's diagnosis triggered the mother's. Many children are not diagnosed by age 5, so other explanations, such as the load of early parenting, are just as possible. We found no registry study that tests the "diagnosed after my child" route directly.

This fits a wider pattern. In Stockholm, females were first diagnosed with ADHD or given medication about 4 years later than males (23.5 vs 19.6 years), with more psychiatric conditions and health-care use before diagnosis (Skoglund 2023) [S]. A review discusses why: presentation, compensating behaviours, and how diagnostic criteria and thresholds are applied (Martin 2024) [S].

3. The autistic-mother version of the story

In a small Spanish interview study, nine autistic mothers described being diagnosed after their autistic children or close relatives (Garcia-Molina 2025) [P]. Partners and children played a part in their self-acceptance, and sensory reactivity and anxiety intensified experiences from pregnancy onwards (Garcia-Molina 2025) [P].

In a larger online survey of 355 autistic and 132 non-autistic mothers, all with at least one autistic child, autistic mothers more often reported depression before or after birth, difficulty multitasking and coping with domestic responsibilities, feeling misunderstood by professionals, anxiety, and isolation (Pohl 2020) [P]. They also reported acting in their child's best interest (Pohl 2020) [P].

More broadly, in a small study of 61 autistic adults, women had a longer delay before referral and were more often misdiagnosed at first (Gesi 2021) [P]. Late-diagnosed autistic women in another small study described "pretending to be normal" and professionals missing their autism because of their gender (Bargiela 2016) [P].

4. Parenting with ADHD: real but small effects, and no blame

A meta-analysis of 32 studies found parents' ADHD symptoms accounted for 2.9% of the variation in harsh parenting, 3.2% in lax parenting and 0.5% in positive parenting (Park 2017) [S]. The effects were relatively small and similar in size to other parental mental health conditions (Park 2017) [S].

Put plainly: ADHD symptoms nudge parenting a little, on average. The vast majority of the differences between parents are explained by other things. And these are averages across many families, not a judgement on yours.

A review explains how ADHD can make day-to-day parenting harder: working memory, planning and inhibition are all things that parenting draws on constantly, as are self-regulation and motivation (Johnston 2012) [P]. It notes that few studies existed at the time (Johnston 2012) [P].

Practical tip: if you recognise this, it may help to treat parenting tasks like any other executive-function load. External reminders, routines written on the wall, fewer decisions per day and splitting tasks with another adult are not signs of failure. They are the same tools that help at work.

5. Why treating the mother may help the child

In 70 mothers of 6-10-year-old children with ADHD, higher maternal ADHD symptoms predicted less improvement in children's disruptive behaviour after a brief parenting programme (Chronis-Tuscano 2011) [P]. This worked through change in observed negative parenting (Chronis-Tuscano 2011) [P]. The authors suggest combining treatment of the parent's ADHD with parent training (Chronis-Tuscano 2011) [P].

What we don't know yet: our library has no trial showing that treating a mother's ADHD improves her child's outcomes. The idea is reasonable and comes from the study's authors, but it has not been tested in the studies we hold. This is a suggestion to raise with your child's team, not a proven route.

The pattern is also a reason to take your own needs seriously. If you are struggling to use the strategies from your child's parenting programme, that may be your own ADHD, not a lack of effort.

6. Getting assessed yourself

If you recognise yourself, the next step is the same as for any adult woman: an assessment. See G01: Getting assessed as an adult woman and G02: Preparing your assessment pack.

Women who were diagnosed with ADHD as adults described strain on wellbeing, difficult relationships and a sense of lack of control before diagnosis, and self-acceptance after it, in a review of eight qualitative studies (Attoe 2023) [P].

Practical tip: your child's assessment paperwork can be useful for your own. Notes about family history, and your own memories that came up while filling in your child's forms, are worth writing down while they are fresh.

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. Write down what you recognised while reading your child's questionnaires, with examples from your own childhood and now.
  2. Mention it to your child's clinician. Some services will note family history; some may suggest where you can be assessed.
  3. Ask your GP for an assessment referral (G01), and prepare a pack (G02).
  4. If you are on a parenting programme and finding it hard, say so. Ask whether the strategies can be adapted, for example with written summaries or reminders.
  5. Look after your mood. If depression or anxiety has been treated before without much change, ask whether ADHD or autism might be part of the picture.
  6. Be kind about the past. Recognition is not a reason for guilt; it is information.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • An assessment for yourself, for ADHD, autism or both.
  • How your own difficulties affect using parenting strategies, and whether treating them could be part of the family plan.
  • Your mood and any past treatment for depression or anxiety.
  • Communication adjustments, if you are autistic, in your child's appointments.

What we don't know yet

  • Whether a child's diagnosis triggers the mother's. No registry study tests this directly; Madsen 2025 gives timing only, and Garcia-Molina 2025 is nine interviews.
  • Whether treating maternal ADHD improves the child's outcomes. Suggested, not tested in our library.
  • Autistic mothers diagnosed after their child, at scale. Only small or survey studies.
  • Fathers and non-birthing parents. Not covered by the timing study.
  • How late diagnosis affects mothers' own wellbeing over time. Mostly qualitative evidence.

Safety and crisis resources

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

Many mothers diagnosed after birth had first been treated for other mental health problems (Madsen 2025) [S]. If your mood is low, if you feel you are failing your family, or if you have thoughts of harming yourself, tell your GP or another clinician. That is a reason to get help sooner, not to wait for an assessment.

Popular claims checked

  • Women with ADHD are often treated for anxiety or depression for years before anyone spots the ADHD. Verdict: mostly true (supported). Over half of Danish mothers diagnosed after birth had earlier psychiatric contact or other psychotropic prescriptions (Madsen 2025) [S].
  • ADHD can start in adulthood. Verdict: overstated (supported). Many mothers are first recognised in adulthood; "late recognition" is more accurate than "late onset".
  • "My ADHD will make me a bad parent." (Not yet in the claims ledger.) Verdict: unsupported. Parental ADHD symptoms explained a small share of variation in parenting (Park 2017) [S].
  • "Mothers are diagnosed because their child was." (Not yet in the claims ledger.) Verdict: plausible but untested. Timing data fit the story but do not test it (Madsen 2025) [S].
