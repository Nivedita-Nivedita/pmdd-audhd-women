Diagnosed after my child: ADHD and autism in mothers

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • ADHD runs strongly in families, so it is common to recognise yourself while your child is being assessed. In Denmark, new ADHD diagnoses in mothers peaked 2 to 5 years after giving birth.

  • Across many families, parents' ADHD symptoms have a small statistical link with harsher or less consistent parenting, similar in size to other parental mental health conditions. Group averages say nothing about your own parenting, and they don't show that a parent's ADHD causes a child's difficulties.

  • In one randomised trial, treating mothers' ADHD (group therapy plus medication) improved the mothers' own ADHD more than supportive counselling did, but children's behaviour improved about the same in both groups after parent training. Treating your own ADHD is worth doing for you; it has not been shown to add extra benefit for your child's behaviour.

Evidence grade: heritability E (Established); timing, parenting meta-analysis and the maternal-treatment trial S (Supported); autistic mothers P (Preliminary). Headline grade P. · Talk to a clinician about: your own assessment, and how your needs fit into your child's support plan.

Not medical advice. Crisis support →

If you are reading this because you filled in your child's questionnaire and kept thinking "this is me", you are not alone and you are not imagining it. This guide explains why, what the timing studies show, and where to go next.

A short glossary (definitions, not evidence claims):

  • Heritability: how much of the variation in a trait across a population is linked to genetic differences. It is not the chance that you will pass something on.
  • Parent training: structured programmes that teach parents specific strategies for managing children's behaviour.
  • Meta-analysis: a study that pools results from many studies.

1. Heritability: why it runs in families

Family, twin and adoption studies show ADHD runs in families, with heritability of 74% (Faraone 2018) [E]. About a third of that heritability comes from many common genetic variants, each with a small effect (Faraone 2018) [E]. There is no single "ADHD gene".

Autism and ADHD also cluster together in families. In Swedish registers covering 1,899,654 people, people with autism had much higher odds of ADHD (odds ratio 22.33) (Ghirardi 2017) [E]. The overlap was stronger in identical twins (odds ratio 17.77) than in non-identical twins (4.33) or full siblings (4.59), consistent with shared genes (Ghirardi 2017) [E]. So a mother of an autistic child might recognise ADHD traits in herself, or the other way round.

What this means in plain terms: when a child is diagnosed, it is common for a parent to have the same or a related condition. It does not mean you "caused" anything, and it does not mean every child of a parent with ADHD will have it.

2. Timing: new diagnoses in mothers peak 2 to 5 years after birth

A Danish study followed 363,904 mothers through 524,936 births (Madsen 2025) [S]:

  • New ADHD diagnoses fell during pregnancy (incidence rate ratio, IRR, 0.28).
  • They stayed below pre-pregnancy levels up to 2 years after birth (IRR 0.82).
  • They rose above them 2 to 5 years after birth (IRR 1.24).
  • Among mothers diagnosed after birth, 53.9% had first had psychiatric contact or other psychotropic prescriptions.

(Madsen 2025) [S]

The authors suggest the postpartum years may be a window when symptoms get worse (Madsen 2025) [S].

What this study does not show. It does not test whether a child's diagnosis triggered the mother's. Many children are not diagnosed by age 5, so other explanations, such as the load of early parenting, are just as possible. We did not find a registry study that tests the "diagnosed after my child" route directly in our search (last updated October 2026).

This fits a wider pattern. In Stockholm, females were first diagnosed with ADHD or given medication about 4 years later than males (23.5 vs 19.6 years), with more psychiatric conditions and health-care use before diagnosis (Skoglund 2023) [S]. A review discusses why: presentation, compensating behaviours, and how diagnostic criteria and thresholds are applied (Martin 2024) [S].

3. The autistic-mother version of the story

In a small Spanish interview study, nine autistic mothers described being diagnosed after their autistic children or close relatives (Garcia-Molina 2025) [P]. Partners and children played a part in their self-acceptance, and sensory reactivity and anxiety intensified experiences from pregnancy onwards (Garcia-Molina 2025) [P].

In a larger online survey of 355 autistic and 132 non-autistic mothers, all with at least one autistic child, autistic mothers more often reported depression before or after birth, difficulty multitasking and coping with domestic responsibilities, feeling misunderstood by professionals, anxiety, and isolation (Pohl 2020) [P]. They also reported acting in their child's best interest (Pohl 2020) [P].

More broadly, in a small study of 61 autistic adults, women had a longer delay before referral and were more often misdiagnosed at first (Gesi 2021) [P]. Late-diagnosed autistic women in another small study described "pretending to be normal" and professionals missing their autism because of their gender (Bargiela 2016) [P].

4. Parenting with ADHD: small average links, and no blame

A meta-analysis of 32 studies found parents' ADHD symptoms accounted for 2.9% of the variation in harsh parenting, 3.2% in lax parenting and 0.5% in positive parenting (Park 2017) [S]. The effects were relatively small and similar in size to other parental mental health conditions (Park 2017) [S].

Put plainly: on average, parents with more ADHD symptoms were a little more likely to report harsher or less consistent parenting. Most of the differences between parents are explained by other things. These are correlations across many families: they don't show that a parent's ADHD causes a child's difficulties (parents and children share genes and circumstances, and a child's behaviour also shapes parenting), and they are not a judgement on yours.

A review explains how ADHD can make day-to-day parenting harder: working memory, planning and inhibition are all things that parenting draws on constantly, as are self-regulation and motivation (Johnston 2012) [P]. It notes that few studies existed at the time (Johnston 2012) [P].

Practical tip: if you recognise this, it may help to treat parenting tasks like any other executive-function load. External reminders, routines written on the wall, fewer decisions per day and splitting tasks with another adult are not signs of failure. They are the same tools that help at work.

5. What treating a mother's ADHD has and hasn't been tested to change

The idea. In 70 mothers of 6-10-year-old children with ADHD, higher maternal ADHD symptoms predicted less improvement in children's disruptive behaviour after a brief parenting programme (Chronis-Tuscano 2011) [P]. Statistically, this link ran through change in observed negative parenting (Chronis-Tuscano 2011) [P]. The authors suggested combining treatment of the parent's ADHD with parent training (Chronis-Tuscano 2011) [P]. This is an observational finding: it shows which families benefited less, not that the mother's ADHD was the cause.

The test. A German randomised trial then tested the idea in 144 mother-child pairs where both had ADHD (Jans 2015) [S]. Mothers were randomly given either 12 weeks of intensive ADHD treatment (group psychotherapy plus methylphenidate) or supportive counselling, and then all pairs had parent-child training (Jans 2015) [S].

  • What it changed: mothers' own ADHD improved more with the intensive treatment (Jans 2015) [S].
  • What it didn't clearly change: after 6 months, no clear difference was found between groups in improvement in children's externalising behaviour (such as defiance or aggression); children in both groups improved (Jans 2015) [S].
  • Harms: no severe treatment-related harms were found, but side effects (adverse events) were more frequent in mothers who had the intensive treatment (Jans 2015) [S].

What this means. Treating your own ADHD has been tested and helped mothers' own symptoms in this trial. It has not been shown to add extra benefit for children's behaviour beyond what parent training already gives. One trial in one country cannot rule out benefits for other outcomes (for example family stress, the mother's mood, or younger children), which we did not find tested in our search (last updated October 2026). ADHD medication has side effects, so decisions about it are made with a prescriber.

If you are struggling to use the strategies from your child's parenting programme, that may be your own ADHD, not a lack of effort, and it is a good reason to seek help for yourself.

6. Getting assessed yourself

If you recognise yourself, the next step is the same as for any adult woman: an assessment. See G01: Getting assessed as an adult woman and G02: Preparing your assessment pack.

Women who were diagnosed with ADHD as adults described strain on wellbeing, difficult relationships and a sense of lack of control before diagnosis, and self-acceptance after it, in a review of eight qualitative studies (Attoe 2023) [P].

Practical tip: your child's assessment paperwork can be useful for your own. Notes about family history, and your own memories that came up while filling in your child's forms, are worth writing down while they are fresh.

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. Write down what you recognised while reading your child's questionnaires, with examples from your own childhood and now.
  2. Mention it to your child's clinician. Some services will note family history; some may suggest where you can be assessed.
  3. Ask your GP for an assessment referral (G01), and prepare a pack (G02).
  4. If you are on a parenting programme and finding it hard, say so. Ask whether the strategies can be adapted, for example with written summaries or reminders.
  5. Look after your mood. If depression or anxiety has been treated before without much change, ask whether ADHD or autism might be part of the picture.
  6. Be kind about the past. Recognition is not a reason for guilt; it is information.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • An assessment for yourself, for ADHD, autism or both.
  • How your own difficulties affect using parenting strategies, and whether treating them could be part of the family plan.
  • Your mood and any past treatment for depression or anxiety.
  • Communication adjustments, if you are autistic, in your child's appointments.

What we don't know yet

  • Whether a child's diagnosis triggers the mother's. We did not find a registry study that tests this directly; Madsen 2025 gives timing only, and Garcia-Molina 2025 is nine interviews.
  • Whether treating maternal ADHD improves the child's outcomes. One randomised trial found no clear added benefit for children's behaviour over supportive counselling when both groups had parent training (Jans 2015) [S]. Other child and family outcomes, and other settings, have not been tested in the studies we found.
  • Autistic mothers diagnosed after their child, at scale. We found only small or survey studies.
  • Fathers and non-birthing parents. Not covered by the timing study.
  • How late diagnosis affects mothers' own wellbeing over time. Mostly qualitative evidence.

Safety and crisis resources

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

Many mothers diagnosed after birth had first been treated for other mental health problems (Madsen 2025) [S]. If your mood is low or you feel you are failing your family, tell your GP or another clinician; that is a reason to get help sooner, not to wait for an assessment. If you have thoughts of suicide or of harming yourself, get help now rather than waiting for them to pass: see crisis help.

Popular claims checked

  • You may have heard: "Women with ADHD are often treated for anxiety or depression for years before anyone spots the ADHD." What the research supports: In a Danish registry cohort, over half (53.9%) of mothers first diagnosed with ADHD after giving birth had earlier psychiatric contact or other psychotropic prescriptions (Madsen 2025) [S]. Evidence on adults more widely, with its own sources and grade, is in Claim check. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "ADHD can start in adulthood." What the research supports: ADHD is often first recognised in adulthood, but the research does not support it starting then. Many mothers are first recognised in adulthood. In 239 people without childhood ADHD, assessed 8 times from a mean age of 9.89 to 24.40 years, there was no evidence for adult-onset ADHD independent of a complex psychiatric history (Sibley 2018) [S]. "Late recognition" is usually more accurate than "late onset".
  • You may have heard: "My ADHD will make me a bad parent." What the research supports: Parental ADHD symptoms have only a small link with parenting across families. Parental ADHD symptoms explained a small share of variation in parenting across families (Park 2017) [S].
  • You may have heard: "If I treat my ADHD, my child's behaviour will improve." What the research supports: Treating a mother's ADHD has not yet been shown to improve her child's behaviour. In one randomised trial, treating mothers' ADHD improved their own symptoms but did not clearly add to children's behavioural improvement after parent training (Jans 2015) [S].
  • You may have heard: "Mothers are diagnosed because their child was." What the research supports: Mothers are often diagnosed soon after their child, but the studies we found have not tested whether one leads to the other. Timing data fit the story but do not test it (Madsen 2025) [S].
