In short

  • PMDD requires prospective daily ratings over ≥2 cycles; retrospective report predicts prospective diagnosis poorly. In ADHD, the key differential is PMDD vs premenstrual exacerbation (PME) of ADHD, depression or anxiety. E

  • The ADHD × PMDD association replicates in direction across survey, occupational and genetic studies (S), but every estimate rests on retrospective screens (P for size). Registry data link hormonal contraception to depression, with the highest risk in young women with ADHD (S).

  • Cycle-related change in ADHD symptoms and premenstrual stimulant adjustment rest on small studies and a 9-patient case series (P). Screen for suicidality, and do it with the cycle in mind.

Evidence grade: E / S / P by section. Headline P (Preliminary), the weakest essential claim. · Talk to a clinician about… is written here as patient-facing prompts you can share.

1. PMDD vs PME: why C-PASS matters in ADHD

PMDD diagnosis rests on history plus daily symptom monitoring across 2 cycles (Yonkers 2018) [E], consistent with the ISPMD consensus on diagnostic criteria and measurement (O'Brien 2011) [E]. The DSM-5 work group moved PMDD to a full category, citing 2–5% of premenopausal women affected (Epperson 2012) [E].

Method drives prevalence. Across 44 studies (50,659 participants), pooled PMDD point prevalence was 3.2% (95% CI 1.7–5.9) with confirmed diagnosis vs 7.7% (5.3–11.0) with provisional diagnosis, and 1.6% (1.0–2.5) in community samples with confirmed diagnosis (Reilly 2024) [S].

The C-PASS standardises DSM-5 PMDD diagnosis from ≥2 months of DRSP ratings. In 200 women it agreed with expert diagnosis with an estimated 98% correct classification, and retrospective reports of premenstrual worsening poorly predicted prospective diagnosis (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S].

PME is the main differential. Around 60% of women with mood disorders report PME; it predicts a more severe course, and PMDD treatments show less or no efficacy in PME, from sparse and mostly small studies (Kuehner 2021) [S]. Perimenstrual worsening can be marked without PMDD. In 28 outpatients with suicidal ideation, 93% had peak SI perimenstrually, yet none met full PMDD criteria (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022) [P].

Practical tip: in ADHD, ask for DRSP ratings plus 2–3 patient-chosen ADHD items (e.g. concentration, overwhelm, task completion) on the same daily scale. The C-PASS establishes whether the PMDD pattern is met. Ruling out exacerbation of another disorder (DSM-5 Criterion E) is a clinical judgement, and the ADHD rows make it possible. Patient-facing instructions are in G04.

Reading the table. Direction is consistent across designs, which supports the association (Broughton 2025; Tsuji 2026; Groenman 2026; Jaholkowski 2023) [S]. Size is not established. No study used prospective, C-PASS-confirmed PMDD, and provisional diagnosis inflates prevalence (Reilly 2024) [S]. The comorbidity gradient in Broughton is compatible with PME confounding (Broughton 2025; Kuehner 2021) [P]. The autism signal is inconsistent: Tsuji found no association with autism traits (Tsuji 2026) [P], and an earlier community study found PMDD not significantly raised in autistic women (14.3% vs 9.5%) (Groenman 2022) [P].

Suggested wording for patients: "Several studies find that women with ADHD are considerably more likely to screen positive for PMDD. No study has yet confirmed this with the daily symptom tracking needed for a formal diagnosis." (The "3x" figure in circulation is a single-study ratio from a provisional screen: see G05.)

3. Hormonal effects on ADHD symptoms, and the estrogen–dopamine hypothesis

PMDD mechanism. PMDD reflects differential sensitivity to normal hormonal change, not abnormal levels (Schmidt 1998; Schmidt 2017) [E], so hormone assays have no diagnostic role. In 12 women whose PMDD remitted on leuprolide, symptoms rose in the first month of continuous estradiol/progesterone add-back but not in months 2–3 (Schmidt 2017) [P].

ADHD symptoms across the cycle. In 32 regularly cycling young women with 35 days of daily saliva and ratings, lower estradiol with higher progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the next day, particularly with high trait impulsivity. Phase analyses suggested increases in the early follicular and early luteal phases (Roberts 2018) [P]. This was not an ADHD sample. A systematic review of 11 studies found largely suggestive evidence linking ADHD symptoms to sex hormones, mainly in puberty and across the cycle, from few, small, heterogeneous studies (Osianlis 2025) [P]. An expert review concludes that hormonal transitions exacerbate ADHD symptoms and mood, and that pharmacological research and tailored treatments are lacking (Kooij 2025) [P].

Estrogen–dopamine. In healthy women, natural estradiol fluctuation affected working memory, with direction depending on baseline prefrontal dopamine (indexed by COMT Val158Met genotype) (Jacobs 2011) [P]. This is a plausible framework for inter-individual variation in cycle effects, but it has not been tested in ADHD. Treat it as a hypothesis when explaining symptoms to patients.

4. Hormonal contraception and depression risk in ADHD

  • Population data. In 1,061,997 Danish women aged 15–34, hormonal contraception was associated with first antidepressant use: combined pill RR 1.23, progestogen-only pill 1.34, patch 2.0, vaginal ring 1.6, LNG-IUS 1.4; adolescents 15–19 on the combined pill RR 1.8 (Skovlund 2016) [S].
  • Suicidality. In nearly half a million Danish women, current or recent use was associated with first suicide attempt (HR 1.97, 1.85–2.10) and suicide (HR 3.08, 1.34–7.08) vs never-use. The association with attempts peaked after 2 months of use, and adolescents had the highest relative risk (Skovlund 2018) [S].
  • ADHD-specific. In Swedish registers (29,767 women with ADHD and 763,146 without, aged 15–24), ADHD carried a 3-fold higher depression risk irrespective of contraception (aHR 3.69). Combined oral contraceptive users with ADHD had aHR 5.19 vs non-users without ADHD, and 6.10 vs combined-pill users without ADHD. Progestogen-only pill users with ADHD had aHR 5.00. Non-oral methods were similarly moderately raised in both groups (Lundin 2023) [S].

These are observational associations, not causal estimates. The relevant counterweight for PMDD is that contraceptives with a shortened or no hormone-free interval are among the most efficacious PMS/PMDD treatments (Yonkers 2018) [S].

Practical tip: in young women with ADHD, discuss mood monitoring when starting oral contraception, with particular attention to the first months. If mood changes, consider the method in the differential before attributing it to ADHD or PMDD. Patient-facing detail is planned in G23.

5. Premenstrual stimulant adjustment: case-series only

In 9 consecutive community patients with ADHD and premenstrual worsening, raising the psychostimulant dose in the premenstrual week improved inattention, irritability and energy, with minimal adverse events over 6–24 months, and all chose to continue (de Jong 2023) [P].

There is no controlled trial. The library contains no data on whether stimulants worsen, improve or don't affect PMDD, and no trials of SSRIs, contraceptives or GnRH analogues in ADHD plus PMDD.

Practical tip: if you consider it, establish the pattern first with ≥2 cycles of daily ratings that include ADHD items and dose timing. Set a defined premenstrual window and a review point, and document the rationale as off-evidence, individualised care.

PMDD treatment evidence (non-ADHD populations). SSRIs beat placebo across 31 RCTs (SMD −0.65 from end scores, low-quality evidence; −0.36 from change scores, moderate-quality), with no clear difference between luteal-only and continuous dosing; nausea NNH 7, low energy NNH 9 (Marjoribanks 2013) [E]. GnRH analogues outperform placebo (SMD −1.19), and add-back does not reduce efficacy (Wyatt 2004) [S]. GnRH agonists are positioned for non-responders (Yonkers 2018) [S]. Interaction data for SSRI plus stimulant in this indication were not found in the library.

6. Suicidality screening across the cycle

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

  • PMDD. Across 10 meta-analysed studies, PMDD was associated with suicide attempt (OR 6.97, 2.98–16.29) and ideation (OR 3.95, 2.97–5.24) (Prasad 2021) [S]. In Swedish registers, 67,748 women with clinically diagnosed premenstrual disorders had no excess all-cause mortality (aHR 0.91) but higher suicide (HR 1.92, 1.43–2.60), and diagnosis before 25 carried suicide HR 3.84 (Opatowski 2024) [S].
  • ADHD. A meta-analysis of 57 studies associated ADHD with suicide attempts (OR 2.37), ideation (3.53), plans (4.54) and completed suicide (6.69), with high heterogeneity (Septier 2019) [E]. Girls with ADHD followed to ages 17–24 had higher rates of suicide attempts and self-injury, concentrated in combined type (Hinshaw 2012) [S].
  • Hormone withdrawal. In a crossover RCT (28 analysed), buffering perimenstrual hormone withdrawal with transdermal estradiol plus oral progesterone reduced perimenstrual suicidal ideation, planning, hopelessness and rejection sensitivity vs placebo (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022) [P].
  • Contraception. The association between hormonal contraception and first suicide attempt peaked after 2 months of use (Skovlund 2018) [S].

No study in the library examines suicidality in ADHD plus PMDD specifically.

Practical tip: ask directly about suicidal thoughts, and ask when in the cycle they occur. A single assessment in the follicular phase can miss luteal-phase risk. Where ratings show a cyclical pattern, time safety planning and follow-up to the high-risk window.

What to do in practice

  1. Don't diagnose PMDD from history or a PSST alone. Use ≥2 cycles of DRSP ratings, scored with the C-PASS.
  2. Add ADHD rows to the DRSP, plus logs of stimulant dose and timing, contraception, sleep and flow.
  3. Make the PMDD vs PME call clinically. Look at all-month symptoms that worsen premenstrually vs luteal-only symptoms that clear after menses. Both can coexist.
  4. Treat PMDD on existing evidence (SSRIs, continuous or short-interval contraception, GnRH for non-responders), and tell patients these were not tested in ADHD.
  5. Treat premenstrual stimulant adjustment as individualised and off-evidence. Agree a window and review.
  6. Ask about mood when starting hormonal contraception in young women with ADHD, especially in the first months.
  7. Screen for suicidality with cycle timing in mind. Plan for the luteal window.
  8. Check ferritin when flow is heavy. Heavy menstrual bleeding is a major, under-recognised cause of iron deficiency (Munro 2023) [S].

Talk to a clinician about…

Prompts patients can bring (share with them, or use as a checklist in consultation):

  • "Can we score my daily charts with the C-PASS?"
  • "Is this PMDD, my ADHD or mood getting worse before my period, or both?"
  • "My medication seems weaker before my period. Can we look at that together before I change anything?"
  • "Could my contraception be affecting my mood?"
  • "My dark thoughts come at the same time each cycle. Can we plan for that window?"
  • "My periods are heavy. Should my ferritin be checked?"

What we don't know yet

  • The size of the ADHD × PMDD association under prospective, C-PASS-confirmed diagnosis.
  • Differential recall in ADHD. Whether retrospective premenstrual reporting is biased differently in ADHD.
  • The PMDD vs PME split in ADHD. How much screen-positive "PMDD" is PME of ADHD, depression or anxiety.
  • Cycle effects in ADHD samples. The best daily-hormone study was not in women with ADHD.
  • The estrogen–dopamine mechanism in ADHD. It is untested.
  • Treatment in ADHD plus PMDD. There are no trials of SSRIs, contraceptives or GnRH analogues in this group, no RCT of premenstrual stimulant adjustment, and no data on stimulant effects on PMDD.
  • Suicidality in ADHD plus PMDD. There are no specific data.
  • Contraception counselling. Whether choosing a non-oral method changes depression outcomes in ADHD has not been tested prospectively.
