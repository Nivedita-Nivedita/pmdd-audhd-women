For clinicians: ADHD, the menstrual cycle & PME

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • PMDD is confirmed with prospective daily ratings over ≥2 cycles; retrospective report predicts prospective diagnosis poorly. Provisional assessment and initial care can proceed while the patient tracks, and acute risk is managed immediately. In ADHD, the key differential is PMDD vs premenstrual exacerbation (PME) of ADHD, depression or anxiety. E

  • The ADHD × PMDD association replicates in direction across survey, occupational and genetic studies (S), but every estimate rests on retrospective screens (P for size). Registry data link hormonal contraception to depression, with the highest risk in young women with ADHD (S).

  • Cycle-related change in ADHD symptoms and premenstrual stimulant adjustment rest on small studies and a 9-patient case series (P). Screen for suicidality, and do it with the cycle in mind.

Evidence grade: E / S / P by section. Headline P (Preliminary), the weakest essential claim. · Talk to a clinician about… is written here as patient-facing prompts you can share.

1. PMDD vs PME: why C-PASS matters in ADHD

PMDD diagnosis rests on history plus daily symptom monitoring across 2 cycles (Yonkers 2018) [E], consistent with the ISPMD consensus on diagnostic criteria and measurement (O'Brien 2011) [E]. The DSM-5 work group moved PMDD to a full category, citing 2–5% of premenopausal women affected (Epperson 2012) [E].

Method drives prevalence. Across 44 studies (50,659 participants), pooled PMDD point prevalence was 3.2% (95% CI 1.7–5.9) with confirmed diagnosis vs 7.7% (5.3–11.0) with provisional diagnosis, and 1.6% (1.0–2.5) in community samples with confirmed diagnosis (Reilly 2024) [S].

The C-PASS standardises DSM-5 PMDD diagnosis from ≥2 months of DRSP ratings; DRSP with C-PASS is a standard approach rather than the only accepted route to confirmation. In 200 women it agreed with expert diagnosis with an estimated 98% correct classification, and retrospective reports of premenstrual worsening poorly predicted prospective diagnosis (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S].

PME is the main differential. Around 60% of women with mood disorders report PME; it predicts a more severe course, and PMDD treatments show less or no efficacy in PME, from sparse and mostly small studies (Kuehner 2021) [S]. Perimenstrual worsening can be marked without PMDD. In 28 outpatients with suicidal ideation, 93% had peak SI perimenstrually, yet none met full PMDD criteria (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022) [P].

Practical tip: in ADHD, ask for DRSP ratings plus 2–3 patient-chosen ADHD items (e.g. concentration, overwhelm, task completion) on the same daily scale. The C-PASS establishes whether the PMDD pattern is met. Ruling out exacerbation of another disorder (DSM-5 Criterion E) is a clinical judgement, and the ADHD rows make it possible. Patient-facing instructions are in G04.

Reading the table. Direction is consistent across designs, which supports the association (Broughton 2025; Tsuji 2026; Groenman 2026; Jaholkowski 2023) [S]. Size is not established. The estimates use different measures (crude prevalence comparison, risk ratio, adjusted odds ratio) in different populations, so they should not be pooled into a single "2–6×" range. We did not find a study using prospective, C-PASS-confirmed PMDD in our search (last updated October 2026), and provisional diagnosis inflates prevalence (Reilly 2024) [S]. The comorbidity gradient in Broughton is compatible with PME confounding (Broughton 2025; Kuehner 2021) [P]. The autism signal is inconsistent: Tsuji found no association with autism traits (Tsuji 2026) [P], and an earlier community study found PMDD not significantly raised in autistic women (14.3% vs 9.5%) (Groenman 2022) [P].

Suggested wording for patients: "Several studies find that women with ADHD are considerably more likely to screen positive for PMDD. We did not find a study in our search (last updated October 2026) that has confirmed this with the daily symptom tracking needed for a formal diagnosis." (The "3x" figure in circulation is an unadjusted ratio of provisional-PMDD percentages from one online sample: see G05.)

3. Hormonal effects on ADHD symptoms, and the estrogen–dopamine hypothesis

PMDD mechanism. PMDD reflects differential sensitivity to normal hormonal change, not abnormal levels (Schmidt 1998; Schmidt 2017) [E], so hormone assays have no diagnostic role. In 12 women whose PMDD remitted on leuprolide, symptoms rose in the first month of continuous estradiol/progesterone add-back but not in months 2–3 (Schmidt 2017) [P].

ADHD symptoms across the cycle. In 32 regularly cycling young women with 35 days of daily saliva and ratings, lower estradiol with higher progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the next day, particularly with high trait impulsivity. Phase analyses suggested increases in the early follicular and early luteal phases (Roberts 2018) [P]. This was not an ADHD sample. A systematic review of 11 studies found largely suggestive evidence linking ADHD symptoms to sex hormones, mainly in puberty and across the cycle, from few, small, heterogeneous studies (Osianlis 2025) [P]. An expert review concludes that hormonal transitions exacerbate ADHD symptoms and mood, and that pharmacological research and tailored treatments are lacking (Kooij 2025) [P].

Estrogen–dopamine. In healthy women, natural estradiol fluctuation affected working memory, with direction depending on baseline prefrontal dopamine (indexed by COMT Val158Met genotype) (Jacobs 2011) [P]. This is a plausible framework for inter-individual variation in cycle effects, but it has not been tested in ADHD. Treat it as a hypothesis when explaining symptoms to patients.

4. Hormonal contraception and depression risk in ADHD

  • Population data. In 1,061,997 Danish women aged 15–34, hormonal contraception was associated with first antidepressant use: combined pill RR 1.23, progestogen-only pill 1.34, patch 2.0, vaginal ring 1.6, LNG-IUS 1.4; adolescents 15–19 on the combined pill RR 1.8 (Skovlund 2016) [S].
  • Suicidality. In nearly half a million Danish women, current or recent use was associated with first suicide attempt (HR 1.97, 1.85–2.10) and suicide (HR 3.08, 1.34–7.08) vs never-use. The association with attempts peaked after 2 months of use, and adolescents had the highest relative risk (Skovlund 2018) [S].
  • ADHD-specific. In Swedish registers (29,767 women with ADHD and 763,146 without, aged 15–24), ADHD carried a 3-fold higher depression risk irrespective of contraception (aHR 3.69). Combined oral contraceptive users with ADHD had aHR 5.19 vs non-users without ADHD, and 6.10 vs combined-pill users without ADHD. Progestogen-only pill users with ADHD had aHR 5.00. Non-oral methods were similarly moderately raised in both groups (Lundin 2023) [S].

These are observational associations, not causal estimates. The relevant counterweight for PMDD is that contraceptives with a shortened or no hormone-free interval are among the most efficacious PMS/PMDD treatments (Yonkers 2018) [S].

Practical tip: in young women with ADHD, discuss mood monitoring when starting oral contraception, with particular attention to the first months. If mood changes, consider the method in the differential before attributing it to ADHD or PMDD. Patient-facing detail is in G23.

5. Premenstrual stimulant adjustment: case-series only

In 9 consecutive patients at one adult ADHD clinic, treated for ADHD with co-occurring conditions (including depression and PMDD) and reporting premenstrual worsening or insufficient effect of their established dose, raising the psychostimulant dose in the premenstrual week improved inattention, irritability and energy, with minimal adverse events over 6–24 months, and all chose to continue (de Jong 2023) [P].

We did not find a controlled trial in our search (last updated October 2026), nor data on whether stimulants worsen, improve or don't affect PMDD, nor trials of SSRIs, contraceptives or GnRH analogues in ADHD plus PMDD. The case series reports observed symptom change with dose adjustment, uncontrolled and unblinded; it did not measure drug efficacy or levels across the cycle, so reported premenstrual worsening should not be equated with measured loss of stimulant efficacy. It also did not test a hormonal mechanism, and the estrogen–dopamine account in §3 remains a hypothesis.

Practical tip: any change is prescriber-led. If you consider it, establish the pattern first with ≥2 cycles of daily ratings that include ADHD items and dose timing. Set a defined premenstrual window and a review point, and document the rationale as off-evidence, individualised care.

PMDD treatment evidence (non-ADHD populations). The 2024 Cochrane update (34 RCTs) found SSRIs probably reduce overall self-rated premenstrual symptoms (SMD −0.57, 95% CI −0.72 to −0.42; moderate certainty) (Jespersen 2024) [E]. Both schedules beat placebo (continuous SMD −0.69; luteal SMD −0.39; moderate certainty), and the review concluded continuous administration is probably more effective (P = 0.03 for subgroup difference) (Jespersen 2024) [S]. This is a between-subgroup comparison of placebo-controlled trials, so it informs the average rather than dictating the schedule for an individual; scheduling is decided with the patient. It supersedes the 2013 version, which found no clear schedule difference (Marjoribanks 2013) [S]. Common adverse effects: nausea (OR 3.30), asthenia (OR 3.28), somnolence (OR 3.26, low certainty) (Jespersen 2024) [E]. Drospirenone-containing COCs may improve premenstrual symptoms and functional impairment (SMD −0.41; low-quality evidence) with more adverse effects (OR 2.31) and withdrawals (OR 3.41) (Ma 2023) [S]. GnRH analogues outperform placebo (SMD −1.19), and add-back does not reduce efficacy (Wyatt 2004) [S]. GnRH agonists are positioned for non-responders (Yonkers 2018) [S]. ACOG's 2023 guideline addresses hormonal and non-hormonal pharmacotherapy, psychological, complementary, lifestyle and surgical options, and notes many patients may benefit from a multimodal approach ACOG Clinical Practice Guideline No. 7, 2023 [Guideline]. We did not find interaction data for SSRI plus stimulant in this indication in our search.

Harms and eligibility (official sources). Combined hormonal contraception needs eligibility screening: NHS information lists age ≥35 with smoking, obesity, previous VTE (or a first-degree relative with VTE under 45), stroke or arterial disease, heart disease or hypertension, migraine with aura, breast cancer and some hepatic, biliary or diabetic complications as reasons it may be unsuitable (NHS: who can take the combined pill) [Reference], and gives VTE risk as up to 1 in 1,000 users (NHS: combined pill risks) [Reference]. Antidepressant information notes uncommon emergent suicidal thoughts or self-harm urges at initiation, with people aged 24 or under or with previous such thoughts possibly most at risk (NHS: antidepressants) [Reference]. Leuprolide information lists hot flashes, new or worsening depression and decreased bone density (MedlinePlus: leuprolide injection) [Reference]. Bilateral oophorectomy causes immediate surgical menopause (NHS: hysterectomy considerations) [Reference]. High-dose pyridoxine supplements can cause peripheral neuropathy (NHS: vitamin B) [Reference]. Detail for patients is in G39.

6. Suicidality screening across the cycle

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait for your period to start or for the feelings to ease. See help.

  • PMDD. In a systematic review (13 studies, 10 meta-analysed), PMDD vs no premenstrual disturbance was associated with suicide attempt (OR 6.97, 2.98–16.29) and ideation (OR 3.95, 2.97–5.24); the authors support routine suicide-risk assessment in moderate-to-severe premenstrual disturbance (Prasad 2021) [S]. In a Swedish matched cohort (67,748 women with premenstrual disorders identified from diagnoses and drug dispensing vs 338,740 matched women; mean follow-up 6.2 years), there was no excess all-cause mortality (aHR 0.91, 0.82–1.02) but higher suicide mortality (HR 1.92, 1.43–2.60); diagnosis before 25 carried suicide HR 3.84 (1.18–12.45) (Opatowski 2024) [S]. These are population associations, not individual risk prediction.
  • ADHD. A meta-analysis of 57 studies associated ADHD with suicide attempts (OR 2.37), ideation (3.53), plans (4.54) and completed suicide (6.69), with high heterogeneity (Septier 2019) [E]. Girls with ADHD followed to ages 17–24 had higher rates of suicide attempts and self-injury, concentrated in combined type (Hinshaw 2012) [S].
  • Hormone withdrawal. In a crossover RCT (28 analysed), buffering perimenstrual hormone withdrawal with transdermal estradiol plus oral progesterone reduced perimenstrual suicidal ideation, planning, hopelessness and rejection sensitivity vs placebo (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022) [P].
  • Contraception. The association between hormonal contraception and first suicide attempt peaked after 2 months of use (Skovlund 2018) [S].

We did not find a study of suicidality in ADHD plus PMDD specifically in our search.

Practical tip: ask directly about suicidal thoughts, and ask when in the cycle they occur. Current risk is managed now, not deferred until a cyclical pattern is confirmed. A single assessment in the follicular phase can miss luteal-phase risk. Where ratings show a cyclical pattern, time safety planning and follow-up to the high-risk window.

What to do in practice

  1. Confirm PMDD prospectively. Use ≥2 cycles of daily ratings; DRSP scored with the C-PASS is the standard approach. A history or PSST supports a provisional assessment, and initial care can start while the patient tracks. Don't delay management of acute risk.
  2. Add ADHD rows to the DRSP, plus logs of stimulant dose and timing, contraception, sleep and flow.
  3. Make the PMDD vs PME call clinically. Look at all-month symptoms that worsen premenstrually vs luteal-only symptoms that clear after menses. Both can coexist.
  4. Treat PMDD on existing evidence (SSRIs, continuous or luteal, with schedule individualised; drospirenone-containing or continuous/short-interval contraception after eligibility screening; GnRH with add-back for non-responders, with bone monitoring), and tell patients these were not tested in ADHD.
  5. Treat premenstrual stimulant adjustment as individualised and off-evidence. Agree a window and review.
  6. Ask about mood when starting hormonal contraception in young women with ADHD, especially in the first months.
  7. Screen for suicidality with cycle timing in mind. Plan for the luteal window.
  8. Check ferritin when flow is heavy. Heavy menstrual bleeding is a major, under-recognised cause of iron deficiency (Munro 2023) [S].

Talk to a clinician about…

Prompts patients can bring (share with them, or use as a checklist in consultation):

  • "Can we score my daily charts with the C-PASS?"
  • "Is this PMDD, my ADHD or mood getting worse before my period, or both?"
  • "My medication seems weaker before my period. Can we look at that together before I change anything?"
  • "Could my contraception be affecting my mood?"
  • "My dark thoughts come at the same time each cycle. Can we plan for that window?"
  • "My periods are heavy. Should my ferritin be checked?"

What we don't know yet

  • The size of the ADHD × PMDD association under prospective, C-PASS-confirmed diagnosis.
  • Differential recall in ADHD. Whether retrospective premenstrual reporting is biased differently in ADHD.
  • The PMDD vs PME split in ADHD. How much screen-positive "PMDD" is PME of ADHD, depression or anxiety.
  • Cycle effects in ADHD samples. The best daily-hormone study was not in women with ADHD.
  • The estrogen–dopamine mechanism in ADHD. It is untested.
  • Treatment in ADHD plus PMDD. We did not find trials of SSRIs, contraceptives or GnRH analogues in this group, an RCT of premenstrual stimulant adjustment, or data on stimulant effects on PMDD.
  • Suicidality in ADHD plus PMDD. We did not find specific data.
  • Contraception counselling. Whether choosing a non-oral method changes depression outcomes in ADHD has not been tested prospectively.
