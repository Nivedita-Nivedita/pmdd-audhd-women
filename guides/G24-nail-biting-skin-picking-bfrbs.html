Nail biting, skin picking & other BFRBs

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Nail biting, skin picking and hair pulling are "body-focused repetitive behaviours" (BFRBs). They may work as a way of managing boredom, tension or difficult feelings.

  • They seem common with ADHD, but the evidence is mostly from children in clinics. Adult data are thin, and we did not find a study of nail biting in adult women with ADHD.

  • Habit reversal therapy has the best evidence, with large effects. The supplement NAC was tested separately for each behaviour: it helped in one trial in adults with hair-pulling disorder and one trial in adults with skin-picking disorder Grant 2016 [P]; for nail biting the only trial was small, short-lived and in children. None of these trials was in people with ADHD.

Evidence grade: ESTABLISHED (E) that habit reversal works · PRELIMINARY (P) for the ADHD link in adults and for NAC · POPULAR BUT UNSUPPORTED (U) that stimulants make BFRBs worse. Headline grade: PRELIMINARY (P). Talk to a clinician about… picking or biting that causes pain, infection, scarring or distress, and a referral for habit reversal therapy. Not medical advice. 1. BFRBs as emotion and arousal regulation

"Body-focused repetitive behaviours" (BFRBs) are repeated actions aimed at your own body: nail biting, skin picking, hair pulling, cheek biting and similar habits. They become a problem when you can't stop, and they cause damage or distress.

One leading explanation is the emotion-regulation model. A review of comorbidity, everyday-life, experimental and treatment studies examined the idea that hair pulling, skin picking and nail biting help people manage uncomfortable feelings Roberts 2013 [S]. The same review also covers other models, including cognitive-behavioural ones Roberts 2013 [S]. In plain terms: picking or biting may soothe tension, relieve boredom or give the brain some stimulation.

Skin picking is not rare. A clinical review gives a community prevalence for skin picking disorder of 1.4% to 5.4% Grant 2012 [S].

What this means for you: if you pick or bite more when you're bored, stressed or concentrating, that fits the main model. It isn't a lack of willpower.

2. ADHD links: mainly child and clinic data

You'll see claims online that nail biting and skin picking are "ADHD traits". The research is thinner than that suggests.

  • Children in a clinic. Among 63 children referred to a mental health clinic who bit their nails, 74.6% had ADHD Ghanizadeh 2008 [P]. That is a striking figure, but it comes from children who were already referred for psychiatric care, not from the general population. How severe or frequent the nail biting was did not depend on which other conditions the children had Ghanizadeh 2008 [P].
  • Adults with hair pulling. Among 308 adults with trichotillomania (hair-pulling disorder), 15.3% met the threshold for ADHD Chesivoir 2022 [P]. They were more impulsive, but having ADHD did not change how severe the hair pulling was, or quality of life Chesivoir 2022 [P].

We did not find an adult or women-specific study of nail biting and ADHD in our search (last updated October 2026). So the honest summary is: BFRBs seem common alongside ADHD, but adult evidence is preliminary.

3. Habit reversal therapy: large effects

Habit reversal therapy (HRT, not to be confused with hormone replacement therapy) is a behaviour therapy. It usually involves noticing the habit and its triggers, then practising a "competing response": something you do with your hands instead.

It has the strongest evidence of any BFRB treatment:

  • Across 18 studies with 575 participants, habit reversal had a large effect compared with control conditions (d = 0.80) for repetitive behaviours including nail biting, tics and stuttering Bate 2011 [E].
  • For hair pulling, across 11 randomised trials, behaviour therapy had a large pooled effect (ES 1.41), and serotonin reuptake inhibitor medicines (SRIs, a group of antidepressants) had a moderate one (ES 0.41) McGuire 2014 [E]. These were separate comparisons, not head-to-head trials.
  • Behaviour therapy worked better with more contact hours and when it included techniques to improve mood McGuire 2014 [E].

A clinical review recommends behaviour therapy, either habit reversal or acceptance-enhanced behaviour therapy, as part of treatment for skin picking disorder Grant 2012 [S].

4. NAC: three behaviours, three separate pieces of evidence

N-acetylcysteine (NAC) is sold as a supplement. It modulates glutamate, a brain chemical messenger Grant 2009 [S]. You'll often see it recommended online for picking and biting as if they were one thing. The trials tested each behaviour separately, in different groups of people, so a result for one can't be carried over to another. None of the trials below was in people with ADHD.

Hair pulling (trichotillomania), adults

  • In one 12-week placebo-controlled trial of 50 adults (45 women) with hair-pulling disorder, 56% were much or very much improved on NAC compared with 16% on placebo Grant 2009 [P].
  • Improvement first showed after 9 weeks, and no side effects were reported in the NAC group Grant 2009 [P]. It is one trial, so we grade it preliminary.

Skin picking (excoriation disorder), adults

  • In a 12-week double-blind trial of 66 adults with skin-picking disorder (59 of them women), NAC was linked to a bigger drop in skin-picking severity than placebo Grant 2016 [P].
  • Among the 53 who finished the trial, 15 of 32 (47%) on NAC were much or very much improved, compared with 4 of 21 (19%) on placebo Grant 2016 [P].
  • Everyday (psychosocial) functioning did not differ clearly between the groups. The authors reported NAC was well tolerated Grant 2016 [P].
  • This is one trial in adults with diagnosed skin-picking disorder. It is not evidence of a cure, and it tells us nothing directly about nail biting or about ADHD.

A clinical review also lists NAC among medicine options for skin picking, alongside behaviour therapy Grant 2012 [S].

Nail biting, children

  • The only nail-biting trial we found had 42 children and teenagers. Nails grew longer on NAC than on placebo after 1 month (5.21 vs 1.18 mm), but there was no clear difference at 2 months Ghanizadeh 2013 [P].
  • Two NAC participants stopped because of side effects Ghanizadeh 2013 [P].
  • We did not find a trial of NAC for nail biting in adults in our search (last updated October 2026).

Safety note. Small trials reporting few side effects don't show a supplement is risk-free. The official prescribing information for acetylcysteine (the medicine form of NAC, licensed for other uses) lists nausea, vomiting and other stomach symptoms with oral use, and rare rash or hives; it advises that people at risk of stomach bleeding, such as those with peptic ulcers, are assessed before use, and that people with asthma are watched for wheezing (bronchospasm) when it is inhaled Acetylcysteine prescribing information [Reference]. Supplement products are not checked in the same way as medicines. Practical tip: check with a clinician or pharmacist before starting NAC or any supplement, especially if you are pregnant, have asthma or a stomach ulcer, or take other medicines. This guide gives no doses.

5. Stimulants and picking: what the adult data show

Some people worry that ADHD stimulants will make picking or biting worse.

In the only adult data we found, among 308 adults with hair-pulling disorder, stimulant use did not appear to affect hair-pulling severity Chesivoir 2022 [P]. That is one study of one BFRB, and it was not a trial.

You may also see individual case reports of stimulants triggering picking. Case reports describe single people, which is too weak to tell you how often it happens, and our selection rules exclude them. So the popular claim "stimulants worsen BFRBs" is graded U (popular but unsupported): it hasn't been shown in adult data. That doesn't rule out that it could happen to some individuals. If you notice a change after starting or changing medication, tell your prescriber.

6. Measuring it

Tracking severity helps you and your clinician see whether something is working.

This section is partly a "What we don't know yet". We did not find a validated nail-biting severity scale in our search (last updated October 2026). General BFRB questionnaires exist, including the GBS-8 (Generic BFRB Scale-8), which covers severity and impairment for any BFRB. See our questionnaires page for details.

Practical tip: a simple daily log works well: - How much: number of episodes, or minutes, or a 0 to 10 rating. - When and where: time of day, place, activity (reading, screens, lectures, driving). - Mode: were you aware of it (focused), or did you only notice afterwards (automatic)? - Feeling before: bored, tense, anxious, understimulated. - Nails: a weekly photo can show change better than memory.

What you can do

  • Spot your triggers. Use the log above for two weeks. Patterns tell you where to put your effort.
  • Give your hands a job. Practical tip: a fidget, putty, a textured ring or a stress ball can serve as a competing response. This is the core idea in habit reversal.
  • Add friction. Practical tip: short nails, plasters on favourite fingers, gloves while watching screens, or covering mirrors where you pick.
  • Treat boredom and tension as signals. If the habit rises during long, understimulating tasks, plan movement breaks.
  • Ask for habit reversal therapy. It has the best evidence. Ask your GP or a psychologist whether it's available locally.
  • Be kind to yourself. Practical tip: a slip is information for your log, not a failure.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Picking or biting that causes pain, bleeding, infection or scarring.
  • A referral for habit reversal therapy or another behaviour therapy.
  • Whether NAC, or a medication, is worth trying for you, especially if you take other medicines.
  • Any change in picking or biting after starting or changing ADHD medication.
  • Anxiety, low mood or other conditions that may sit alongside the habit.

What we don't know yet

  • How common BFRBs are in adult women with ADHD. We did not find adult or women-specific nail-biting data in our search (last updated October 2026).
  • Whether NAC helps adult nail biting. We did not find an adult nail-biting trial. For skin picking there is one adult trial Grant 2016 [P], and it needs repeating.
  • Whether NAC works in people with ADHD. None of the NAC trials we found was in people with ADHD.
  • Whether stimulants affect BFRBs other than hair pulling. The one adult study we found covered hair pulling only.
  • Whether habit reversal works as well in people with ADHD. The trials we have were not specific to ADHD.
  • How best to measure nail biting. We did not find a validated nail-biting-specific scale (section 6).

Popular claims checked

  • nail-biting-and-picking-are-adhd-traits: "Nail biting and skin picking are ADHD traits." Verdict: partly true. BFRBs seem common with ADHD, but the strongest figure comes from children in a clinic Ghanizadeh 2008 [P], and the adult data we found cover hair pulling only Chesivoir 2022 [P], so they say nothing directly about nail biting or skin picking.
  • nac-for-nail-biting: "NAC stops nail biting." Verdict: partly true. The only trial we found was in 42 children: nails were longer at 1 month but not clearly at 2 months Ghanizadeh 2013 [P]. We did not find an adult trial. Habit reversal has the best evidence Bate 2011 [E].
  • nac-for-hair-pulling: "NAC helps hair pulling (trichotillomania)." Verdict: partly true. One trial in 50 adults found 56% much improved vs 16% on placebo Grant 2009 [P]; behaviour therapy has stronger evidence McGuire 2014 [E].
  • nac-for-skin-picking: "NAC helps skin picking." Verdict: partly true. One trial in 66 adults with skin-picking disorder found 47% vs 19% of completers much improved Grant 2016 [P]. It is one trial, not a cure, and not in ADHD.
  • pop-stimulants-worsen-nail-biting-picking: "Stimulants make nail biting and skin picking worse." Verdict: partly true. We did not find a study of stimulants and nail biting or skin picking. The only adult data we found are on hair pulling, where stimulant use did not appear to affect severity Chesivoir 2022 [P]; that can't be carried over to other behaviours. If you notice a change after starting or changing medication, tell your prescriber.
