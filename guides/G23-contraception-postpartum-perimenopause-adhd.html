Contraception, postpartum & perimenopause with ADHD

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Hormonal milestones can matter for mood in women with ADHD. Large registry studies link hormonal contraception with later depression, especially in teenagers. Young women with ADHD have a higher depression risk whether or not they use contraception; these studies show associations, not that contraception causes depression.

  • ADHD is linked to much higher rates of depression and anxiety diagnoses after birth. For perimenopause the evidence conflicts: one large cohort found more severe symptoms, starting earlier, in women with ADHD; a survey of 656 midlife women found no clear difference.

  • Some women seem especially sensitive to hormone changes. We did not find a trial of HRT for ADHD symptoms.

Evidence grade: contraception and postpartum S (Supported) as associations; perimenopause P (Preliminary); HRT for ADHD no eligible trial found. Headline grade P. · Talk to a clinician about: mood monitoring when starting contraception, a postpartum mood plan, and midlife changes in focus or mood.

Not medical advice. Crisis support →

A short glossary (definitions, not evidence claims):

  • Hormonal contraception: the combined pill, progestogen-only pill, patch, vaginal ring, hormonal IUS (coil) and similar methods.
  • Perimenopause: the years of changing hormones before periods stop. Menopause: 12 months after the last period. HRT (hormone replacement therapy, also called MHT): oestrogen, usually with a progestogen.
  • Registry study: a study of national health records. It shows links (associations), not causes.
  • Differential sensitivity: the idea that some brains react more strongly than others to normal hormone changes.

1. Hormonal contraception and depression

Population data. In 1,061,997 Danish women aged 15-34, hormonal contraception users had higher rates of starting an antidepressant for the first time than non-users (Skovlund 2016) [S]. The figures below are adjusted incidence rate ratios (RR), each comparing users of that method with non-users of hormonal contraception:

  • combined pill: RR 1.23
  • progestogen-only pill: 1.34
  • patch: 2.0
  • vaginal ring: 1.6
  • hormonal IUS: 1.4
  • teenagers aged 15-19 on the combined pill: RR 1.8

(Skovlund 2016) [S]

A follow-up in nearly half a million Danish women linked current or recent hormonal contraception to a first suicide attempt (hazard ratio 1.97, 1.85-2.10) and suicide (hazard ratio 3.08, 1.34-7.08) compared with never-use (Skovlund 2018) [S]. The link with attempts peaked after 2 months of use, and teenagers had the highest relative risk (Skovlund 2018) [S]. These are associations. They do not prove contraception causes depression, and they do not mean contraception should be avoided by default.

ADHD-specific data. In Swedish registers, 29,767 young women with ADHD and 763,146 without (aged 15-24) were compared (Lundin 2023) [S]. All figures are adjusted hazard ratios (aHR). Each compares a group with a different reference group, so read the comparator carefully:

  • ADHD vs no ADHD, whatever the contraception: aHR 3.69 for depression.
  • Combined-pill users with ADHD vs women without ADHD not using the combined pill: aHR 5.19.
  • Combined-pill users with ADHD vs combined-pill users without ADHD: aHR 6.10.
  • Progestogen-only pill users with ADHD: aHR 5.00 (the abstract describes this as the corresponding risk, without restating the reference group).
  • Non-oral methods: depression risk was similarly moderately raised in both groups.

(Lundin 2023) [S]

How to read this. Every comparison above uses women without ADHD as the reference, so a large part of each figure reflects ADHD itself (aHR 3.69), not the pill. The 6.10 figure compares two groups who were both on the combined pill, so it mainly describes the ADHD difference among pill users. The abstract does not give a direct comparison of women with ADHD who used the pill against women with ADHD who did not, so these numbers cannot be read as "the pill multiplies depression risk five- or six-fold in ADHD". The authors conclude that young women with ADHD have a higher depression risk when using oral hormonal contraception than their peers without ADHD, and that contraception counselling should include this (Lundin 2023) [S]. These are observational associations: women who choose or are prescribed the pill may differ in other ways, and the study cannot show that the pill causes depression.

The practical reading: young women with ADHD start with a higher depression risk. That is a reason to monitor mood, especially in the first months on an oral method, not a reason to go without contraception.

Why contraception matters especially with ADHD. Danish females with ADHD were more likely to become parents at 12 to 16 years (IRR 3.62, 95% CI 2.14-6.13) and at 17 to 19 years (IRR 1.94, 1.62-2.33) than females without ADHD (Østergaard 2017) [S]. In a US cohort of 140 women with and 88 without childhood ADHD, the link with unplanned pregnancy ran through low school achievement in early adolescence and then risky sexual behaviour (Owens 2019) [P]. A major review lists unplanned pregnancy among the heightened risks for girls and women with ADHD (Hinshaw 2021) [E].

Practical tip: if remembering a daily pill is hard, that is a fair point to raise when choosing a method. Ask about methods that don't depend on daily memory, and about how each might affect your mood.

Safety note on combined hormonal contraception (the combined pill, patch and ring). These contain oestrogen, which brings a small risk of blood clots. NHS information says the risk is very small, affecting up to 1 in 1,000 people using combined hormonal contraception, and that clots can affect the leg or lung or cause a heart attack or stroke (NHS: Combined pill side effects and risks [Reference]). The combined pill may not be suitable if you have had blood clots (or a close relative had them under 45), a stroke or narrowed arteries, heart disease or high blood pressure, migraine with aura, breast cancer, certain liver or gallbladder problems or diabetes complications, or if you are living with obesity, or are 35 or over and smoke (NHS: Who can take the combined pill [Reference]). If you are breastfeeding, NHS information says to wait 6 weeks after birth before starting the combined pill (NHS: Who can take the combined pill [Reference]). A clinician checks these before prescribing; this is a summary, not a checklist to decide on your own.

2. Postpartum: much higher rates of diagnosed depression and anxiety

Among 773,047 women giving birth in Sweden, 3,515 had ADHD (Andersson 2023) [S]. In the year after delivery, 16.76% of women with ADHD were diagnosed with depression (PR 5.09, 4.68-5.54) and 24.92% with anxiety (PR 5.41, 5.06-5.78) (Andersson 2023) [S]. The authors note surveillance bias is possible.

Other studies agree in direction. A review of ten studies found higher odds of perinatal depression with ADHD (odds ratios 1.8-2.63) (Pierotti 2025) [P]. In 2,544 Canadian couples, mothers with possible ADHD had higher odds of depression (adjusted odds ratio 1.70, 1.13-2.56) and anxiety (adjusted odds ratio 1.74, 1.30-2.34) up to two years after birth (Johnson 2025) [P].

A premenstrual disorder history is another strong flag: in Swedish registers it was recorded in 2.9% of women with perinatal depression vs 0.6% of controls (odds ratio 4.76, 95% CI 4.52,5.01) (Yang 2024) [S].

G27 covers postpartum mood in full, with a plan you can fill in. G26 covers medication in pregnancy and breastfeeding.

3. Perimenopause: more severe symptoms, possibly earlier, but the studies conflict

Two recent studies asked whether menopause is harder with ADHD, and they disagree.

Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025 [P] Chapman 2025 [P]
Who 535 women with self-reported ADHD and 4,857 without, from a population-based cohort 656 women aged 45 to 60; 245 with an ADHD diagnosis
Finding Higher total perimenopausal symptom scores (18.0 vs 13.0). Severe symptoms more common (54.2% vs 30.1%; prevalence ratio 1.80, 1.64-1.98), across psychological, somatic and urogenital symptoms. After correcting for multiple tests, no clear difference was found in menopausal complaints between women with and without ADHD at any stage, with or without medication.
Extra The gap was largest at ages 35-39 (19.0 vs 12.5), which the authors read as earlier perimenopause in ADHD. ADHD symptoms and menopausal complaints went together in everyone, less strongly within the ADHD group. The authors suggest women with ADHD may attribute symptoms differently.

Both are self-report studies. The larger one is population-based, which is a strength. The smaller one used a diagnosis rather than self-report of ADHD, which is also a strength. Neither settles the question.

What is clearer is that ADHD does not simply fade by midlife. In 323 adults with ADHD followed from about age 34 to 47, 68.8% had a stable course, and being a woman predicted a stable course (Grevet 2022) [P]. An expert review concludes that hormonal transitions worsen ADHD symptoms and mood, and that tailored treatments are lacking (Kooij 2025) [P].

For autistic women, the menopause picture and HRT trial evidence are in G30.

4. Differential sensitivity, again

The idea behind PMDD (G05) is that some women react strongly to normal hormone changes. In a small experiment, women with PMS got their symptoms back when normal levels of oestrogen or progesterone were added back after hormone suppression; women without PMS showed no clear mood change on the same regimen (Schmidt 1998) [S].

The same pattern shows up in perimenopause. In 26 women with past perimenopausal depression and 30 controls, all were given oestrogen for 3 weeks and then some were switched, without knowing, to placebo (Schmidt 2015) [P]. Depression scores rose in women with past perimenopausal depression (CES-D 2.4 to 8.8); no clear change was seen in controls, despite similar hormone levels (Schmidt 2015) [P].

Depressive symptoms are more likely during the transition to menopause and less likely afterwards, in a cohort of 218 African American and 218 white women aged 35 to 47 followed over 4 years (Freeman 2004) [S]. And on daily ratings, 21% of depressed vs 3% of non-depressed perimenopausal women met criteria for premenstrual dysphoria (Richards 2006) [P]. Women could not reliably report this from memory (Richards 2006) [P].

Is ADHD itself sensitive to hormone changes? Early research hints yes. In 32 young women tracked daily, lower oestrogen together with higher progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the next day (Roberts 2018) [P]. A review of eleven studies found the evidence suggestive but few, small and mixed, and called for research on menopause (Osianlis 2025) [P]. We did not find an eligible study testing differential sensitivity in perimenopausal women with ADHD in our search (last updated October 2026).

5. What's not known: HRT for ADHD symptoms

We did not find an eligible trial of HRT for ADHD symptoms in our search (last updated October 2026). That is the honest headline.

What trials in women without ADHD show:

  • Cognition. In one trial of 567 healthy postmenopausal women, oral oestradiol for a mean 57 months showed no clear effect on memory, executive functions or global thinking, whether started within 6 years or 10 or more years after menopause (Henderson 2016) [E]. This applies to that population and that regimen.
  • Mood. In 172 perimenopausal and early postmenopausal women without depression, 12 months of oestradiol patches plus progesterone meant fewer developed clinically significant depressive symptoms than on placebo (17.3% vs 32.3%) (Gordon 2018) [S]. The benefit was in the early transition, not later (Gordon 2018) [S].

These results cannot be transferred to ADHD symptoms. Executive functions are not the same as ADHD, and none of these women were selected for ADHD.

What HRT is recommended for, and its risks. UK guidance recommends offering HRT for menopause-related hot flushes and night sweats, and considering it for low mood that starts around menopause but does not meet criteria for depression (NICE NG23 [Guideline]). The same guidance notes that oral HRT raises the risk of blood clots (transdermal HRT does not), that combined HRT raises breast cancer risk with longer use, and that oral-oestrogen combined HRT raises stroke risk (NICE NG23 [Guideline]). These decisions belong with a clinician. G30 has more on HRT.

6. Questions for your clinician

Practical tip: prompts to bring, not evidence claims.

  • Contraception: "I have ADHD. Which methods fit my life, and how will we check my mood in the first few months?"
  • Mood history: "I've had depression, PMDD or anxiety before. Does that change which method you'd suggest, or how we monitor?"
  • Pregnancy planning: "What is my plan for ADHD medication before, during and after pregnancy?" (G26)
  • After birth: "Who will screen my mood, and how often?" (G27)
  • Midlife: "My focus, sleep and mood have changed. Could this be perimenopause, my ADHD changing, depression, or a mix? How would we tell?"
  • HRT: "What would HRT be for, in my case? What is known about mood, and what isn't known about ADHD?"
  • Medication review: "Does my ADHD treatment need reviewing now that my hormones are changing?"

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. When starting or changing contraception, keep a short mood log for the first three months. Note the start date.
  2. Track your cycle with daily ratings if you notice cyclical changes, including in perimenopause while you still have periods (G04).
  3. Tell your midwife or GP about ADHD and any premenstrual mood history during pregnancy.
  4. In midlife, write down what changed and when (focus, sleep, mood, periods, hot flushes) before an appointment.
  5. Be wary of anyone promising that HRT will fix ADHD. We did not find a trial testing it.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Mood monitoring when starting hormonal contraception, especially if you are young, have ADHD, or have a history of depression.
  • Contraception options that fit how you manage daily tasks.
  • A postpartum mood plan, especially with a premenstrual disorder history.
  • Midlife changes in ADHD symptoms or mood, and whether perimenopause may be part of it.
  • What HRT is and isn't expected to help with, for you.

What we don't know yet

  • HRT for ADHD symptoms. We did not find an eligible trial in our search (last updated October 2026). This is the main gap behind section 5.
  • Whether menopause worsens ADHD. Two studies disagree; neither tracked the same women over time.
  • Whether perimenopause starts earlier in ADHD. One cohort's age pattern suggests it; it was not measured directly.
  • Differential sensitivity in ADHD. Tested in PMS, PMDD and perimenopausal depression; we did not find a study in women with ADHD.
  • Which contraceptive methods are best for mood in ADHD. Lundin 2023 compared groups but was not a trial of methods, and did not directly compare pill users and non-users within the ADHD group in its abstract.
  • ADHD medication across the menopause. We did not find an eligible study of whether doses need adjusting.

Safety and crisis resources

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

Hormonal contraception has been linked to suicide attempts, especially soon after starting and in teenagers (Skovlund 2018) [S]. ADHD itself is linked to suicidal thoughts and attempts (Septier 2019) [E]. If your mood drops sharply after starting a method, after birth or in midlife, tell a clinician promptly. If you have thoughts of suicide, get help now rather than waiting for them to pass: see crisis help. After birth, sudden confusion, hallucinations or very high or rapidly changing mood can be postpartum psychosis, which is an emergency (G27).

Popular claims checked

  • The pill causes depression, especially if you have ADHD. Verdict: partly true (supported). Registry studies link hormonal contraception to depression (Skovlund 2016) [S]. Young women with ADHD on the pill had a higher depression risk than their peers without ADHD, but much of that difference reflects ADHD itself (Lundin 2023) [S]. These are associations, not proof of cause.
  • Perimenopause makes ADHD much worse. Verdict: partly true (preliminary). One cohort found more severe perimenopausal symptoms with ADHD (Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025) [P]; a newer survey found no clear difference after correction for multiple tests (Chapman 2025) [P]. Neither measured ADHD symptoms changing within the same women over time.
  • Estrogen boosts dopamine, so ADHD gets worse whenever estrogen drops. Verdict: partly true (preliminary). One small daily study supports a link (Roberts 2018) [P]; the mechanism has not been tested in ADHD.
  • Menopause is always worse for women with ADHD. Verdict: overstated (preliminary). Studies disagree (Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025; Chapman 2025) [P]; "always" is not supported.
  • "HRT will fix midlife brain fog or ADHD." (Not yet in the claims ledger.) Verdict: unsupported. In one long trial of healthy postmenopausal women, oral oestradiol showed no clear effect on cognition (Henderson 2016) [E], and we did not find a trial of HRT for ADHD symptoms.
