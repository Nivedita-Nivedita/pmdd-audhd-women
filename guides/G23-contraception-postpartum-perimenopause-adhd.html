Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Hormonal milestones can matter for mood in women with ADHD. Large registry studies link hormonal contraception to depression, most strongly in teenagers and in young women with ADHD, and link ADHD to much higher rates of depression and anxiety diagnoses after birth.

  • For perimenopause the evidence conflicts. One large cohort found more severe symptoms, starting earlier, in women with ADHD; a survey of 656 midlife women found no difference.

  • Some women seem especially sensitive to hormone changes. Whether HRT helps ADHD symptoms has never been tested.

Evidence grade: contraception and postpartum S (Supported) as associations; perimenopause P (Preliminary); HRT for ADHD untested. Headline grade P. · Talk to a clinician about: mood monitoring when starting contraception, a postpartum mood plan, and midlife changes in focus or mood.

A short glossary (definitions, not evidence claims):

  • Hormonal contraception: the combined pill, progestogen-only pill, patch, vaginal ring, hormonal IUS (coil) and similar methods.
  • Perimenopause: the years of changing hormones before periods stop. Menopause: 12 months after the last period. HRT (hormone replacement therapy, also called MHT): oestrogen, usually with a progestogen.
  • Registry study: a study of national health records. It shows links (associations), not causes.
  • Differential sensitivity: the idea that some brains react more strongly than others to normal hormone changes.

1. Hormonal contraception and depression

Population data. In 1,061,997 Danish women aged 15-34, hormonal contraception users had higher rates of starting an antidepressant for the first time than non-users (Skovlund 2016) [S]:

  • combined pill: RR 1.23
  • progestogen-only pill: 1.34
  • patch: 2.0
  • vaginal ring: 1.6
  • hormonal IUS: 1.4
  • teenagers aged 15-19 on the combined pill: RR 1.8

(Skovlund 2016) [S]

A follow-up in nearly half a million Danish women linked current or recent hormonal contraception to a first suicide attempt (HR 1.97, 1.85-2.10) and suicide (HR 3.08, 1.34-7.08) compared with never-use (Skovlund 2018) [S]. The link with attempts peaked after 2 months of use, and teenagers had the highest relative risk (Skovlund 2018) [S]. These are associations. They do not prove contraception causes depression, and they do not mean contraception should be avoided by default.

ADHD-specific data. In Swedish registers, 29,767 young women with ADHD and 763,146 without (aged 15-24) were compared (Lundin 2023) [S]:

  • Women with ADHD had about a 3-fold higher risk of depression regardless of contraception (aHR 3.69).
  • Combined-pill users with ADHD: aHR 5.19 compared with non-users without ADHD, and 6.10 compared with combined-pill users without ADHD.
  • Progestogen-only pill users with ADHD: aHR 5.00.
  • Non-oral methods were moderately raised to a similar degree in both groups.

(Lundin 2023) [S]

The practical reading: young women with ADHD start with a higher depression risk, and oral contraception was where the extra risk was largest. That is a reason to monitor mood, especially in the first months, not a reason to go without contraception.

Why contraception matters especially with ADHD. Danish females with ADHD were more likely to become parents at 12 to 16 years (IRR 3.62, 95% CI 2.14-6.13) and at 17 to 19 years (IRR 1.94, 1.62-2.33) than females without ADHD (Østergaard 2017) [S]. In a US cohort of 140 women with and 88 without childhood ADHD, the link with unplanned pregnancy ran through low school achievement in early adolescence and then risky sexual behaviour (Owens 2019) [P]. A major review lists unplanned pregnancy among the heightened risks for girls and women with ADHD (Hinshaw 2021) [E].

Practical tip: if remembering a daily pill is hard, that is a fair point to raise when choosing a method. Ask about methods that don't depend on daily memory, and about how each might affect your mood.

2. Postpartum: much higher rates of diagnosed depression and anxiety

Among 773,047 women giving birth in Sweden, 3,515 had ADHD (Andersson 2023) [S]. In the year after delivery, 16.76% of women with ADHD were diagnosed with depression (PR 5.09, 4.68-5.54) and 24.92% with anxiety (PR 5.41, 5.06-5.78) (Andersson 2023) [S]. The authors note surveillance bias is possible.

Other studies agree in direction. A review of ten studies found higher odds of perinatal depression with ADHD (OR 1.8-2.63) (Pierotti 2025) [P]. In 2,544 Canadian couples, mothers with possible ADHD had higher odds of depression (aOR 1.70, 1.13-2.56) and anxiety (aOR 1.74, 1.30-2.34) up to two years after birth (Johnson 2025) [P].

A premenstrual disorder history is another strong flag: in Swedish registers it was recorded in 2.9% of women with perinatal depression vs 0.6% of controls (OR 4.76, 95% CI 4.52,5.01) (Yang 2024) [S].

G27 covers postpartum mood in full, with a plan you can fill in. G26 covers medication in pregnancy and breastfeeding.

3. Perimenopause: more severe symptoms, possibly earlier, but the studies conflict

Two recent studies asked whether menopause is harder with ADHD, and they disagree.

Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025 [P] Chapman 2025 [P]
Who 535 women with self-reported ADHD and 4,857 without, from a population-based cohort 656 women aged 45 to 60; 245 with an ADHD diagnosis
Finding Higher total perimenopausal symptom scores (18.0 vs 13.0). Severe symptoms more common (54.2% vs 30.1%; PR 1.80, 1.64-1.98), across psychological, somatic and urogenital symptoms. After correcting for multiple tests, women with ADHD did not report more menopausal complaints at any stage, with or without medication.
Extra The gap was largest at ages 35-39 (19.0 vs 12.5), which the authors read as earlier perimenopause in ADHD. ADHD symptoms and menopausal complaints went together in everyone, less strongly within the ADHD group. The authors suggest women with ADHD may attribute symptoms differently.

Both are self-report studies. The larger one is population-based, which is a strength. The smaller one used a diagnosis rather than self-report of ADHD, which is also a strength. Neither settles the question.

What is clearer is that ADHD does not simply fade by midlife. In 323 adults with ADHD followed from about age 34 to 47, 68.8% had a stable course, and being a woman predicted a stable course (Grevet 2022) [P]. An expert review concludes that hormonal transitions worsen ADHD symptoms and mood, and that tailored treatments are lacking (Kooij 2025) [P].

For autistic women, the menopause picture and HRT trial evidence are in G30.

4. Differential sensitivity, again

The idea behind PMDD (G05) is that some women react strongly to normal hormone changes. In a small experiment, women with PMS got their symptoms back when normal levels of oestrogen or progesterone were added back after hormone suppression; women without PMS had no mood change on the same regimen (Schmidt 1998) [S].

The same pattern shows up in perimenopause. In 26 women with past perimenopausal depression and 30 controls, all were given oestrogen for 3 weeks and then some were switched, without knowing, to placebo (Schmidt 2015) [P]. Depression scores rose in women with past perimenopausal depression (CES-D 2.4 to 8.8) but not in controls, despite similar hormone levels (Schmidt 2015) [P].

Depressive symptoms are more likely during the transition to menopause and less likely afterwards, in a cohort of 218 African American and 218 white women aged 35 to 47 followed over 4 years (Freeman 2004) [S]. And on daily ratings, 21% of depressed vs 3% of non-depressed perimenopausal women met criteria for premenstrual dysphoria (Richards 2006) [P]. Women could not reliably report this from memory (Richards 2006) [P].

Is ADHD itself sensitive to hormone changes? Early research hints yes. In 32 young women tracked daily, lower oestrogen together with higher progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the next day (Roberts 2018) [P]. A review of eleven studies found the evidence suggestive but few, small and mixed, and called for research on menopause (Osianlis 2025) [P]. No study has tested differential sensitivity in perimenopausal women with ADHD.

5. What's not known: HRT for ADHD symptoms

No trial has tested HRT for ADHD symptoms. That is the honest headline.

What trials in women without ADHD show:

  • Cognition. In 567 healthy postmenopausal women, oral oestradiol for a mean 57 months had no effect on memory, executive functions or global thinking, whenever it was started (Henderson 2016) [E].
  • Mood. In 172 perimenopausal and early postmenopausal women without depression, 12 months of oestradiol patches plus progesterone meant fewer developed clinically significant depressive symptoms than on placebo (17.3% vs 32.3%) (Gordon 2018) [S]. The benefit was in the early transition, not later (Gordon 2018) [S].

These results cannot be transferred to ADHD symptoms. Executive functions are not the same as ADHD, and none of these women were selected for ADHD. G30 has more on HRT.

6. Questions for your clinician

Practical tip: prompts to bring, not evidence claims.

  • Contraception: "I have ADHD. Which methods fit my life, and how will we check my mood in the first few months?"
  • Mood history: "I've had depression, PMDD or anxiety before. Does that change which method you'd suggest, or how we monitor?"
  • Pregnancy planning: "What is my plan for ADHD medication before, during and after pregnancy?" (G26)
  • After birth: "Who will screen my mood, and how often?" (G27)
  • Midlife: "My focus, sleep and mood have changed. Could this be perimenopause, my ADHD changing, depression, or a mix? How would we tell?"
  • HRT: "What would HRT be for, in my case? What is known about mood, and what isn't known about ADHD?"
  • Medication review: "Does my ADHD treatment need reviewing now that my hormones are changing?"

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. When starting or changing contraception, keep a short mood log for the first three months. Note the start date.
  2. Track your cycle with daily ratings if you notice cyclical changes, including in perimenopause while you still have periods (G04).
  3. Tell your midwife or GP about ADHD and any premenstrual mood history during pregnancy.
  4. In midlife, write down what changed and when (focus, sleep, mood, periods, hot flushes) before an appointment.
  5. Be wary of anyone promising that HRT will fix ADHD. That has not been tested.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Mood monitoring when starting hormonal contraception, especially if you are young, have ADHD, or have a history of depression.
  • Contraception options that fit how you manage daily tasks.
  • A postpartum mood plan, especially with a premenstrual disorder history.
  • Midlife changes in ADHD symptoms or mood, and whether perimenopause may be part of it.
  • What HRT is and isn't expected to help with, for you.

What we don't know yet

  • HRT for ADHD symptoms. No trial. This is the main gap behind section 5.
  • Whether menopause worsens ADHD. Two studies disagree; neither tracked the same women over time.
  • Whether perimenopause starts earlier in ADHD. One cohort's age pattern suggests it; it was not measured directly.
  • Differential sensitivity in ADHD. Tested in PMS, PMDD and perimenopausal depression, but not in women with ADHD.
  • Which contraceptive methods are best for mood in ADHD. Lundin 2023 compared groups but was not a trial of methods.
  • ADHD medication across the menopause. Whether doses need adjusting is unstudied.

Safety and crisis resources

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

Hormonal contraception has been linked to suicide attempts, especially soon after starting and in teenagers (Skovlund 2018) [S]. ADHD itself is linked to suicidal thoughts and attempts (Septier 2019) [E]. If your mood drops sharply after starting a method, after birth or in midlife, tell a clinician promptly.

Popular claims checked

  • The pill causes depression, especially if you have ADHD. Verdict: partly true (supported). Registry studies link hormonal contraception to depression, and young women with ADHD on the pill had the highest risk (Lundin 2023) [S], but these are associations, not proof of cause.
  • Perimenopause makes ADHD much worse. Verdict: partly true (preliminary). One cohort found more severe symptoms with ADHD (Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025) [P]; a newer survey found no difference (Chapman 2025) [P].
  • Estrogen boosts dopamine, so ADHD gets worse whenever estrogen drops. Verdict: partly true (preliminary). One small daily study supports a link (Roberts 2018) [P]; the mechanism has not been tested in ADHD.
  • "HRT will fix midlife brain fog or ADHD." (Not yet in the claims ledger.) Verdict: unsupported. Oestradiol had no effect on cognition in a long trial (Henderson 2016) [E], and HRT for ADHD symptoms is untested.
