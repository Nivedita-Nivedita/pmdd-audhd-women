ADHD + PMDD: what the evidence says

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Every study so far points the same way: women with ADHD, or with more ADHD traits, are more likely to screen positive for PMDD. The direction is Supported (S).

  • How much more likely is not settled. Every study used a one-off questionnaire, and none used the daily tracking that a PMDD diagnosis needs. The size is Preliminary (P).

  • PMDD is a sensitivity to normal hormone changes, not a hormone imbalance. It can be treated, and it is linked to a higher risk of suicidal thoughts, so it deserves to be taken seriously.

Evidence grade: direction S, size P. Headline grade P (Preliminary), the weakest essential claim. · Talk to a clinician about: daily cycle tracking, treatment options, and any premenstrual change in your ADHD medication.

Not medical advice. Crisis support →

A quick glossary before we start (definitions, not evidence claims):

  • PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder): a severe, cyclical mood condition. Symptoms appear in the days before a period and ease soon after it starts.
  • PME (premenstrual exacerbation): an existing condition, such as ADHD, depression or anxiety, that gets worse before a period. It is not the same as PMDD.
  • Provisional vs prospective (confirmed) diagnosis: in research, "provisional" means a one-off, look-back questionnaire suggests PMDD. A "prospective" or confirmed diagnosis means daily symptom ratings, kept as you go over at least two cycles, show the pattern. In the clinic, a clinician can also make a provisional diagnosis from your history and discuss care while you track.
  • Odds ratio (OR) vs risk ratio (RR): two different ways of comparing groups. A risk ratio compares the chance of an outcome; an odds ratio compares the odds. When an outcome is fairly common, an odds ratio can be noticeably larger than the matching risk ratio, so the two can't be merged into one "times more likely" figure.
  • Luteal phase: the second half of the cycle, from ovulation to your period.

1. The claim you've seen ("3x more likely"), and where it comes from

You may have seen the claim that women with ADHD are "3x more likely" to have PMDD. It is widespread on social media. It is worth knowing where it comes from, because the real finding is both more interesting and less certain than the slogan.

Our claim check traces it mainly to one survey and its media coverage (claim check: women with ADHD are 3x more likely to have PMDD). In that survey, 715 females aged 18–34 were recruited online through the Prolific platform (Broughton 2025) [P]. Of these, 102 reported a clinical ADHD diagnosis, 229 met ADHD criteria on a symptom questionnaire (the ASRS), and 305 had no ADHD (Broughton 2025) [P].

Possible PMDD, measured with a one-off screening questionnaire called the PSST, was found in:

  • 31.4% of those with a self-reported clinical ADHD diagnosis
  • 41.1% of those with ADHD on the symptom questionnaire
  • 9.8% of those without ADHD

(Broughton 2025) [P]

The "3x" figure comes from dividing one of those percentages by another (31.4% vs 9.8%). That is a simple, unadjusted ratio of provisional (questionnaire-based) PMDD in one online sample. It is not a consensus figure, and it is not a confirmed diagnosis rate.

An earlier source is often quoted too. In 209 consecutive women attending an adult ADHD clinic, PMDD symptoms were common compared with general-population figures (Dorani 2021) [P]. That study had no matched control group, so it cannot tell us how much higher the risk is (Dorani 2021) [P].

2. What the studies actually found: all in the same direction

The reason we grade the direction as Supported is that different kinds of study, in different places, keep finding the same thing.

An online survey of young women. As above, possible PMDD was more common in young women with ADHD than without it (31.4% and 41.1% vs 9.8%) (Broughton 2025) [P]. The risk was highest in those with ADHD on the questionnaire plus depression or anxiety (risk ratio 4.53, 95% CI 3.10–6.61) (Broughton 2025) [P].

Working women in Japan. Among 2,000 employed Japanese women aged 20–44 who had never been pregnant and were not using contraception, ADHD traits were linked to PMDD (adjusted odds ratio 6.49, 4.10–10.2) (Tsuji 2026) [P]. Autistic traits were not (Tsuji 2026) [P]. PMDD prevalence in this sample was 5.1%, measured on a Japanese scale based on older (DSM-IV-TR) research criteria (Tsuji 2026) [P].

ADHD and autism compared. A study of 199 women aged 20–40 not on hormonal contraception included 89 with ADHD, 39 autistic women and 71 controls (Groenman 2026) [P]. Both the ADHD and the autistic groups had higher rates of provisional PMDD than controls, with no difference between the two groups (Groenman 2026) [P]. Premenstrual symptoms rose with inattention, hyperactivity-impulsivity and social difficulties (Groenman 2026) [P].

Genetics. In 56,725 Norwegian women, 21.7% reported premenstrual depression or irritability (Jaholkowski 2023) [S]. These symptoms were associated with higher polygenic risk scores for ADHD (beta 0.07), as well as for depression, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and autism, but not with the score for height (Jaholkowski 2023) [S]. The link held in women without a psychiatric diagnosis (Jaholkowski 2023) [S]. A polygenic risk score adds up many small genetic differences, so this points to a shared genetic background, not a single cause.

Expert opinion agrees on direction. An expert review from a European special-interest group on female ADHD concludes that undiagnosed women with ADHD have increased vulnerability to PMDD (Kooij 2025) [P].

Put together: the studies use different people, countries and methods, and they all point the same way. That consistency is why the direction earns an S. Note that the figures use different measures: a simple ratio of percentages, a risk ratio (Broughton) and an adjusted odds ratio (Tsuji). They can't be combined into a single "times more likely" range.

3. Why the size isn't settled

The size of the link earns only a P. There are three main reasons.

1. Every study used a one-off questionnaire. Confirming PMDD rests on a careful history plus daily symptom ratings across at least two cycles (Yonkers 2018) [E]. In a study of 200 women with two to four months of daily ratings, women's memories of premenstrual worsening poorly predicted what the daily ratings showed (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S]. This matters a lot for numbers. A meta-analysis of 44 studies (50,659 participants) found PMDD in 3.2% of people when diagnosis was confirmed, but 7.7% when it was provisional (Reilly 2024) [S]. The authors conclude that studies relying on provisional diagnosis are likely to produce artificially high rates (Reilly 2024) [S].

None of the ADHD and PMDD studies above used confirmed, prospective diagnosis. We don't know whether women with ADHD over-report or under-report on one-off questionnaires, or whether that differs from other women.

2. The samples were convenience samples. They include an online panel (Broughton 2025) [P], employed women (Tsuji 2026) [P] and an ADHD clinic (Dorani 2021) [P]. The authors of one study warn directly that prevalence depends on how participants are recruited (Groenman 2026) [P].

3. PME can look like PMDD. About 60% of women with mood disorders report that their symptoms get worse before a period (Kuehner 2021) [S]. In the online survey, the highest risk was in women with ADHD plus depression or anxiety (Broughton 2025) [P]. A one-off questionnaire cannot tell whether someone has PMDD, or has ADHD, depression or anxiety that worsens premenstrually. Daily ratings, read by a clinician, are what begin to separate them (see section 5).

How to say it accurately. Here is wording you can reuse:

"Several studies find that women with ADHD are considerably more likely to screen positive for PMDD than women without ADHD. We did not find a study in our search (last updated October 2026) that has confirmed this with the daily symptom tracking needed for a formal diagnosis."

You may see a range such as "2–6 times" quoted elsewhere. We don't use it. It merges different measures (a ratio of percentages, a risk ratio and an adjusted odds ratio) from different populations, all based on provisional screening. Quoting each figure with its measure and method is more accurate.

4. PMDD isn't a hormone imbalance: differential sensitivity

A common belief is that PMDD is caused by "low progesterone" or a "hormone imbalance". The evidence points somewhere else.

In PMDD, hormone levels are usually normal. The brain reacts unusually to the normal rise and fall of hormones across the cycle (Schmidt 1998; Schmidt 2017; Hantsoo 2020) [E]. Researchers call this differential sensitivity.

The key experiments are small but striking:

  • In women with premenstrual syndrome (PMS), switching off the ovaries with a drug called leuprolide reduced symptoms compared with placebo (Schmidt 1998) [S]. Adding back normal levels of estradiol or progesterone brought symptoms back in the 10 women who had responded (Schmidt 1998) [S]. In 15 women without PMS, the same hormones caused no mood change (Schmidt 1998) [S].
  • In 12 women whose PMDD went away when their ovaries were switched off, symptoms rose in the first month of steady add-back hormones, but not in months 2 and 3 (Schmidt 2017) [P]. The authors conclude that the change in hormone levels, not the steady level, was associated with symptoms (Schmidt 2017) [P].

Scientists are still working out the mechanism. One leading theory involves allopregnanolone, a brain-active substance made from progesterone. Reviews propose that in PMDD the brain's calming GABA-A system reacts abnormally to normal luteal-phase levels of it (Bäckström 2014; Hantsoo 2020) [P]. In a small trial of 16 women with PMDD, blocking allopregnanolone production with a high dose of dutasteride reduced irritability, sadness and anxiety, while no clear effect was found with a low dose (Martinez 2015) [P]. Lab-grown cells from women with PMDD also handled some hormone-responsive genes differently from other women's cells (Dubey 2017) [P].

What this means for you. A blood test of your hormone levels cannot diagnose PMDD (see our claim check on hormone tests). Your hormones may be completely normal while your brain responds strongly to them. This is a real biological difference, not a character flaw.

5. PMDD vs premenstrual exacerbation (PME) of ADHD, depression or anxiety

This distinction is especially important if you have ADHD.

PMDD is its own diagnosis. The DSM-5 working group concluded that the evidence was strong enough to make PMDD a full diagnostic category, and cites it as affecting 2–5% of premenopausal women (Epperson 2012) [E]. When diagnosis is confirmed with daily ratings, a meta-analysis puts it at 3.2%, and at 1.6% in community samples (Reilly 2024) [S].

PME means a condition you already have, which is there all month, gets worse before your period. Around 60% of women with mood disorders report PME, and PME predicts a more severe course (Kuehner 2021) [S]. Treatments that help PMDD show less or no benefit in PME, though the evidence is sparse and mostly from small studies (Kuehner 2021) [S].

ADHD symptoms themselves may shift across the cycle. In a small diary study of 32 young women followed for 35 days, lower estradiol together with higher progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the next day (Roberts 2018) [P]. Symptoms seemed to rise in the early follicular phase (just after a period starts) and the early luteal phase (just after ovulation) (Roberts 2018) [P]. These women were not selected for ADHD. A systematic review of 11 studies found only suggestive evidence linking ADHD symptoms to sex hormones, from studies that were few, small and varied (Osianlis 2025) [P].

Premenstrual worsening can also be dramatic without being PMDD. In a trial of 28 women with suicidal thoughts, 93% had their peak around menstruation, yet none met full PMDD criteria (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022) [P].

How the two are told apart. Daily ratings kept over at least two cycles confirm whether the pattern is premenstrual. A standard scoring system called the C-PASS, one widely used tool rather than the only accepted route, turns two or more months of daily ratings into a DSM-5 PMDD diagnosis (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S]. In 200 women it agreed with expert clinical diagnosis with an estimated 98% correct classification (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017) [S].

Practical tip: the C-PASS tells your clinician whether your daily ratings meet the PMDD pattern. Deciding whether the pattern is PMDD or PME of ADHD, depression or anxiety still needs a clinician who knows your history. Tracking your ADHD symptoms on the same chart (see G04) gives them what they need.

6. Treatment evidence for PMDD, and what's untested in ADHD

PMDD has a reasonable treatment evidence base. Almost none of it was tested in women with ADHD.

A clinical review describes the most effective treatments for PMS and PMDD as serotonin reuptake inhibitors and hormonal contraceptives taken with a shortened or no hormone-free break. Women who do not respond may benefit from GnRH agonist treatment, which switches off the ovarian cycle (Yonkers 2018) [S].

SSRIs (a type of antidepressant). The 2024 Cochrane review of 34 trials found SSRIs probably reduce premenstrual symptoms in PMS and PMDD (standardised mean difference −0.57; moderate-certainty evidence) (Jespersen 2024) [E]. Both schedules have evidence: SSRIs beat placebo when taken every day and when taken only in the luteal phase (Jespersen 2024) [S]. Comparing the two groups of trials, the review concluded that continuous dosing is probably more effective on average (Jespersen 2024) [S]. That is a comparison between subgroups of trials, not a rule for everyone; which schedule suits you is decided with your prescriber. An earlier, 2013 version of the review had found no clear difference between schedules (Marjoribanks 2013) [S]; the 2024 update replaces it. SSRIs probably increase side effects, most commonly nausea (odds ratio 3.30), low energy (odds ratio 3.28) and sleepiness (odds ratio 3.26) (Jespersen 2024) [E]. G39 covers side effects, safety checks and the other options in more detail. PMDD is not only emotional: in a trial of 320 women with confirmed PMDD, daily fluoxetine also beat placebo for breast tenderness, bloating and headache (Steiner 2001) [S].

Switching off the cycle. In a meta-analysis, GnRH analogues were more effective than placebo for PMS (SMD −1.19), and adding back hormones to reduce side effects did not reduce the benefit (Wyatt 2004) [S]. Official drug information for one GnRH agonist, leuprolide, lists hot flashes and new or worsening depression among side effects and says it may decrease bone density (MedlinePlus: leuprolide injection) [Reference], so these are specialist treatments; see G39.

Experimental options. Ulipristal acetate improved premenstrual symptom scores by 41% vs 22% on placebo in one trial of 95 women with confirmed PMDD (Comasco 2021) [P]. It needs repeating before it can be relied on. Sepranolone did not beat placebo on its main outcome in 206 women (Bäckström 2021) [P].

What's untested in ADHD. An expert review of female ADHD notes that pharmacological research and tailored treatments for hormonal transitions are lacking (Kooij 2025) [P]. We did not find a trial of any PMDD treatment in women with ADHD and PMDD in our search (last updated October 2026). We also did not find data on whether combining an SSRI or hormonal treatment with ADHD medication changes how well either works. This doesn't mean these treatments won't help you. It means a clinician has to use judgement rather than ADHD-specific trial data.

7. Cycle-adjusted stimulant dosing: a 9-person case series, so talk to a clinician

Many women describe their ADHD medication seeming to "wear off" before their period. The idea of raising the dose that week is spreading online. Two things are easy to blur here: women report that ADHD and mood symptoms get worse premenstrually, but we did not find a study in our search that measured whether the medicine itself works less well at that time.

The published evidence is one case series. In 9 consecutive women treated in one ADHD clinic for ADHD and co-occurring conditions (including depression and PMDD), who described premenstrual worsening or an insufficient effect of their usual dose, raising the stimulant dose in the premenstrual week improved inattention, irritability and energy, with minimal side effects over 6–24 months, and all chose to continue (de Jong 2023) [P].

That is encouraging, but nine people, with no comparison group and no blinding, cannot tell us whether it works, for whom, or how safe it is. We did not find a randomised trial in our search.

Practical tip: medication changes are made only with your prescriber, so don't change your dose on your own. If you notice a premenstrual dip, track it daily for two cycles alongside your period dates and medication times, and take that chart to your prescriber.

8. Safety: suicidal thoughts and crisis resources

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait for your period to start or for the feelings to ease. See help.

PMDD is linked to a higher risk of suicidal thoughts and attempts. This is one of the main reasons to take it seriously.

  • A systematic review included 13 studies and meta-analysed 10. Compared with women without premenstrual disturbances, women with PMDD had higher odds of suicide attempt (odds ratio 6.97, 95% CI 2.98–16.29) and suicidal thoughts (odds ratio 3.95, 2.97–5.24) (Prasad 2021) [S]. The authors support routine suicide-risk assessment for women with moderate-to-severe premenstrual disturbance (Prasad 2021) [S].
  • In a Swedish matched cohort study, 67,748 women with premenstrual disorders (identified from hospital and outpatient diagnoses and drug dispensing) were compared with 338,740 matched women. They had no higher death rate overall, but a higher rate of death by suicide (hazard ratio 1.92, 1.43–2.60) (Opatowski 2024) [S]. The suicide hazard ratio was higher when diagnosis came before age 25 (3.84, 95% CI 1.18–12.45, a wide interval) (Opatowski 2024) [S].
  • ADHD on its own is also linked to suicidal thoughts and behaviour. A meta-analysis of 57 studies found ADHD associated with suicide attempts (OR 2.37), suicidal thoughts (OR 3.53) and death by suicide (OR 6.69) (Septier 2019) [E].
  • Hormone changes themselves may play a part. In a small crossover trial, keeping hormone levels steady around the period reduced suicidal thoughts, hopelessness and rejection sensitivity compared with placebo (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022) [P].

These are group-level associations. They can't predict what will happen to any one person, and they are a reason to ask about suicidal thoughts directly and to get help early, not a forecast. We did not find a study of suicidality specifically in women with both ADHD and PMDD in our search.

Practical tip: if you are having suicidal thoughts now, get help now, using the box above. Don't wait to see whether they ease with your period. If dark thoughts come at the same point in each cycle, write the dates down: it gives your clinician a pattern to act on. Make a short safety plan for those days while you are well: who you will contact, and what you will do.

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. Start daily tracking now. Use a DRSP-based tracker for at least two full cycles. Add a line for your ADHD symptoms. G04 shows how. You don't have to finish tracking before seeing a clinician, and urgent help never waits for a chart.
  2. Don't rely on memory or a one-off quiz. An online PMDD screen can tell you it's worth tracking. It can't tell you that you have PMDD.
  3. Log what changes. Record ADHD medication and timing, hormonal contraception, sleep and how heavy your period is.
  4. Bring the chart, not a summary. Clinicians can score daily ratings. They can't score "it's bad before my period".
  5. Name both possibilities. Ask: "Could this be PMDD, or my ADHD (or mood) getting worse before my period, or both?"
  6. Plan for the hard days. If your symptoms are cyclical, put a safety plan and lighter commitments into the days your chart says are hardest.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Whether your daily ratings meet the pattern for PMDD, PME, or both.
  • Treatment options for PMDD: SSRIs (daily or luteal-phase only; both have evidence, and the schedule is decided together), hormonal options and whether they are safe for you, and what to try next if the first step doesn't help.
  • Any premenstrual change in how well your ADHD medication works, before changing a dose.
  • How any new treatment might interact with your ADHD medication.
  • Suicidal thoughts, especially if they come and go with your cycle.
  • If you use hormonal contraception: any change in your mood since starting it.

What we don't know yet

  • The true size of the ADHD–PMDD link. We did not find a study that used daily, prospective ratings over two cycles in women with ADHD in our search (last updated October 2026).
  • Whether women with ADHD report premenstrual symptoms differently on one-off questionnaires, for example because of emotion regulation difficulties or trouble separating cyclical from everyday symptoms.
  • How much of the "PMDD" seen in ADHD is actually PME of ADHD, depression or anxiety.
  • Whether PMDD treatments work the same way in women with ADHD. We did not find a trial in this group.
  • Whether ADHD medication helps, worsens or doesn't affect PMDD. We did not find data in our search.
  • Whether premenstrual stimulant dose changes work and are safe. One 9-person case series is the only evidence.
  • The autism question. One study found raised provisional PMDD rates in autistic women (Groenman 2026) [P], another found no link with autistic traits (Tsuji 2026) [P], and a third found PMDD not significantly more common in autistic women (14.3% vs 9.5%) (Groenman 2022) [P].
  • Suicide risk specifically in ADHD plus PMDD. We did not find a study in our search.
