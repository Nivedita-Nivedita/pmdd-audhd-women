Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • In adults, stimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamines including lisdexamfetamine) and atomoxetine are the medicines that most clearly reduce ADHD symptoms in the short term (Ostinelli 2025) [E]. Cochrane reviews rate much of the adult evidence low or very low certainty, and the trials lean male (Castells 2018) [S].

  • Side effects such as appetite loss, stomach upset and a rise in pulse are common and worth tracking. In large records studies, only 48% of adults aged 25 or over were still on medication a year after starting, and patterns were similar for women and men (Brikell 2024) [E].

  • Whether women respond differently, and whether medication "stops working" before a period, has barely been studied. The best evidence is one review of inconsistent findings and a 9-woman case series (Kok 2020; de Jong 2023) [P].

Evidence grade: ESTABLISHED (E) for short-term benefit of stimulants and atomoxetine. SUPPORTED (S) for side effects and stopping rates. PRELIMINARY (P) for sex differences, cycle-adjusted dosing and shortages. Headline grade P, the weakest essential claim. Talk to a clinician about: which medicine fits your health history, how you'll review it, what to monitor, and what to do if it seems weaker before your period. Not medical advice. Crisis support →

This guide does not tell you which medicine to take, at what dose, or whether to start or stop. It sets out what the adult studies found so that you can have a better-informed conversation with your prescriber. Any change to your medication should be made with them.

A few terms first (definitions, not evidence claims):

  • Meta-analysis / network meta-analysis: a study that pools many trials. A network meta-analysis also compares medicines that were never tested head to head.
  • SMD (standardised mean difference): a way of expressing how much a treatment changed symptoms compared with placebo (a dummy pill). A minus sign here means symptoms went down. The further from zero, the bigger the effect.
  • RR / OR (relative risk / odds ratio): compare the chance of something happening on the medicine with placebo. 1.00 means no difference; 2.00 means roughly twice as likely.
  • Certainty of evidence: Cochrane's judgement of how much we can trust a result. "Low" or "very low" means future studies could easily change it.

1. What the options are and how well they work

The network meta-analyses. Pooling 133 double-blind trials, amphetamines, methylphenidate, bupropion and atomoxetine (but not modafinil) beat placebo in adults at around 12 weeks. Weighing benefit against side effects, the authors judged amphetamines the preferred first choice for adults in the short term (Cortese 2018) [E]. A newer analysis of 113 adult trials (14,887 adults, 45.6% female) found that only stimulants and atomoxetine reduced core symptoms at 12 weeks on both self-rated and clinician-rated scales (Ostinelli 2025) [E].

The Cochrane reviews are more cautious. They find symptom reductions but rate the adult evidence low or very low certainty (Boesen 2022; Castells 2018) [S]; (Cândido 2021) [P]. For long-acting methylphenidate, the main reasons were high risk of bias, short trials and limited generalisability (Boesen 2022) [S].

Neither has good long-term data. Data at 26 and 52 weeks were insufficient (Cortese 2018) [E], and longer-term evidence is lacking (Ostinelli 2025) [E].

Why it may matter beyond symptoms. In a Swedish study of 148,578 people (41.3% female), starting ADHD medication within 3 months of diagnosis was associated with lower 2-year mortality (39.1 vs 48.1 per 10,000; HR 0.79), driven by unnatural causes such as injuries (HR 0.75) (Li 2024) [S]. This is an association from observational data, not proof that medication caused the difference.

Expert groups agree that effective, evidence-based treatments exist for adult ADHD, and that more research on gender differences is needed (Kooij 2018) [E]. A UK consensus on girls and women recommends a lifespan approach to care (Young 2020) [E].

2. Stimulants: methylphenidate and amphetamines

Medicine What the adult review found Certainty / who was studied
Extended-release (long-acting) methylphenidate Self-rated symptoms SMD -0.37; investigator-rated SMD -0.42; small effect on quality of life (SMD -0.15); no effect on days missed at work; any adverse event RR 1.27 (Boesen 2022) [S] 24 trials, 5066 participants; all evidence very low certainty
Immediate-release (short-acting) methylphenidate Low to very low certainty that it reduces symptoms; more gastrointestinal (stomach and gut) complications (RR 1.96) and loss of appetite (RR 1.77) than placebo; side effects poorly reported (Cândido 2021) [P] 10 trials, 497 adults
Amphetamines, including lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse/Elvanse) Clinician-rated SMD -0.90; patient-rated SMD -0.51; lisdexamfetamine SMD -1.06. Did not improve staying in treatment; more people withdrew because of side effects (RR 2.69). Doses at the end of the studies did not appear to differ in effect (Castells 2018) [S] 19 studies, 2521 participants; low or very low quality; 57.2% male

Two things stand out for women. First, the adult amphetamine trials were mostly men (57.2% male) (Castells 2018) [S]. Second, notice the gap between how much clinicians and patients saw change: in the amphetamine trials, clinician ratings showed a bigger effect than patients' own ratings (Castells 2018) [S]. Your own sense of whether a medicine helps is part of the picture.

3. Non-stimulants: atomoxetine, viloxazine, guanfacine

  • Atomoxetine had modest benefit in adults (patient-rated SMD -0.33; clinician-rated SMD -0.40). More people stopped it than stopped placebo, both for any reason (OR 1.39) and because of side effects (OR 2.57), and the authors judged its benefit-risk balance poor (Cunill 2013) [S]. In the larger network analysis it was less acceptable than placebo (Ostinelli 2025) [E].
  • Viloxazine (extended-release) reduced investigator-rated symptoms more than placebo at 6 weeks in one trial of 374 adults, with effects visible by week 2. Common side effects were insomnia (14.8%), fatigue (11.6%), nausea (10.1%), decreased appetite (10.1%), dry mouth (9.0%) and headache (9.0%); 9.0% vs 4.9% stopped because of side effects (Nasser 2022) [P].
  • Guanfacine (extended-release) improved symptoms more than placebo at week 10 in a trial of 201 Japanese adults (effect size 0.52). More people had side effects (81.2% vs 62.0%) and stopped because of them (19.8% vs 3.0%); the main ones were sleepiness, thirst, low blood pressure, dizziness on standing and constipation (Iwanami 2020) [P].

Who they suit. None of the reviews in our library tested which kind of person does better on a non-stimulant. One review suggests atomoxetine may be promising for girls and women, but says the finding needs confirming (Kok 2020) [P]. Choosing between them is a conversation about your health history, other medicines and priorities.

4. Titration in practice: starting, reviewing, switching

"Titration" means adjusting the dose step by step, with check-ins, to find what works with tolerable side effects.

We could not find a peer-reviewed adult titration guideline with an abstract we could check, so we can't cite a specific schedule. National guidelines exist but are not in our library.

What the data do show is that trying more than one medicine is common and is linked with staying on treatment. In 23,916 Danish adults, those who switched medication two or more times were less likely to stop altogether (OR 0.53 for two switches; 0.26 for three or more) (Ishøy 2024) [S]. That is an association: people who keep working with a prescriber may differ in other ways. Still, it suggests that a first medicine that doesn't suit you is not the end of the road.

Practical tip: ask at the start how titration will work in your clinic. Who do you contact between appointments? How soon is the first review? What would make them adjust or switch?

5. Common side effects and what to monitor

Appetite and weight. Short-acting methylphenidate caused more appetite loss (RR 1.77) and stomach upset (RR 1.96) than placebo (Cândido 2021) [P]. In adults without ADHD, a single dose of methylphenidate reduced appetite and snack intake in women and normal-weight men, but not in men with obesity (Davis 2011) [P]. If eating regularly is already hard for you, see G25.

Sleep. In adults, studies that measured sleep properly during stimulant treatment found mixed effects, and some found sleep improved (Surman 2022) [P]. Stimulants may push the body clock later (Snitselaar 2017) [S]. Insomnia was the commonest side effect of viloxazine (Nasser 2022) [P]. More on sleep timing in G07.

Heart rate and blood pressure. In short-term trials, amphetamines, atomoxetine, lisdexamfetamine, methylphenidate and viloxazine all raised blood pressure and pulse. In adults, pulse rose by 4.37 bpm (methylphenidate) to 5.8 bpm (viloxazine) versus placebo, with stimulants no worse than atomoxetine or viloxazine; guanfacine lowered values (Farhat 2025) [S]. The authors advise monitoring blood pressure and pulse with any ADHD medicine (Farhat 2025) [S]. Across 19 observational studies, ADHD medication was not significantly linked to cardiovascular disease in adults, but a modest rise in heart-rhythm problems could not be ruled out, and evidence in women needs more study (Zhang 2022) [S]. If you have POTS or a fast heart rate, see G38 and G37.

Mood. Stimulants and atomoxetine eased emotional ups and downs in adults (SMD -0.41), about half as much as they eased core ADHD symptoms (SMD -0.8) (Moukhtarian 2017) [S]. So medication may not settle emotional swings fully on its own; see G08.

Side-effect tracker

Practical tip: this is a simple template, not a validated tool. Fill in one row a day for the first few weeks, and after any change, and bring it to your review. Add your period dates so patterns around your cycle show up (see G04 for a full cycle tracker).

Date Medicine and time taken Cycle day (day 1 = first day of period) Focus / getting started (0-3) Appetite: ate breakfast? lunch? Sleep: time asleep, hours Heart racing or pounding? (Y/N) Pulse or BP if you measure it Mood / irritability (0-3) Headache, stomach, dry mouth, other When it wore off

Scale: 0 = none or no problem, 3 = severe or a big problem. Keep it short; three or four columns filled in every day beats a perfect sheet you abandon.

6. Do women respond differently?

The honest answer is: possibly, in some ways, but we don't know enough to say how.

The only systematic review on this found 21 studies. Each of the 14 that looked at medication effects found at least one difference between the sexes, but the findings were inconsistent, and the authors say their robustness needs further investigation (Kok 2020) [P]. They suggest single daily methylphenidate may not be optimal for girls and that atomoxetine may be promising for girls and women (Kok 2020) [P]. Girls received fewer prescriptions than boys, a gap no longer seen in adults (Kok 2020) [P].

Two small studies hint at specific differences:

  • In 24 adults with heart tracings (ECGs) before and after treatment, only women had a lengthened QT interval (a heart-rhythm measure) on atomoxetine, and in women it tracked the dose (Suzuki 2017) [P].
  • Methylphenidate suppressed appetite differently by sex and weight in adults without ADHD (Davis 2011) [P].

Women are not more likely to stop medication. In over a million new users, patterns were similar across sex (Brikell 2024) [E]. In Denmark, women were slightly more likely to stay on it over five years (29% vs 23.5%) (Ishøy 2024) [S].

7. The menstrual cycle: "it stops working before my period"

Hormones and symptoms. In 32 regularly cycling young women who gave daily saliva samples and symptom ratings for 35 days, lower oestrogen together with higher progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the next day, especially in women high in impulsivity (Roberts 2018) [P]. Symptoms seemed to rise just after a period starts and just after ovulation (Roberts 2018) [P].

Adjusting the dose before a period. In 9 women with ADHD and premenstrual worsening, raising the stimulant dose in the premenstrual week improved inattention, irritability and energy, with minimal side effects over 6-24 months; all chose to continue (de Jong 2023) [P]. With nine people and no comparison group, this can't show that it works or is safe in general.

What hasn't been tested is whether the medicine itself works less well at certain cycle phases, or whether ADHD symptoms rise and the same dose simply covers less. No study in our library has measured stimulant levels or effects across the cycle in women with ADHD.

For more, see G05 (ADHD and PMDD) and, for clinicians, G19.

Practical tip: don't change your dose on your own. Track two cycles (period dates, medication times, how well it works each day) and take the chart to your prescriber.

9. Stopping, restarting and sticking with it

Stopping is common, and it is not a personal failure.

  • Within a year of starting, only 48% of adults aged 25 or over, and 39% of young adults, were still on treatment (Brikell 2024) [E].
  • Many people restart. At 5 years, counting restarts, 30-40% of adolescents and adults were covered by treatment in most countries (Brikell 2024) [E].
  • In one US health system, only 42% of adults renewed their first stimulant prescription on time (Biederman 2020) [P].
  • Early stopping in adults ranged from 38% to 68% between countries, with few consistent clinical predictors and limited sex differences, which points to health-system factors (Brikell 2026) [S].
  • In Denmark, adults aged 31-50 were less likely to stop (Ishøy 2024) [S].

These studies record that people stopped, not why.

10. Shortages

Research on shortages is limited to US qualitative studies. In posts on r/ADHD during the US shortage, people described effects on thinking, mood and daily functioning, barriers such as complex systems, unsupportive providers and cost, and coping by changing or skipping doses, saving medication, caffeine, nicotine, meditation and exercise (Shrestha 2025) [P]. In interviews, people described harm to work, study and personal life; controls meant to reduce non-medical use made access harder, and some sought medication through unofficial channels (Johnson 2024) [P].

These studies describe what people did, not what was safe or effective. No study in our library measured outcomes of rationing, switching or substitutes during a shortage.

Practical tip: if your medicine is hard to get, contact your prescriber and pharmacist early rather than rationing alone. Options to ask about may include another strength, formulation or brand of the same medicine, a different pharmacy, or a different medicine. Medication from unofficial sources may not be what it claims to be.

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. Write down what you want medication to change. Concrete goals ("I start work before 10", "I stop missing bills") make reviews easier.
  2. Use the tracker in section 5 for the first few weeks and after any change, with your cycle days.
  3. Expect adjustment. A first medicine that doesn't suit you is common.
  4. Don't stop suddenly or change doses on your own. Talk to your prescriber first.

Questions to bring to your prescriber

Practical tip: these are questions, not recommendations. Pick the ones that matter to you.

  • Which medicine are you suggesting first, and why for me?
  • How will we adjust it, and when is the first review?
  • Which side effects should I expect, and which mean I should contact you?
  • Should my pulse and blood pressure be checked? What if my appetite or sleep suffers?
  • Does it interact with my other medicines, including contraception or antidepressants?
  • If it seems to work less well before my period, what should I track and bring to you?
  • If it doesn't suit me, what would we try next?
  • If I'm planning a pregnancy, or might become pregnant, what's our plan? (see G26)
  • What should I do if my pharmacy can't fill the prescription?
  • If I want to stop or take a break, how should I do it?

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Which medicine suits your health history (heart, POTS, blood pressure, eating, sleep), and how it will be reviewed.
  • Pulse and blood pressure checks with any ADHD medicine.
  • Premenstrual worsening, with two cycles of tracking, and mood swings medication doesn't touch.
  • Pregnancy plans, and what to do during a shortage.

What we don't know yet

  • Sex-specific efficacy in adults. No adult trial or meta-analysis in our library reports results separately for women. The one review of sex differences mixes girls and women, and its findings are inconsistent (Kok 2020) [P].
  • How stimulants are processed in women's bodies. We found no study of stimulant pharmacokinetics by sex in adults with ADHD.
  • Titration. We found no peer-reviewed adult titration guideline we could cite.
  • Cycle-adjusted dosing. There is one 9-person case series and no randomised trial.
  • Wearing off and tolerance. We found no adult-specific peer-reviewed study.
  • Long-term benefit and safety. Trials are mostly 12 weeks or shorter.
  • Shortages. Only US qualitative studies exist; there are no outcome data for adults or women.
  • Why people stop. Registry studies count stopping but can't tell us the reasons.

Safety and crisis resources

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

If your mood drops sharply after starting, changing or stopping medication, or you notice frightening thoughts, contact your prescriber or GP promptly. See G08 and G05.

Popular claims checked

  • adhdmed-vyvanse-stops-working-before-period: "Vyvanse (or other ADHD medication) stops working before your period." Verdict: partly true (preliminary). Symptoms appear to shift with hormones (Roberts 2018) [P], and a 9-woman case series found premenstrual dose increases helped (de Jong 2023) [P], but no study has measured whether the medicine itself works less well across the cycle.
  • adhdmed-stimulants-work-differently-in-women: "ADHD medications work differently in women." Verdict: partly true (preliminary). Sex differences were found in every effect study in one review, but inconsistently (Kok 2020) [P]. Stopping rates are similar (Brikell 2024) [E] or slightly lower in women (Ishøy 2024) [S].
  • raise-stimulant-dose-before-period: "Your ADHD meds stop working before your period, so you should increase the dose that week." Verdict: partly true (preliminary). A promising idea from a 9-person case series, to be tried only with a prescriber (de Jong 2023) [P].
  • pots-stimulants-dangerous: "Stimulants are dangerous if you have POTS." Verdict: overstated (preliminary). Covered in G38.
