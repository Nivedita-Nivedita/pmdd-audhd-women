For clinicians: screening women with high IQ

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

Educational clinician resource, pending clinical review. This is a reading aid, not a diagnostic protocol. Diagnosis still rests on the full DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria (onset, persistence, pervasiveness across settings, impairment and exclusion of better explanations) assessed by a qualified clinician.

In short

  • Childhood referral for ADHD in girls has tracked externalising problems more closely than it does in boys. Women who present as adults with internalising problems and high achievement are the group most likely to have been missed.

  • In high-IQ adults, normal-range neuropsychological scores don't exclude ADHD. Weight real-world impairment, informant data and comparison with similar-ability peers.

  • The ASRS and WURS were validated in general-population, managed-care and clinic samples, not specifically in high-IQ adults or women who compensate heavily. A low score doesn't rule ADHD out.

Evidence grade: SUPPORTED (S). High-IQ ADHD validity and test-rating dissociation: S/E. IQ-masking on tests: P. Screener performance in this group: not established (see "What we don't know yet").

Talk to a clinician about… (patient-facing prompts) see the section near the end.

Not medical advice.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait for it to pass. See crisis support.

Tags: [E] established · [S] supported · [P] preliminary · [U] popular but unsupported. Practice point and Practical tip paragraphs are clinical suggestions, not graded evidence.

1. Referral bias: girls need more externalising problems to be diagnosed

  • In 19,804 Swedish twins with parent ratings at age 9, hyperactivity/impulsivity and conduct problems were stronger predictors of clinical diagnosis (conduct: odds ratio 1.43) and medication (conduct: odds ratio 2.20) in girls than in boys Mowlem 2018 [S].
  • Girls meet criteria at just under half the rate of boys, approaching parity by adulthood. They show more inattention and internalising problems, and are often overlooked because of less overt symptoms and compensation Hinshaw 2021 [S].
  • Females are diagnosed later and less often than males, and are less often medicated. Proposed contributors include the fit of the criteria, thresholds, diagnostic overshadowing, presentation and compensatory behaviour Martin 2024 [S].
  • The UK ADHD Partnership consensus names symptom differences, gender bias, comorbidity and compensatory strategies as barriers. It recommends attention to the internalised presentation and a lifespan model Young 2020 [E].

Adult ADHD as a whole is under-diagnosed and under-treated, according to a consensus that also flags gender differences as under-researched Kooij 2018 [E].

2. Screeners' limits

What the validation studies show: - ASRS v1.1 six-item screener (DSM-IV wording). In 154 US survey respondents with blind clinical ratings, the unweighted six-item screener had sensitivity 68.7% and specificity 99.5%, outperforming the 18-item ASRS Kessler 2005 [E]. - ASRS for DSM-5. A six-question version derived by machine learning in 637 participants had sensitivity 91.4% and specificity 96.0% in weighted general-population data. In a clinical sample with 57.7% prevalence, sensitivity was 91.9% and specificity 74.0% Ustun 2017 [E]. - WURS-25. In 81 adult outpatients with ADHD, 100 adults without a diagnosis and 70 adults with unipolar depression, a cut-off of 46 or higher correctly identified 86% of patients with ADHD, 99% of the comparison adults and 81% of the depressed adults Ward 1993 [S]. It is a retrospective childhood self-report. The abstract does not report the sex of the sample, so the common statement that it was "developed in men" is unverified. - DIVA 2.0 interview. In 40 adult outpatients with ADHD, DIVA 2.0 diagnoses agreed 100% with diagnoses from the CAADID interview Ramos-Quiroga 2016 [P]. This is a small concurrent-validity study, not a test in women or high-IQ adults.

None of these samples was high-IQ or selected for heavy compensation, and the abstracts do not report accuracy separately for women. We did not find an eligible study of screener cut-offs in gifted adults, or a validated ADHD-specific masking measure, in our search (last updated October 2026).

Context from other studies: - Comorbid depression and anxiety are very common in adult ADHD (section 5), so screeners used in mood or anxiety clinics operate amid high symptom overlap. - Compensation is a recognised barrier to recognition in women Young 2020 [E]. That makes under-endorsement on self-report plausible, but it has not been quantified.

Practice point. Treat a negative screen as uninformative when history, informant data or a high-cost compensation pattern suggest ADHD. Use the WURS alongside school records and an informant where these exist, not instead of them.

3. Test performance vs real-world impairment

  • Across 20 studies, only 68 of 286 correlations between EF tests and EF ratings were significant (median r = .19). Tests index cognitive efficiency; ratings index success in goal pursuit Toplak 2012 [E].
  • In hyperactive children followed to a mean age of 27, self-reported EF deficits predicted impairment across 10 life-activity domains and 12 occupational measures better than EF tests. Most adults with persistent ADHD were impaired on ratings, but few on tests Barkley 2011 [S].
  • In 51 treatment-naive adults with ADHD split at IQ 110, against 33 controls, the higher-IQ group differed from controls only on CPT commission errors, while the standard-IQ group was impaired across many measures Milioni 2016 [P]. The sample was small, and 110 is not a gifted cut-off.
  • Neuropsychological scores did not predict work functioning. Work problems mainly took the form of not meeting one's own standards and potential Fuermaier 2021 [S].

4. Use IQ-matched comparisons and everyday EF ratings

  • High-IQ (≥120) adults with ADHD (n = 64) performed worse than high-IQ controls (n = 53) on WCST, Stroop, ROCF, CVLT and CPT, yet scored in the average range. Several test scores predicted real-world functioning Antshel 2010 [S].
  • In 157 adults with ADHD and IQ ≥120, 73% were impaired on at least 5 of 8 normed EF measures combining test indices and self-report. There was no control group Brown 2009 [S].
  • High-IQ adults with ADHD had lower quality of life, poorer occupational and family functioning, more accidents, and more depression, OCD and GAD than high-IQ controls. ADHD was also more common in their relatives Antshel 2008 [S].
  • A systematic review found limited evidence that ADHD is valid at high intelligence. It notes that compensatory strategies may increase the risk of a missed diagnosis Rommelse 2016 [S].

Practice point (our inference from the studies above, not a tested protocol): - Interpret test scores against the patient's own ability level, not population means. - Add a standardised everyday-EF rating scale, self- and informant-report (for example the BDEFS, which is proprietary). - Document the cost of compensation: hours, sleep, recovery time.

5. Internalising comorbidity and diagnostic overshadowing

  • A meta-analysis of large studies (550,748 with ADHD vs 14,546,814 without) found pooled odds ratios of 5.0 for anxiety, 4.5 for major depression, 8.7 for bipolar disorder and 4.6 for SUD, with no moderation by sex Hartman 2023 [E].
  • In 40,103 Norwegian adults with ADHD, ADHD-associated excess anxiety, depression, bipolar and personality disorders were larger in women Solberg 2017 [S].
  • In a US national survey, most adults with ADHD were untreated for ADHD, though many had received treatment for other mental or substance disorders Kessler 2006 [S].
  • Overshadowing also runs from ADHD to autism. In children, a prior ADHD diagnosis delayed autism diagnosis by about 3 years Miodovnik 2015 [S]. In another sample the delay was 2.6 years in girls vs 1.5 in boys Kentrou 2019 [S]. These are child data.

Practice point. When anxiety or depression treatment produces only a partial response, ask about lifelong attention, organisation and time-management difficulties that predate the mood episode.

6. Differential: BPD, bipolar, autism, PME

  • Bipolar disorder and BPD. ADHD co-occurs in about 20% of adults with either. BD is episodic, while ADHD and BPD are chronic and trait-like. Abandonment fear, suicidal behaviour and self-harm, chronic emptiness, and stress-related paranoia or dissociation point to BPD Asherson 2014 [S]. BPD impulsivity is more stress-dependent, while ADHD involves more motor impulsivity, and both are highest when the two co-occur Ditrich 2021 [S]. Ecological momentary assessment (repeated real-time mood ratings) has been proposed to help separate the two Moukhtarian 2018 [P].
  • Autism. The pooled prevalence of ADHD in autistic people is 28% Lai 2019 [E]. 21% of youth with ADHD meet the threshold for ASD Hollingdale 2019 [E]. Adults with ADHD camouflage more than comparison adults but less than autistic adults, and camouflaging tracks autistic, not ADHD, traits van der Putten 2024 [P]. In a clinic sample, women with autism were more likely than men to be misdiagnosed at first evaluation Gesi 2021 [P]. If the patient describes social masking, assess for autism.
  • PME. Around 60% of women with mood disorders report premenstrual exacerbation. Treatments effective in PMDD show less or no efficacy in PME Kuehner 2021 [S]. Retrospective reports poorly predict prospective PMDD diagnosis. The C-PASS applied to two or more cycles of DRSP ratings agreed with expert diagnosis in about 98% of 200 women Eisenlohr-Moul 2017 [E]. In 32 young women followed daily, hormone levels predicted next-day ADHD symptoms Roberts 2018 [P].
  • Late-onset presentations. In 239 people followed with 8 assessments from childhood to mean age 24.40, about 95% of apparent late-onset cases were excluded. Symptoms confined to heavy substance use were the most common reason Sibley 2018 [S].
  • Cognitive disengagement syndrome (formerly sluggish cognitive tempo) is now regarded by an international work group as a distinct syndrome of uncertain nature Becker 2023 [S].

What to do in practice

Practice points 1. Don't stop at a negative screen if the history fits. Proceed to a structured interview, such as the DIVA-5, which covers childhood and adult symptoms with impairment across five domains. The full diagnostic criteria still apply. 2. Seek an informant and school records where available. Look for inattentive descriptors, not only conduct. Missing school reports or no available childhood informant is not automatically disqualifying: assess the developmental evidence you have (the patient's own account, any documents, later-life pattern), record the uncertainty, and do not prompt the patient to reconstruct childhood symptoms to fit criteria. 3. Measure everyday impairment with self- and informant-rated everyday-EF scales, and record the cost of compensation. 4. Read neuropsychology against ability, not population means. 5. Screen the differential systematically: mood (episodic vs trait), BPD features, autism traits, substance use, sleep timing and cycle-linked change. 6. Use prospective daily ratings for cycle-linked symptoms (two or more cycles) before attributing them to PMDD. 7. Ask directly about suicidality and self-harm, especially where BPD features, PMDD or autism are in the differential.

Talk to a clinician about…

Prompts you can give patients: - "My screening score was low, but I compensate heavily. Can we look at my history and everyday functioning?" - "Could my anxiety or depression be sitting alongside ADHD or autism?" - "My symptoms change with my cycle. Can we use daily tracking to tell PMDD from premenstrual exacerbation?"

What we don't know yet

  • Screener accuracy in high-IQ women. The ASRS and WURS validation samples were not high-IQ or heavily compensating women, and we did not find an eligible study of screener cut-offs in gifted adults or in women who compensate. Whether the WURS was developed mainly in men is unverified.
  • IQ-matched norms. The recommendation to compare against similar-ability peers is an inference from case-control studies Antshel 2010 [S]. We did not find validated IQ-adjusted norms in our search.
  • Masking in ADHD. We did not find a validated ADHD-specific camouflaging measure in our search. Existing camouflaging scores track autistic traits van der Putten 2024 [P].
  • Sex-specific high-IQ data. The high-IQ ADHD studies above did not report results separately for women in the abstracts we checked.
  • DIVA-5. We did not find a validation study of DIVA-5 in our search; the validation we found is for DIVA 2.0 in 40 outpatients Ramos-Quiroga 2016 [P].

Popular claims checked

  • high-iq-protects-against-adhd: False (supported).
  • high-iq-masks-adhd-on-tests: Partly true (preliminary).
  • girls-with-adhd-get-missed: Mostly true (supported).
  • adhd-first-treated-as-anxiety-or-depression: Mostly true (supported). We did not find a precise misdiagnosis rate in our search.
  • online-quiz-can-diagnose-adhd-or-autism: False (established).
  • premenstrual-worsening-means-pmdd: Overstated (established).
