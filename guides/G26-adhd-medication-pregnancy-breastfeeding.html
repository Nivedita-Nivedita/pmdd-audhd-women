Guides › G26

Women, Clinicians

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short - Large studies of millions of pregnancies are broadly reassuring about ADHD medication in pregnancy. They do show a few small, specific signals: a possible small rise in heart defects with methylphenidate, and small rises in preeclampsia and preterm birth, mainly with longer use. Modafinil is the clear exception. - Children exposed to ADHD medication before birth were not more likely to be diagnosed with ADHD or autism once the mother's own ADHD was taken into account. - For breastfeeding, there are almost no data: three reported babies. Whether to continue, pause or change medication is a decision to make with your prescriber and your obstetric team, weighing your own health too.

Evidence grade: pregnancy findings S (Supported); breastfeeding P (Preliminary). Headline grade P, the weakest essential claim. · Talk to a clinician about: your plan for medication before and during pregnancy, after birth and while feeding.

Not medical advice. Crisis resources →

This guide does not tell you whether to start, stop or continue any medication. It sets out what the studies found so that you and your clinicians can make that decision together, with your own history in view.

A quick glossary (definitions, not evidence claims):

  • Registry study: a study using national health records, often of hundreds of thousands or millions of people. It shows associations, not causes.
  • Relative risk (RR), odds ratio (OR), hazard ratio (HR): ways of comparing risk between two groups. A value of 1.00 means no difference; above 1.00 means higher risk in the exposed group. These are relative risks. When an outcome is rare to begin with, even a noticeable relative rise is a small absolute change in the number of people affected.
  • 95% confidence interval (CI): the range the true value probably lies in. If it includes 1.00, the study could not rule out "no difference".
  • Preeclampsia: a pregnancy complication involving high blood pressure. Preterm birth: birth before 37 weeks.
  • Confounding by indication: when the reason someone takes a medicine (here, having ADHD) is itself linked to the outcome. This is the biggest trap in this research.

1. Why this question is now common

More women are being diagnosed and treated for ADHD in their reproductive years, so more women face this decision. In US office visits, ADHD diagnoses in adults rose 36% between 2008-2009 and 2012-2013, and diagnosis plus medication rose 29% in female youths (Fairman 2017) [P]. In Swedish women having a first baby in 2007-2014, 1.7% had ADHD defined by medication dispensing (Hesselman 2020) [S]. In Ontario, 2.2% of deliveries in 2013-2023 were to women diagnosed with ADHD before conception (Zipursky 2026) [S].

Many women do change course in pregnancy. Among 3,676 US women with ADHD who were taking stimulants regularly before pregnancy, 41.4% stopped during pregnancy (Xu 2026) [S].

We did not find a study that tracks, over time, how many pregnant women take ADHD medication. That trend is a gap.

2. Birth defects (malformations)

Methylphenidate and amphetamines. The largest study looked at 1,813,894 US Medicaid pregnancies, with a replication in 2,560,069 Nordic pregnancies (Huybrechts 2018) [S]. For methylphenidate taken in the first trimester:

  • Any malformation: adjusted RR 1.11 (95% CI 0.91-1.35). This is not a clear difference.
  • Heart (cardiac) malformations: adjusted RR 1.28 (95% CI 0.94-1.74) in the US data; pooled US-Nordic estimate 1.28 (95% CI 1.00-1.64).

(Huybrechts 2018) [S]

Amphetamines showed no increased risk (RR 1.05 for any malformation; 0.96 for cardiac malformations) (Huybrechts 2018) [S]. The authors conclude there may be a small increase in cardiac malformations with methylphenidate but not with amphetamines (Huybrechts 2018) [S]. Note that the pooled interval only just touches 1.00, so this is a possible signal, not a settled one. The study summary in our library does not give absolute risks, so we cannot tell you how many extra babies per thousand this would mean.

Methylphenidate and atomoxetine, pooled. A meta-analysis of ten studies (16,621,481 pregnant women, 30,830 with ADHD) compared exposed pregnancies with unexposed mothers with ADHD and with the general population (di Giacomo 2024) [S]. Congenital anomalies (OR 1.14, 95% CI 0.83-1.55) and miscarriages (OR 1.01, 0.70-1.47) were not more frequent (di Giacomo 2024) [S]. The authors conclude continuation appears safe for these outcomes but call for further studies (di Giacomo 2024) [S].

The two studies are not in conflict. One looked at all malformations together; the other found a possible signal in one subgroup (heart defects) with one drug.

3. Pregnancy complications: preeclampsia and preterm birth

Here the studies point to small signals, mostly linked to how long the medication is taken.

Study Who Finding
Cohen 2017 [S] US Medicaid 2000-2010: 3,331 amphetamine-dextroamphetamine, 1,515 methylphenidate and 453 atomoxetine pregnancies vs 1,461,493 unexposed Stimulants in the first half of pregnancy: preeclampsia adjusted RR 1.29 (95% CI 1.11-1.49). Not clearly linked to placental abruption, small for gestational age or preterm birth. Continuing after 20 weeks (vs stopping): preterm birth adjusted RR 1.30 (1.10-1.55). Atomoxetine: no association.
Srinivas 2025 [P] 11,075 pregnancies in Norway and Sweden, all in people using ADHD medication in the year before conception Two or more prescriptions in early pregnancy: preterm birth aRR 1.29 (95% CI 1.08, 1.53). One or more: aRR 1.08 (0.93, 1.25). In late pregnancy, each 30 days of cumulative exposure: aHR 1.07 (1.02, 1.12).

Cohen's authors state that the absolute increases are small and that women with significant ADHD should not be counselled to stop treatment on the basis of these findings (Cohen 2017) [S]. Srinivas's authors conclude ADHD medication may modestly increase preterm birth risk, especially with longer use (Srinivas 2025) [P].

One complication for interpretation: ADHD itself, without medication, has been linked to preterm birth and preeclampsia in some registries but not others. G28 sets those studies side by side. In Ontario, the links between maternal ADHD and complications were largely unchanged after removing people who filled a stimulant prescription in pregnancy (Zipursky 2026) [S].

4. Children's development: ADHD and autism

You may have seen claims that stimulants in pregnancy cause ADHD or autism in the child. Three large studies looked at this, and all three found the apparent risk disappears once the mother's own ADHD is accounted for. ADHD is highly heritable, so children of mothers with ADHD are more likely to have it whether or not medication was taken.

Study Who Finding
Suarez 2024 [S] US public and commercial insurance: 4,693 + 2,372 amphetamine/dextroamphetamine-exposed and 786 + 337 methylphenidate-exposed pregnancies, vs millions unexposed Unadjusted: 2- to 3-fold higher risk. After adjustment, amphetamine/dextroamphetamine: autism HR 0.80 (0.56-1.14), ADHD HR 1.07 (0.89-1.28). Methylphenidate: child ADHD HR 1.43 (1.12-1.82), but this did not persist with stricter control for maternal ADHD.
Bang Madsen 2023 [S] Denmark: 898 children whose mothers continued ADHD medication vs 1,270 whose mothers stopped before pregnancy Any developmental disorder: aHR 0.97 (95% CI 0.81 to 1.17); no subcategory raised, including in sibling analyses.
Bang Madsen 2025 [S] Sweden: 861,650 children, pooled with the Danish cohort Methylphenidate, amphetamines or atomoxetine vs mothers who stopped before conception: any neurodevelopmental disorder adjusted HR 0.95 (0.82-1.11); ADHD 0.92 (0.78-1.08); autism 0.86 (0.63-1.18). Pooled Swedish-Danish HR 1.00 (0.83;1.20). Held in sibling comparisons.

Suarez's authors conclude that in utero stimulant exposure is not likely to meaningfully increase childhood neurodevelopmental disorders (Suarez 2024) [S]. The fair one-line summary: three countries, and no increase in child ADHD or autism once maternal ADHD is taken into account [S].

5. Continuing vs stopping: what the comparisons show

This is the question most women actually face, and the evidence is thinner than for single outcomes. The studies do not agree on what they imply.

Western Australia, dexamphetamine (Russell 2024) [P]. 547 women who continued, 297 who stopped partway, and 844 matched unexposed women (births 2003-2018).

  • Continuing throughout pregnancy was not linked to more maternal or newborn problems than stopping partway.
  • Women who stopped had higher odds of threatened miscarriage (the medical term is "threatened abortion") (OR 2.28, 95% CI 1.00, 5.15).
  • Women not exposed at all during pregnancy had lower odds of preeclampsia (OR 0.58) and of their baby needing special care (OR 0.16) than continuers.
  • The authors suggest stopping before pregnancy may be advisable where possible, and note their samples were small.

A systematic review of twelve cohort studies, 2019-2024 (Tai 2025) [S]. Seven of twelve studies found no significant negative effect on mother or child. One found continuing reduced several negative outcomes; one found stopping may increase threatened miscarriage; three reported links with preeclampsia or specific rare birth defects (gastroschisis, omphalocele, transverse limb deficiency). The authors conclude that, for ADHD medications other than modafinil, the benefits of continuing where effective are likely to outweigh the risks.

Your mental health after birth (Xu 2026) [S]. Among 3,676 US women with ADHD on stimulants before pregnancy:

  • 16.2% overall had a new mood or anxiety diagnosis after birth.
  • Continuers looked worse at first (19.3% vs 12.8%), but this disappeared after adjustment.
  • Continuing was not associated with postpartum mental health admissions (RR 1.01, 0.59-1.71) or new mood/anxiety diagnoses (RR 1.30, 0.97-1.74).
  • Pre-existing mental health diagnoses were the strongest predictor of admissions (RR 2.60).

Reading these side by side. Russell's authors lean toward stopping before pregnancy where possible; Tai's and Cohen's authors lean toward continuing where treatment is effective. Both readings come from real data. The difference reflects small samples, different drugs and different comparison groups. It is also why this is a decision about your situation, not a rule. An older review noted that interrupting ADHD treatment in pregnancy may itself carry significant risk (Bolea-Alamanac 2013) [P], though it did not measure those risks.

6. Modafinil is different

Modafinil is sometimes prescribed off-label for ADHD. In the twelve-study review, modafinil significantly increased congenital malformations (Tai 2025) [S]. The review's authors conclude that women taking modafinil should consider stopping before pregnancy (Tai 2025) [S]. If you take modafinil and could become pregnant, this is worth raising with your prescriber soon, rather than waiting for a positive test.

7. Breastfeeding: almost no data

This is the biggest gap in this guide. The only peer-reviewed synthesis in our library found one case report and one case series of breastfeeding exposure to methylphenidate, covering three babies (Bolea-Alamanac 2013) [P]. In those few cases, children developed normally and no adverse effects were reported (Bolea-Alamanac 2013) [P]. Three babies cannot tell us whether a rare problem exists. We found no cohort study of stimulants, atomoxetine or other ADHD medicines in breastfeeding.

Reference monographs on medicines in breast milk exist, but they are not peer-reviewed studies, so they are not in our library. Your prescriber or a pharmacist can consult them with you.

Practical tip: questions you might ask about feeding:

  • "What is known about this specific medicine in breast milk, and where does that information come from?"
  • "Does the timing of my dose relative to feeds matter for this medicine?"
  • "What should I watch for in my baby, and who do I call if I notice it?"
  • "If I pause medication while feeding, what is the plan for my own symptoms, sleep and mood?"

8. Questions to bring to a pre-conception appointment (one-page handout)

Practical tip: print this page, or copy it into your phone. These are prompts, not evidence claims.

Before pregnancy, ask your prescriber and obstetric team: 1. Which medicine am I on, and what do the large studies show for that medicine specifically? (Methylphenidate, amphetamines, atomoxetine and modafinil have different findings.) 2. How much has my medication helped me day to day: driving, work, safety, mood, eating and sleep? What happened the last time I went without it? 3. If I continue, what extra monitoring makes sense (for example, blood pressure for preeclampsia)? 4. If I pause or reduce, what is the plan for the hardest parts of my day, and when do we review it? 5. Do I have, or have I had, depression, anxiety, PMDD or another condition that raises my risk after birth? Who will check in on my mood? 6. If I am on modafinil, what are my options before trying to conceive? 7. What is known about feeding on my medicine, and how will we decide? 8. Who coordinates between my ADHD prescriber, my midwife or obstetrician and my GP?

Write down: your current medicine and dose, how long you have taken it, and any past pregnancies or periods off medication.

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. Start the conversation before pregnancy if you can. Many decisions are easier when they are not made in a rush after a positive test.
  2. Know your medicine's name. The evidence differs by drug, so "my ADHD meds" is not specific enough.
  3. Write down what your medication does for you. A short list of concrete effects (for example, "I can drive safely", "I stop missing appointments") helps your team weigh the other side of the decision.
  4. Ask for a shared plan in writing, covering pregnancy, the weeks after birth and feeding, with a review date.
  5. Plan for your mood after birth regardless of your medication decision. See G27.
  6. Make any change with your prescriber, not on your own. Whatever you decide, decide it together.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Your own balance of benefits and risks for continuing, reducing, pausing or switching medication, before and during pregnancy.
  • The specific findings for your medicine, including the possible methylphenidate heart-defect signal and the modafinil findings.
  • Monitoring for preeclampsia and preterm birth, especially if medication continues into later pregnancy.
  • Feeding plans and what information exists for your medicine.
  • Your mental health history, and a plan for mood and anxiety after birth.

What we don't know yet

  • Breastfeeding. There are no cohort studies of ADHD medication in breastfeeding. Three reported babies is the whole peer-reviewed evidence base in our library.
  • Absolute risks for the methylphenidate heart signal. Our library summary gives only relative risks.
  • The trend in medication use during pregnancy. We found no study tracking how many pregnant women take ADHD medication over time.
  • The cost of stopping. No study in our library measures what happens to women's functioning, safety, work or mood when they stop ADHD medication for pregnancy. One review says interruption may carry significant risk but does not quantify it.
  • Dose and timing. Whether lower doses, or pausing at particular weeks, change any of the small risks is not known.
  • Non-stimulants other than atomoxetine, and newer medicines, have little or no pregnancy data in our library.

Safety and crisis resources

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

Pregnancy and the months after birth are a time of higher risk for depression and anxiety, and a past mental health diagnosis is the strongest predictor of needing hospital care after birth (Xu 2026) [S]. If your mood drops sharply, you can't sleep even when the baby sleeps, or you have frightening thoughts, contact your midwife, GP, obstetric team or crisis line the same day. See G27.

Popular claims checked

These claims were checked against the library in thread 11 but are not yet in the claims ledger.

  • "Stimulants in pregnancy cause ADHD or autism in the child." (Not yet in the claims ledger.) Verdict: unsupported. The apparent risk disappears once maternal ADHD and other factors are accounted for, in US, Danish and Swedish data (Suarez 2024; Bang Madsen 2023; Bang Madsen 2025) [S].
  • "You must stop ADHD medication when pregnant." (Not yet in the claims ledger.) Verdict: unsupported as a rule. We found no guideline-level evidence requiring it. The risks found are small and specific, and Cohen's authors say women with significant ADHD should not be counselled to stop on the basis of their findings (Cohen 2017) [S]. Modafinil is the exception, where review authors conclude women should consider stopping before pregnancy (Tai 2025) [S]. Either way, it is a decision to make with your prescriber.
  • "ADHD medication in pregnancy is completely risk-free." (Not yet in the claims ledger.) Verdict: overstated. Most findings are reassuring, but there are small signals for preeclampsia and preterm birth (Cohen 2017) [S]; (Srinivas 2025) [P], and a possible methylphenidate cardiac signal (Huybrechts 2018) [S].
