ADHD medication in pregnancy and breastfeeding: deciding together

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

This page has not yet had specialist review by an obstetric or perinatal pharmacy specialist.

In short

  • The findings differ by medicine, by outcome and by timing, so no single "safe" or "unsafe" answer applies to all ADHD medicines.

  • Major birth defects (first-trimester use): in large registry studies, no clear rise in malformations overall was found for methylphenidate or for amphetamines. For methylphenidate there was a possible small rise in heart defects; for amphetamines there was not. A meta-analysis pooling methylphenidate and atomoxetine found no clear association with congenital anomalies. Modafinil was linked to more malformations in a 2025 review.

  • Pregnancy and birth complications: in US and Nordic registries, stimulants (results pooled across amphetamine-type medicines and methylphenidate) were linked to a small rise in preeclampsia with use in the first half of pregnancy, and to preterm birth mainly with continued or longer use. Atomoxetine was not clearly linked to these outcomes in the one US study that looked.

  • Longer-term child development: in US, Danish and Swedish registries, children exposed before birth were not clearly more likely to be diagnosed with ADHD or autism once the mother's own ADHD was taken into account.

  • Breastfeeding: the amount and kind of evidence differs a lot by medicine (see the table in section 7). There are milk-level and infant-level measurements for methylphenidate, amphetamines and atomoxetine, very little for viloxazine, and none found for guanfacine.

  • Untreated ADHD has its own risks. Whether to continue, pause or change medication is an individual decision to make with your prescriber and your maternity team, weighing your own health too.

Evidence grade: pregnancy findings S (Supported); breastfeeding P (Preliminary), based on small studies and drug monographs. Headline grade P, the weakest essential claim. · Talk to a clinician about: your plan for medication before and during pregnancy, after birth and while feeding.

Not medical advice. Crisis support →

This guide does not tell you whether to start, stop or continue any medication. It sets out what the studies found so that you and your clinicians can make that decision together, with your own history in view.

A quick glossary (definitions, not evidence claims):

  • Registry study: a study using national health records, often of hundreds of thousands or millions of people. It shows associations, not causes.
  • Risk ratio or relative risk (RR), odds ratio (OR), hazard ratio (HR): ways of comparing risk between two groups. A value of 1.00 means no difference; above 1.00 means higher risk in the exposed group. These are relative risks. When an outcome is rare to begin with, even a noticeable relative rise is a small absolute change in the number of people affected.
  • 95% confidence interval (CI): the range the true value probably lies in. If it includes 1.00, the study could not rule out "no difference".
  • Preeclampsia: a pregnancy complication involving high blood pressure. Preterm birth: birth before 37 weeks.
  • Confounding by indication: when the reason someone takes a medicine (here, having ADHD) is itself linked to the outcome. This is the biggest trap in this research.

1. Why this question is now common

More women are being diagnosed and treated for ADHD in their reproductive years, so more women face this decision. In US office visits, ADHD diagnoses in adults rose 36% between 2008-2009 and 2012-2013, and diagnosis plus medication rose 29% in female youths (Fairman 2017) [P]. In Swedish women having a first baby in 2007-2014, 1.7% had ADHD defined by medication dispensing (Hesselman 2020) [S]. In Ontario, 2.2% of deliveries in 2013-2023 were to women diagnosed with ADHD before conception (Zipursky 2026) [S].

Many women do change course in pregnancy. Among 3,676 US women with ADHD who were taking stimulants regularly before pregnancy, 41.4% stopped during pregnancy (Xu 2026) [S].

We did not find an eligible study in our search (last updated October 2026) that tracks, over time, how many pregnant women take ADHD medication.

2. Major congenital malformations (birth defects)

This section is about structural birth defects after use in the first trimester, when organs form. It does not cover pregnancy complications (section 3) or children's later development (section 4).

Methylphenidate and amphetamines. The largest study looked at 1,813,894 US Medicaid pregnancies, with a replication in 2,560,069 Nordic pregnancies (Huybrechts 2018) [S]. For methylphenidate taken in the first trimester:

  • Any malformation: adjusted risk ratio (RR) 1.11 (95% CI 0.91-1.35). No clear association was found.
  • Heart (cardiac) malformations: adjusted risk ratio 1.28 (95% CI 0.94-1.74) in the US data; pooled US-Nordic estimate 1.28 (95% CI 1.00-1.64).

(Huybrechts 2018) [S]

For amphetamines, no clear increase in risk was found (risk ratio 1.05 for any malformation; 0.96 for cardiac malformations) (Huybrechts 2018) [S]. The authors conclude there may be a small increase in cardiac malformations with methylphenidate but not with amphetamines (Huybrechts 2018) [S]. Note that the pooled interval only just touches 1.00, so this is a possible signal, not a settled one. The study's abstract does not give absolute risks, so we cannot tell you how many extra babies per thousand this would mean.

Methylphenidate and atomoxetine, pooled. A meta-analysis of ten studies (16,621,481 pregnant women, 30,830 with ADHD) compared exposed pregnancies with unexposed mothers with ADHD and with the general population (di Giacomo 2024) [S]. No clear association was found with congenital anomalies (odds ratio 1.14, 95% CI 0.83-1.55) or miscarriage (odds ratio 1.01, 0.70-1.47) (di Giacomo 2024) [S]. The authors conclude continuation appears safe for these outcomes but call for further studies (di Giacomo 2024) [S].

The two studies are not in conflict. The meta-analysis pooled methylphenidate with atomoxetine and looked at all anomalies together; the registry study looked at each stimulant separately and found a possible signal in one subgroup (heart defects) with one medicine, methylphenidate. Neither study gives malformation results for lisdexamfetamine separately, and we did not find separate malformation results for guanfacine, clonidine or viloxazine in the studies cited here.

3. Obstetric and perinatal outcomes: preeclampsia, preterm birth and birth weight

These outcomes are about the pregnancy and birth, not birth defects. Here the registry studies point to small signals that depend on timing: preeclampsia with use in the first half of pregnancy, and preterm birth mainly with continued or longer use. Most results pool stimulants together rather than reporting each medicine separately.

Study Who Finding
Cohen 2017 [S] US Medicaid 2000-2010: 3,331 amphetamine-dextroamphetamine, 1,515 methylphenidate and 453 atomoxetine pregnancies vs 1,461,493 unexposed Stimulants in the first half of pregnancy: preeclampsia adjusted RR 1.29 (95% CI 1.11-1.49). No clear association with placental abruption, small for gestational age or preterm birth. Continuing after 20 weeks (vs stopping): preterm birth adjusted RR 1.30 (1.10-1.55). Atomoxetine: no clear association found.
Srinivas 2025 [P] 11,075 pregnancies in Norway and Sweden, all in people using ADHD medication in the year before conception Two or more prescriptions in early pregnancy: preterm birth adjusted risk ratio (aRR) 1.29 (95% CI 1.08, 1.53). One or more: aRR 1.08 (0.93, 1.25). In late pregnancy, each 30 days of cumulative exposure: adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) 1.07 (1.02, 1.12).

Which medicines these findings are about: Cohen's preeclampsia and preterm results are for stimulants pooled (mainly amphetamine-dextroamphetamine, plus methylphenidate); atomoxetine was analysed separately. Srinivas's results are for ADHD medication as a group, not for single medicines. On birth weight, Cohen reports small for gestational age (no clear association); the cited studies do not report low birth weight as a separate outcome.

Cohen's authors state that the absolute increases are small and that women with significant ADHD should not be counselled to stop treatment on the basis of these findings (Cohen 2017) [S]. Srinivas's authors conclude ADHD medication may modestly increase preterm birth risk, especially with longer use (Srinivas 2025) [P].

One complication for interpretation: ADHD itself, without medication, has been linked to preterm birth and preeclampsia in some registries but not others. G28 sets those studies side by side. In Ontario, the links between maternal ADHD and complications were largely unchanged after removing people who filled a stimulant prescription in pregnancy (Zipursky 2026) [S].

4. Longer-term child development: ADHD and autism

You may have seen claims that stimulants in pregnancy cause ADHD or autism in the child. Three large registry studies looked at this. Suarez reports amphetamine/dextroamphetamine and methylphenidate separately; the Danish study looks at ADHD medication as a group; the Swedish study covers methylphenidate, amphetamines and atomoxetine, with a pooled result. In all three, no clear increase remained once the mother's own ADHD was accounted for. ADHD is highly heritable, so children of mothers with ADHD are more likely to have it whether or not medication was taken.

Study Who Finding
Suarez 2024 [S] US public and commercial insurance: 4,693 + 2,372 amphetamine/dextroamphetamine-exposed and 786 + 337 methylphenidate-exposed pregnancies, vs millions unexposed Unadjusted: 2- to 3-fold higher risk. After adjustment, amphetamine/dextroamphetamine: autism hazard ratio (HR) 0.80 (0.56-1.14), ADHD HR 1.07 (0.89-1.28). Methylphenidate: child ADHD HR 1.43 (1.12-1.82), but this did not persist with stricter control for maternal ADHD.
Bang Madsen 2023 [S] Denmark: 898 children whose mothers continued ADHD medication vs 1,270 whose mothers stopped before pregnancy Any developmental disorder: adjusted HR 0.97 (95% CI 0.81 to 1.17); no clear increase in any subcategory, including in sibling analyses.
Bang Madsen 2025 [S] Sweden: 861,650 children, pooled with the Danish cohort Methylphenidate, amphetamines or atomoxetine vs mothers who stopped before conception: any neurodevelopmental disorder adjusted HR 0.95 (0.82-1.11); ADHD 0.92 (0.78-1.08); autism 0.86 (0.63-1.18). Pooled Swedish-Danish HR 1.00 (0.83;1.20). Held in sibling comparisons.

Suarez's authors conclude that in utero stimulant exposure is not likely to meaningfully increase childhood neurodevelopmental disorders (Suarez 2024) [S]. The fair one-line summary: three countries, and no clear increase in child ADHD or autism once maternal ADHD is taken into account [S]. These are group-level findings; they cannot rule out small effects for particular medicines, doses or children.

5. Continuing vs stopping: what the comparisons show

This is the question most women actually face, and the evidence is thinner than for single outcomes. The studies do not agree on what they imply.

Western Australia, dexamphetamine (Russell 2024) [P]. 547 women who continued, 297 who stopped partway, and 844 matched unexposed women (births 2003-2018).

  • No clear association was found between continuing throughout pregnancy and more maternal or newborn problems, compared with stopping partway.
  • Women who stopped had higher odds of threatened miscarriage (the medical term is "threatened abortion") (OR 2.28, 95% CI 1.00, 5.15).
  • Women not exposed at all during pregnancy had lower odds of preeclampsia (odds ratio 0.58) and of their baby needing special care (odds ratio 0.16) than continuers.
  • The authors suggest stopping before pregnancy may be advisable where possible, and note their samples were small.

A systematic review of twelve cohort studies, 2019-2024 (Tai 2025) [S]. Seven of twelve studies found no statistically significant negative effect on mother or child, which is not proof of no effect. One found continuing reduced several negative outcomes; one found stopping may increase threatened miscarriage; three reported links with preeclampsia or specific rare birth defects (gastroschisis, omphalocele, transverse limb deficiency). The authors conclude that, for ADHD medications other than modafinil, the benefits of continuing where effective are likely to outweigh the risks. This is the review authors' overall judgement across medicines and studies; it does not tell you how the balance falls for one particular medicine or person.

Your mental health after birth (Xu 2026) [S]. Among 3,676 US women with ADHD on stimulants before pregnancy:

  • 16.2% overall had a new mood or anxiety diagnosis after birth.
  • Continuers looked worse at first (19.3% vs 12.8%), but this disappeared after adjustment.
  • No clear association was found between continuing and postpartum mental health admissions (risk ratio 1.01, 0.59-1.71) or new mood/anxiety diagnoses (risk ratio 1.30, 0.97-1.74).
  • Pre-existing mental health diagnoses were the strongest predictor of admissions (risk ratio 2.60).

Reading these side by side. Russell's authors lean toward stopping before pregnancy where possible; Tai's and Cohen's authors lean toward continuing where treatment is effective. Both readings come from real data. The difference reflects small samples, different drugs and different comparison groups. It is also why this is a decision about your situation, not a rule. An older review noted that interrupting ADHD treatment in pregnancy may itself carry significant risk (Bolea-Alamanac 2013) [P], though it did not measure those risks.

6. Modafinil is different

Modafinil is sometimes prescribed off-label for ADHD. In the twelve-study review, modafinil significantly increased congenital malformations (Tai 2025) [S]. The review's authors conclude that women taking modafinil should consider stopping before pregnancy (Tai 2025) [S]. If you take modafinil and could become pregnant, this is worth raising with your prescriber soon, rather than waiting for a positive test.

7. Breastfeeding: what is known, medicine by medicine

The evidence on breastfeeding is still limited, but there is more of it than a single older review suggests, and it differs by medicine. Three kinds of information answer different questions:

  • Milk-transfer data: how much medicine gets into breast milk, and whether it can be measured in the baby's blood.
  • Short-term infant outcomes: whether babies showed problems such as irritability, poor sleep, feeding difficulty or sleepiness.
  • Long-term development: whether children developed normally over months or years.

Much of what is known comes from small studies and case reports summarised in LactMed, a drug-and-breastfeeding database run by the US National Library of Medicine. LactMed is a clinical reference, not a research study, so we tag it [Reference].

At a glance: what kind of breastfeeding evidence exists for each medicine. "How much evidence" describes the amount of data, not whether a medicine is safe. "Some" still means small numbers of mothers and babies.

Medicine What the lactation data are How much evidence
Methylphenidate Milk levels measured in 3 mothers; medicine not detected in the blood of the 2 infants tested, plus a few more single-infant reports; case reports and small series of breastfed babies followed for growth and development, some to 1 year (LactMed: Methylphenidate [Reference]) Some
Amphetamine (mixed amphetamine salts) Milk levels in 1 mother; infant urine in 1 infant and infant blood in 1 infant; a prospective follow-up of 13 children (7 mixed amphetamine salts, 6 lisdexamfetamine) (LactMed: Amphetamine [Reference]) Some
Dexamfetamine (dextroamphetamine) Milk levels in 4 mothers; infant blood levels in 3 infants (low or undetectable); an old manufacturer communication about 103 mothers with no details; the same 13-child follow-up (LactMed: Dextroamphetamine [Reference]) Some
Lisdexamfetamine No milk or infant levels for lisdexamfetamine itself; relies on dextroamphetamine data, which it is converted to in the body. Outcome reports: 6 children in the 13-child follow-up, a retrospective Swedish study of 17 breastfed infants, and a case report (LactMed: Lisdexamfetamine [Reference]) Some
Atomoxetine Milk levels in 10 mothers; no infant blood levels found; a questionnaire about 11 breastfed infants and manufacturer reports on 2 infants (LactMed: Atomoxetine [Reference]) Some
Viloxazine A manufacturer milk study in 15 women; no infant levels and no infant outcome reports found (LactMed: Viloxazine [Reference]) Very little
Guanfacine LactMed found no published information on use during breastfeeding (LactMed: Guanfacine [Reference]) None found
Clonidine Milk and infant blood levels in 9 mothers and their infants (infant levels about half the mother's); one further infant level; case reports, including one newborn with drowsiness and breathing pauses after exposure in pregnancy and through milk. These data come mostly from use for blood pressure, not ADHD (LactMed: Clonidine [Reference]) Some

Modafinil is not in this table because this guide has not reviewed its breastfeeding information.

Methylphenidate. - Milk transfer: LactMed summarises limited evidence that, at prescribed doses, methylphenidate levels in milk are very low and not detectable in infant blood (LactMed: Methylphenidate [Reference]). - Milk supply: large doses might possibly reduce milk production, especially before breastfeeding is well established (LactMed: Methylphenidate [Reference]). - Infant outcomes: an older systematic review found one case report and one case series covering three breastfed babies; they developed normally and no adverse effects were reported (Bolea-Alamanac 2013) [P]. LactMed states that if a mother needs methylphenidate, it is not a reason to stop breastfeeding (LactMed: Methylphenidate [Reference]). - Long-term development: studied in very few children.

Amphetamines (including dexamfetamine and mixed amphetamine salts). - Short-term infant outcomes: LactMed states that, at prescribed doses, some evidence indicates amphetamine does not adversely affect breastfed infants (LactMed: Amphetamine [Reference]). - Long-term development: LactMed notes that effects on infants' neurological development have not been well studied (LactMed: Amphetamine [Reference]). In a 2024 pilot study, 13 children exposed to amphetamines through breast milk were assessed for development and quality of life; the study reported normal neurodevelopment with no significant adverse effects (Benassayag Kaduri 2024) [P]. LactMed's summary of the same study notes that 5 of the 13 children had a possible reaction (such as sleepiness, crying or restlessness, or colic or constipation), while all had normal developmental scores (LactMed: Amphetamine [Reference]). Its authors conclude amphetamines are likely compatible with breastfeeding but that larger studies are needed (Benassayag Kaduri 2024) [P]. Thirteen children cannot rule out uncommon effects. - Milk supply: large doses might interfere with milk production, especially when breastfeeding is not well established (LactMed: Amphetamine [Reference]). - Monitoring: most manufacturers advise against breastfeeding while taking amphetamine, while some experts say prescribed doses can be used with monitoring of the baby for irritability, poor sleep and feeding difficulty (LactMed: Amphetamine [Reference]).

Lisdexamfetamine. The body converts lisdexamfetamine into dextroamphetamine. LactMed gives the same picture as for amphetamine: some evidence of no adverse effect at prescribed doses, neurological development not well studied, a possible effect of large doses on milk supply, and monitoring of the baby for irritability, poor sleep and feeding difficulty (LactMed: Lisdexamfetamine [Reference]).

Atomoxetine. LactMed reports that amounts in milk are very low after usual doses, based on milk measured in 10 mothers. Manufacturer reports found no serious adverse effects in two breastfed infants, and a questionnaire of mothers of 11 breastfed infants reported no suspected adverse effects or delayed development. It advises monitoring breastfed babies for excess sleepiness (LactMed: Atomoxetine [Reference]). No infant blood levels have been reported, and thirteen infants is little outcome data.

Non-stimulants other than atomoxetine. For viloxazine, LactMed reports low amounts in milk from one manufacturer study but no infant data, and notes another medicine may be preferred while feeding a newborn or premature baby until more is known (LactMed: Viloxazine [Reference]). For guanfacine, LactMed found no published information (LactMed: Guanfacine [Reference]). For clonidine, LactMed's monograph is written mainly about its use for blood pressure; it notes high levels in some breastfed infants and possible effects on milk supply (LactMed: Clonidine [Reference]). Ask your prescriber or a pharmacist about these, and about modafinil, which this guide has not reviewed for breastfeeding.

What this adds up to. For methylphenidate and atomoxetine, milk levels measured in small numbers of mothers were low, but infant-outcome data are sparse. For amphetamines and lisdexamfetamine, short-term reports and small follow-up studies did not find clear developmental problems, though some babies had possible reactions such as sleepiness or restlessness, and long-term development has not been well studied. For viloxazine there is very little information, and for guanfacine we found none in LactMed. None of this means any ADHD medicine is "safe" for every mother and baby. It means you can make the decision with information: with your prescriber and your obstetric or midwifery team, ideally with a pharmacist, and with your own health (sleep, mood, driving, safety) counted too. NICE guidance says clinicians can seek advice from a specialist perinatal mental health service about the risks of psychotropic medicines while breastfeeding (NICE CG192 [Guideline]).

Practical tip: questions you might ask about feeding:

  • "What is known about this specific medicine in breast milk, and where does that information come from (for example, LactMed)?"
  • "Does the timing of my dose relative to feeds matter for this medicine?"
  • "What should I watch for in my baby (for example, irritability, poor sleep, feeding difficulty or sleepiness), and who do I call if I notice it?"
  • "Could this medicine affect my milk supply, and how would we notice?"
  • "If I pause medication while feeding, what is the plan for my own symptoms, sleep and mood?"

8. Questions to bring to a pre-conception appointment (one-page handout)

Practical tip: print this page, or copy it into your phone. These are prompts, not evidence claims.

Before pregnancy, ask your prescriber and obstetric team: 1. Which medicine am I on, and what do the large studies show for that medicine specifically? (Methylphenidate, amphetamines, atomoxetine and modafinil have different findings.) 2. How much has my medication helped me day to day: driving, work, safety, mood, eating and sleep? What happened the last time I went without it? 3. If I continue, what extra monitoring makes sense (for example, blood pressure for preeclampsia)? 4. If I pause or reduce, what is the plan for the hardest parts of my day, and when do we review it? 5. Do I have, or have I had, depression, anxiety, PMDD or another condition that raises my risk after birth? Who will check in on my mood? 6. If I am on modafinil, what are my options before trying to conceive? 7. What is known about feeding on my medicine, and how will we decide? 8. Who coordinates between my ADHD prescriber, my midwife or obstetrician and my GP?

Write down: your current medicine and dose, how long you have taken it, and any past pregnancies or periods off medication.

What you can do

These are practical steps, not evidence claims.

  1. Start the conversation before pregnancy if you can. Many decisions are easier when they are not made in a rush after a positive test.
  2. Know your medicine's name. The evidence differs by drug, so "my ADHD meds" is not specific enough.
  3. Write down what your medication does for you. A short list of concrete effects (for example, "I can drive safely", "I stop missing appointments") helps your team weigh the other side of the decision.
  4. Ask for a shared plan in writing, covering pregnancy, the weeks after birth and feeding, with a review date.
  5. Plan for your mood after birth regardless of your medication decision. See G27.
  6. Make any change with your prescriber, not on your own. Whatever you decide, decide it together.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Your own balance of benefits and risks for continuing, reducing, pausing or switching medication, before and during pregnancy.
  • The specific findings for your medicine, including the possible methylphenidate heart-defect signal and the modafinil findings.
  • Monitoring for preeclampsia and preterm birth, especially if medication continues into later pregnancy.
  • Feeding plans, what is known for your specific medicine, what to watch for in your baby, and any effect on milk supply.
  • Your mental health history, and a plan for mood and anxiety after birth.

What we don't know yet

  • Breastfeeding, long term. Breastfeeding evidence comes from milk-level measurements, case reports, manufacturer reports and one small follow-up study of 13 children. We did not find a large comparison study of children's long-term development in our search (last updated October 2026).
  • Absolute risks for the methylphenidate heart signal. The study's abstract gives only relative risks.
  • The trend in medication use during pregnancy. We did not find an eligible study tracking this over time in our search.
  • The cost of stopping. We did not find an eligible study in our search that measures what happens to women's functioning, safety, work or mood when they stop ADHD medication for pregnancy. One review says interruption may carry significant risk but does not quantify it.
  • Dose and timing. Whether lower doses, or pausing at particular weeks, change any of the small risks is not known.
  • Non-stimulants other than atomoxetine, and newer medicines: we did not find eligible pregnancy studies for these in our search.

Safety and crisis resources

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

Pregnancy and the months after birth are a time of higher risk for depression and anxiety. In women with ADHD who were taking stimulants before pregnancy, a past mental health diagnosis was the strongest predictor of needing hospital care after birth (Xu 2026) [S].

  • Get help now, not later, if you have thoughts of suicide or of harming yourself or your baby. Don't wait to see whether they pass. See crisis help.
  • Postpartum psychosis is a medical emergency. It usually starts suddenly in the first two weeks after birth, with signs such as hallucinations, strange beliefs, feeling very "high" or confused, or rapidly changing mood (NHS: Postpartum psychosis [Reference]). Get an urgent same-day assessment, or call your local emergency number if anyone may be in danger (NHS: Postpartum psychosis [Reference]). NICE says a woman with sudden symptoms suggesting postpartum psychosis should be assessed by a mental health service within 4 hours of referral (NICE CG192 [Guideline]).
  • If your mood drops sharply or you can't sleep even when the baby sleeps, contact your midwife, GP or obstetric team the same day. See G27.

Popular claims checked

These claims were checked against the research cited in this guide; they are not yet in Claim check.

  • You may have heard: "Stimulants in pregnancy cause ADHD or autism in the child." What the research supports: Once maternal ADHD and other factors were taken into account, stimulant use in pregnancy was not clearly linked to ADHD or autism in the child. No clear increase remained once maternal ADHD and other factors were accounted for, in US, Danish and Swedish data (Suarez 2024; Bang Madsen 2023; Bang Madsen 2025) [S].
  • You may have heard: "You must stop ADHD medication when pregnant." What the research supports: Stopping is not a fixed rule in the studies and guidance we found. We did not find a guideline in our search that requires it. The risks found are small and specific, and Cohen's authors say women with significant ADHD should not be counselled to stop on the basis of their findings (Cohen 2017) [S]. Modafinil is the exception, where review authors conclude women should consider stopping before pregnancy (Tai 2025) [S]. Either way, it is a decision to make with your prescriber.
  • You may have heard: "ADHD medication in pregnancy is completely risk-free." What the research supports: ADHD medication in pregnancy is not risk-free, and the findings differ by medicine, outcome and timing. Registry studies found no clear rise in malformations overall for methylphenidate or amphetamines, but small signals for preeclampsia and preterm birth with stimulants (results pooled across stimulants) (Cohen 2017) [S]; (Srinivas 2025) [P], and a possible methylphenidate cardiac signal (Huybrechts 2018) [S].
