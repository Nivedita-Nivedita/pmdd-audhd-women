Gender-diverse and neurodivergent: what the evidence says

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • Autism diagnoses and autistic traits are more common in transgender and gender-diverse people than in cisgender people. This is shown in large datasets and a meta-analysis, though the exact size of the overlap is uncertain.

  • ADHD in trans and gender-diverse people has hardly been studied.

  • Expert consensus for adolescents recommends assessing both together, often over a longer period; we did not find equivalent guidance for adults. Studies linking gender-affirming hormones with better mood are encouraging but low-certainty, and we did not find an eligible study of autistic or ADHD trans people specifically in our search (last updated October 2026).

Evidence grade: SUPPORTED (S) that the autism overlap exists · PRELIMINARY (P) for its size, for ADHD, for assessment guidance and for hormones and mood. Headline grade: PRELIMINARY (P).

Talk to a clinician about… an assessment that takes both your gender and your neurodivergence seriously, without one being used to dismiss the other.

Not medical advice. Crisis support →

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Please don't wait to see whether the feeling passes: crisis support.

This guide is for trans, non-binary and gender-diverse readers, for anyone questioning their gender, and for clinicians. It uses "trans and gender-diverse" as an umbrella term, but mirror the words each person uses for themselves. Three kinds of statement are kept apart here. Identity is how a person describes their own gender; research doesn't validate or invalidate it. Diagnosis is a clinical label (autism, ADHD, gender dysphoria or incongruence) that studies use to select or count people. Causal claims say one thing produces another; none of the studies below test that. The research describes how often diagnoses or traits occur together. It does not tell us why, and this guide does not speculate about the causes of anyone's gender identity.

Tags: [E] established · [S] supported · [P] preliminary · [U] popular but unsupported. Practical tip paragraphs are general guidance, not graded evidence.

1. How big is the overlap?

  • The largest dataset. Across five datasets with 641,860 people, transgender and gender-diverse people had higher rates of autism and of other neurodevelopmental and psychiatric diagnoses than cisgender people Warrier 2020 [S]. On average they also had higher autistic traits and sensory sensitivity, in both autistic and non-autistic groups Warrier 2020 [S]. The authors note implications for access to mental health care and tailored support Warrier 2020 [S].
  • A pooled estimate. In a meta-analysis, 11% of people with gender dysphoria or gender incongruence had an autism diagnosis, and autistic traits were higher than in controls (g = 0.67) Kallitsounaki 2022 [S]. The studies varied a lot. The authors conclude that a link is very likely but its size is uncertain Kallitsounaki 2022 [S]. So treat "11%" as a rough guide, not a fixed number.

Glossary: "gender dysphoria" means distress about a mismatch between your gender and your sex assigned at birth; "gender incongruence" is the related clinical term used in some classifications. These are the terms the studies used to select participants, so their figures describe people in those clinical groups rather than every trans or gender-diverse person.

2. Why clinic comparisons matter, and how little ADHD data exist

  • The comparison group changes the picture. A systematic review of 30 studies found that, in studies using diagnostic criteria, autism prevalence in transgender groups was 6-26% Thrower 2019 [P]. That is higher than in the general population, but no clear difference was found compared with people attending psychiatry clinics Thrower 2019 [P]. The authors rate the evidence for a link as low-level Thrower 2019 [P].
  • ADHD is barely studied. The same review found few studies of ADHD Thrower 2019 [P]. We can't give you a reliable figure for ADHD in trans and gender-diverse people.

What this means: the autism overlap is consistently reported, but how big it looks depends on who it is compared with. These are group figures; they can't tell you whether any one person is autistic or has ADHD. For ADHD, the honest answer is "we don't know yet".

3. Assessment when both are present: expert consensus (adolescents only)

The consensus guidance we found is for adolescents, not adults.

  • In a two-stage Delphi process (a structured way of reaching expert agreement) with 22 experts and a 75% agreement threshold, experts recommended assessing gender dysphoria in autistic adolescents, and autism in adolescents with gender dysphoria Strang 2016 [P].
  • They suggested a longer diagnostic period in which assessment and support overlap Strang 2016 [P].
  • They also recommended attention to social functioning, medical safety, victimisation, school and the move to adulthood Strang 2016 [P].

This is expert opinion about adolescents, not a tested assessment method, and it shouldn't be read as a rule for adult care. We did not find equivalent consensus guidance for adults, or for ADHD, in our search (last updated October 2026). Adults deciding about assessment or care should talk with their own clinicians.

4. Gender-affirming hormones and mood: low-certainty evidence

  • A systematic review of 20 studies found gender-affirming hormone therapy was associated with better quality of life and less depression and anxiety Baker 2021 [P].
  • Certainty is limited by high risk of bias, small samples and confounding, and no conclusions could be drawn about suicide Baker 2021 [P].
  • The review's abstract reports no results specifically for autistic or ADHD trans people, and we did not find a separate eligible study of them in our search (last updated October 2026).

So: the direction of the evidence is positive, but it is weak, and it can't tell you how hormones might affect ADHD symptoms, ADHD medication or autistic experience. Hormone therapy is prescribed and monitored by a clinician, who can go through its risks and how it might interact with other medicines you take.

5. Language, safety and what is not known

This section is mostly practical guidance and gaps.

  • Language. Practical tip: use the person's name, pronouns and identity words, and ask if unsure. Avoid framing either autism or gender identity as a "symptom" of the other. The research above does not support that framing; it shows co-occurrence only.
  • Safety. Experts list victimisation as an area to address for autistic adolescents with gender dysphoria Strang 2016 [P]. We did not find an eligible study measuring victimisation rates in neurodivergent trans adults in our search. For victimisation in autistic people generally, see G35.
  • Mental health. Higher rates of psychiatric diagnoses were found alongside the autism overlap Warrier 2020 [S]. Ask for mental health support directly; you don't need to wait for an autism or gender assessment to finish.

What you can do

  • Ask for an assessment that holds both. Practical tip: "I'm trans (or questioning) and I think I may be autistic or have ADHD. I'd like both to be taken seriously, without one being used to explain away the other."
  • Bring your own words. Practical tip: a short written summary of your sensory experiences, social energy, focus and history helps clinicians who don't know you.
  • Expect assessment to take time. Expert guidance for adolescents suggests a longer, overlapping process Strang 2016 [P].
  • Track mood and focus if you start hormones. Practical tip: a simple weekly log of mood, sleep and concentration gives you and your clinician something to review.
  • Find community. Practical tip: peer groups for autistic and ADHD trans people can be a source of practical advice. They are not a substitute for clinical care.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • An autism or ADHD assessment that is also affirming of your gender.
  • How hormone therapy might interact with ADHD medication or other medicines you take.
  • Low mood, anxiety or suicidal thoughts, whether or not you are on hormones.
  • Safety concerns, including harassment or abuse.

What we don't know yet

  • ADHD in trans and gender-diverse people. The review found few studies Thrower 2019 [P]; we can't give a reliable prevalence figure.
  • Hormones and neurodivergence. Our search (last updated October 2026) did not find an eligible study of gender-affirming hormones and ADHD symptoms, ADHD medication, or mood outcomes in autistic or ADHD trans people.
  • Adults. The assessment consensus we found is for adolescents only.
  • The exact size of the autism overlap. Estimates vary widely and depend on the comparison group.
  • Why the overlap exists. The studies don't test this, and we don't speculate about it.
  • Victimisation and safety in neurodivergent trans adults specifically (not found in our search).

Popular claims checked

  • "Being trans is just a symptom of autism." Verdict: unsupported. The studies show the two occur together more often Warrier 2020 [S]; they do not test whether one causes or explains the other, and experts recommend assessing both Strang 2016 [P].
  • "Most trans people are autistic." Verdict: false. The pooled figure is 11% with autism diagnoses among people with gender dysphoria or incongruence, with high uncertainty Kallitsounaki 2022 [S].
  • "ADHD is just as common in trans people as autism." Verdict: unknown. ADHD has hardly been studied Thrower 2019 [P].
  • "Hormones are proven to fix mental health." Verdict: overstated. The studies report an association with better mood and quality of life, but certainty is low and they don't show cause Baker 2021 [P].
