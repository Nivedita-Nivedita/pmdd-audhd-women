Autism traits, sensory sensitivity & "AuDHD"

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • ADHD and autism often occur together and share genetic roots. If you have one, it's reasonable to ask about the other.

  • Sensory sensitivity, masking and trouble naming feelings are not proof of autism. Sensory sensitivity is common in ADHD too, especially in women, and masking happens across diagnoses.

  • "AuDHD" is a useful community word for having both, but it isn't a separate diagnosis. Online quizzes can't tell you whether you are autistic.

Evidence grade: ESTABLISHED (E) that ADHD and autism often co-occur · SUPPORTED (S) for sensory sensitivity in adult ADHD · PRELIMINARY (P) for the experiences of late-diagnosed women and for camouflaging research · POPULAR BUT UNSUPPORTED (U) for "AuDHD" as a validated construct. Headline grade: PRELIMINARY (P). Talk to a clinician about… having both autism and ADHD considered in one assessment, and how each affects your treatment and support. Not medical advice. 1. How often ADHD and autism occur together, and shared genetics

Autism and ADHD are two separate neurodevelopmental conditions. They overlap more than chance would predict.

  • ADHD in autistic people. A meta-analysis of 96 studies found ADHD in 28% of autistic people, the most common co-occurring diagnosis Lai 2019 [E]. A second meta-analysis of 63 studies estimated 38.5% currently and 40.2% at some point in life Rong 2021 [S]. Estimates vary a lot between studies Lai 2019 [E].
  • Autism in people with ADHD. In 22 publications covering 61,985 children and adolescents with ADHD, 21% met the threshold for autism Hollingdale 2019 [E]. This is child data. There is no matching meta-analysis in adults (see "What we don't know yet").
  • Shared genetics. In 1,899,654 Swedish people, those with autism had much higher odds of ADHD (OR 22.33) Ghirardi 2017 [E]. The link was strongest in identical twins (OR 17.77) and weaker in non-identical twins (4.33) and full siblings (4.59), a pattern consistent with shared genetic liability Ghirardi 2017 [E]. A large genetic study of eight psychiatric conditions, including ADHD and autism, found 109 gene regions linked to at least two conditions Lee 2019 [S].

The overlap in traits may be most visible in the teenage years. In one large set of ratings across ages 0 to 84, combined autism and ADHD traits peaked in adolescence Hartman 2016 [P]. The authors suggest this is when life asks most of planning and social skills, but that is a hypothesis.

2. Sex ratio: about 3 to 1, not 1 to 1

You may have read that autism is equally common in women and men, and that women are simply missed. The evidence supports the "missed" part, but not full equality.

A meta-analysis of 54 studies found a male-to-female ratio of 4.20 overall Loomes 2017 [E]. In studies that actively screened the general population, the ratio was 3.25. In studies that relied on existing diagnoses, it was 4.56 Loomes 2017 [E]. The authors conclude the true ratio is closer to 3 to 1, and that there is a diagnostic bias against girls Loomes 2017 [E].

Many adults, especially women, were missed under older, narrower criteria. A clinical review describes this "lost generation" and notes that camouflaging and co-occurring conditions make adult diagnosis harder Lai 2015 [S]. In one study of 61 autistic adults, women waited longer for referral, were older at diagnosis and were more often misdiagnosed first Gesi 2021 [P].

An earlier ADHD diagnosis can also delay autism recognition. Children first diagnosed with ADHD got their autism diagnosis later: by 1.5 years in boys and 2.6 years in girls in one survey Kentrou 2019 [S], and by about 3 years in a US national survey Miodovnik 2015 [S]. These are child data. In the adult part of the first survey there was no significant delay, which the authors say to read with caution Kentrou 2019 [S].

3. Sensory sensitivity: in ADHD too, and higher in women

"Sensory sensitivity" means reacting more strongly than most people to sound, light, touch, smell or other input. You might also notice the opposite: missing some signals altogether.

Sensory differences are a core part of autism. Across 55 questionnaire studies of 4,606 autistic people, sensory over-responsivity, under-responsivity and seeking were all much higher than in controls Ben-Sasson 2019 [E]. Compared with other clinical groups, only over-responsivity stood out, which suggests it is especially typical of autism Ben-Sasson 2019 [E]. Autistic adults report over-responsivity across sight, hearing, touch, smell, taste and body position Tavassoli 2013 [S].

But sensory sensitivity is not only an autism feature:

  • In 116 adults with ADHD, sensory over- and under-sensitivity were higher than population norms Bijlenga 2017 [S]. They rose with ADHD severity, but were not explained by autistic symptoms Bijlenga 2017 [S].
  • In that study, 43% of women vs 22% of men reported atypical sensitivity Bijlenga 2017 [S].
  • In an online survey of 494 people, 69% of those with ADHD symptoms reported sensitivity to light, compared with 28% of those without Kooij 2014 [P].

Sensory load can also shift across the menstrual cycle. In an online survey, autistic respondents described their sensory and emotional difficulties being amplified around their periods Steward 2018 [P].

What this means for you: sensory sensitivity is real and worth taking seriously. On its own it doesn't tell you whether you are autistic, have ADHD, or both.

4. Camouflaging: not unique to autism or to women

"Camouflaging" or "masking" means hiding or compensating for traits so you fit in socially. Examples include copying other people's expressions, rehearsing conversations, or holding back stimming. The research comes mainly from autism.

  • What it costs. In 92 autistic adults, camouflaging was described as motivated by wanting to fit in and connect, and led to exhaustion and threats to self-perception Hull 2017 [P]. Late-diagnosed women in qualitative studies describe "pretending to be normal" for years Bargiela 2016 [P]; Leedham 2020 [P].
  • Not only autistic people. Adults with ADHD scored higher on camouflaging than comparison adults, but lower than autistic adults van der Putten 2024 [P]. Autistic traits, not ADHD traits, predicted camouflaging, whatever the diagnosis van der Putten 2024 [P]. Undiagnosed adults with social difficulties used strategies similar to those of diagnosed autistic adults Livingston 2019 [P].
  • Not only women. In a meta-analysis, autistic females used more camouflaging strategies than males Cruz 2025 [S]. So it is more common in women, but not exclusive to them. A review concludes the evidence supports a female-typical autism presentation that includes camouflaging, while saying much more research is needed Hull 2020 [P].
  • Mental health. A systematic review of 29 studies found that more camouflaging was linked to worse mental health, and called this conclusion preliminary Cook 2021 [S]. In 113 autistic adults with social anxiety, camouflaging did not predict distress once social anxiety was taken into account Roisenberg 2026 [P].

5. Alexithymia: about half of autistic people, not all

"Alexithymia" means difficulty identifying and describing your own emotions. It is a trait, not a diagnosis.

A meta-analysis of 15 studies found that 49.93% of autistic people met the questionnaire threshold for alexithymia, compared with 4.89% of non-autistic people Kinnaird 2019 [E]. That's common, but it also means about half of autistic people don't have it.

A review argues that the emotional-processing difficulties seen in some autistic people come from co-occurring alexithymia rather than autism itself Bird 2013 [S]. Alexithymia is also raised in eating disorders, anxiety and other conditions Bird 2013 [S]. See guide G25 for how this connects to body signals.

6. Online screeners (AQ, RAADS-R, CAT-Q): what they can't do

Many women first wonder about autism after an online quiz. Here is what we can and can't say from our checked library.

  • What we can say. Diagnosing autism in adults is hard. There may be no one who knew you as a child, and camouflaging can hide traits Lai 2015 [S]. A self-report questionnaire can't take account of those things on its own. For ADHD, an expert consensus stresses using validated screening and diagnostic instruments, not screening alone Kooij 2018 [E].
  • What we can't say yet. Our checked library doesn't yet contain validation studies of the AQ (Autism-Spectrum Quotient), RAADS-R or CAT-Q in adult women. So we can't give you accuracy figures for them here. This part of the guide is partly a "What we don't know yet" section.

Practical tip: treat a high score as a reason to seek an assessment, not as a diagnosis. Treat a low score as not ruling autism out, especially if you camouflage. Bring your answers to the assessment as examples, not as a verdict. Our questionnaires page explains what each tool was built to measure.

7. Getting both considered in an assessment

Autism and ADHD share genetic roots and both affect social and executive skills, but a review concludes they remain distinct enough to need separate diagnoses Antshel 2019 [S]. The same review says each condition should be considered for support and treatment to work well Antshel 2019 [S].

Having both matters for daily life. In 724 autistic adults (58% women), those who rated themselves as having more ADHD symptoms reported less independence in daily living and lower quality of life Yerys 2022 [P].

What about ADHD medication if you are autistic? Evidence is limited:

  • In autistic children (4 small trials, mostly boys), methylphenidate improved hyperactivity, with no evidence of benefit or harm to core autism symptoms. Reduced appetite was much more common. The evidence was low quality Sturman 2017 [P].
  • In 15 autistic adults with ADHD, mostly men, an open-label trial (no placebo group) of extended-release methylphenidate was linked to improved ADHD symptoms Joshi 2020 [P].

Safety. Having both conditions carries real risk, especially for women. In Swedish registers, suicide attempt risk was highest in autistic people without intellectual disability who also had ADHD (OR 7.25), and among women with both, the OR for attempts was 10.27 Hirvikoski 2020 [S]. In Denmark, most of the extra risk in autistic people was seen in those with another psychiatric diagnosis Kõlves 2021 [S]. That is a reason to get co-occurring conditions recognised and treated.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now.

What you can do

  • Ask for both to be considered. Practical tip: when you book an ADHD assessment, say "I'd like autism considered as well, because they often co-occur". Ask whether the service assesses both, or how to get a referral for the other.
  • Collect examples. Practical tip: write down specific situations: sensory overload (what, where, how long it took to recover), social situations you script or rehearse, and how tired you feel afterwards. Specific examples help more than quiz scores.
  • Find someone who knew you young. A parent, sibling or old friend can describe your childhood. Having no one who knew you as a child is one reason adult diagnosis is hard Lai 2015 [S].
  • Track sensory load across your cycle if you menstruate. It may show a pattern worth mentioning.
  • Adjust your environment now. Practical tip: you don't need a diagnosis to use earplugs, sunglasses, softer lighting or quieter workspaces, or to ask for them.
  • Use "AuDHD" if it helps you. It's a community word, not a diagnosis. Expect clinicians to assess each condition separately.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Having both ADHD and autism considered, and who in your area can assess adults for each.
  • Sensory sensitivities that limit work, study or daily life, and what adjustments might help.
  • How co-occurring conditions (anxiety, depression, eating difficulties) fit into the picture.
  • How ADHD medication might suit you if you are, or might be, autistic, given that the evidence in autistic adults is thin.
  • Any thoughts of suicide or self-harm, straight away.

What we don't know yet

  • How common autism is in adults with ADHD. Our library has a meta-analysis in children (21%) but none in adults.
  • Whether "AuDHD" is a distinct profile. There is no validated research definition of a combined autism–ADHD profile.
  • How accurate online screeners are for adult women. Our checked library doesn't yet hold validation data for the AQ, RAADS-R or CAT-Q in this group (see section 6).
  • How to measure masking in ADHD. No validated ADHD-specific masking measure exists.
  • Whether camouflaging causes poor mental health, or the two simply occur together. The studies are cross-sectional.
  • Sensory sensitivity in adult ADHD rests mainly on one study of 116 adults and an online survey. There is no meta-analysis.
  • ADHD medication in autistic women. The trials we have are small and mostly in boys and men.

Popular claims checked

  • autism-and-adhd-often-co-occur: "Lots of people with ADHD are also autistic, and vice versa." Verdict: true. See section 1 Lai 2019 [E]; Hollingdale 2019 [E].
  • audhd-is-its-own-condition: "AuDHD is its own distinct condition." Verdict: unsupported. The two overlap but remain distinct diagnoses Antshel 2019 [S]. Better wording: "AuDHD" is a useful community word for having both, which is common and can delay diagnosis, but it isn't a separate diagnosis.
  • sensory-sensitivity-means-autism: "If you have sensory sensitivities, you must be autistic." Verdict: false. Sensory sensitivity is common in adult ADHD, especially in women Bijlenga 2017 [S].
  • only-autistic-women-mask: "Masking is something only autistic women do." Verdict: false. Adults with ADHD camouflage too van der Putten 2024 [P], and it occurs in men, though less often Cruz 2025 [S].
  • women-mask-their-adhd: "Women with ADHD 'mask', and that's why they're diagnosed late." Verdict: partly true. Camouflaging in ADHD has been measured only with autism tools, and tracked autistic traits rather than ADHD traits van der Putten 2024 [P].
  • autistic-adults-6x-alexithymia: "Autistic adults are 6x more likely to have alexithymia." Verdict: partly true. The direction is right, but the best estimate is 49.93% vs 4.89% Kinnaird 2019 [E], a ratio of about ten.
  • online-quiz-can-diagnose-adhd-or-autism: "An online quiz can tell you whether you have ADHD or autism." Verdict: false. Screeners can suggest an assessment is worth pursuing; they can't diagnose (section 6).
  • masking-causes-burnout-and-suicide: "Masking causes autistic burnout and suicide." Verdict: overstated. Camouflaging is linked to poorer mental health, but the evidence is preliminary and doesn't show cause Cook 2021 [S]. See guide G11.
