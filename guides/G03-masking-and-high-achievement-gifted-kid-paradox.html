Masking & high achievement: the "gifted kid" paradox

Draft. Not yet reviewed by a clinician. Not medical advice.

In short

  • A high IQ doesn't protect you from ADHD. Bright adults with ADHD still struggle more than equally bright adults without it.

  • Test scores can look "normal" in bright adults with ADHD, because tests and everyday life measure different things. Real-world impairment matters more.

  • Coping with ADHD by working harder is common. "Masking" or camouflaging, as researchers measure it, comes from autism research, and we did not find a validated ADHD-specific measure in our search (last updated October 2026).

Evidence grade: PRELIMINARY (P). That ADHD is real and impairing at high IQ is Supported (S). How masking works in ADHD, and how much it delays diagnosis, is Preliminary (P).

Talk to a clinician about… see the section near the end.

Not medical advice.

If you are thinking about suicide or are in crisis, contact your local emergency number or a local crisis line now. Don't wait for it to pass. See crisis support.

Tags: [E] established · [S] supported · [P] preliminary · [U] no direct evidence. Practical tip paragraphs are general guidance and carry no grade.

Some women with ADHD recognise this story: the "gifted kid" whose reports said "bright but…", who got through exams on last-minute adrenaline, and for whom a degree, a job or a PhD now takes everything she has. Many women with ADHD don't fit it, and many bright, stretched people don't have ADHD. The story is not a diagnostic sign. This guide is about why that pattern can hide ADHD when it is present, and what the research can and can't say yet.

1. High IQ doesn't protect against ADHD

ADHD occurs at every level of intelligence. Researchers have tested this by comparing bright people with ADHD against equally bright people without it. Most of these studies come from US samples, several from one research group, and the findings we checked do not report women separately.

  • In children (US family study): among children with an IQ of 120 or more, the 49 with ADHD had more ADHD in their families, more repeated school years, more other mental health conditions and more impairment than 92 without ADHD Antshel 2007 [S]. This pattern matched ADHD in children of average IQ Antshel 2007 [S].
  • In adults (US clinic sample): among adults with an IQ of 120 or more, the 64 with ADHD had lower quality of life and poorer family and work functioning than 53 without ADHD Antshel 2008 [S]. They also had more speeding tickets, accidents and arrests, and more depression, OCD and generalised anxiety Antshel 2008 [S].
  • In a whole population: in a birth cohort of 5,718 children in Rochester, Minnesota, no clear differences were found between the 34 children with ADHD and high IQ and other children with ADHD in age at meeting criteria, other conditions or stimulant treatment, though they read better Katusic 2011 [S].

A systematic review concluded that limited evidence suggests ADHD is a valid diagnosis in highly intelligent people, with typical clinical features, course and treatment response Rommelse 2016 [S]. It also noted that compensatory strategies may increase the risk of a missed diagnosis Rommelse 2016 [S]. A 20-year review of "twice-exceptional" students (gifted and with a disability such as ADHD) concluded they need comprehensive, individualised assessment, and that little is known about which interventions work Foley Nicpon 2010 [P].

Good grades don't rule ADHD out either. A review of 41 articles on women and girls concluded that satisfactory academic achievement should not rule out ADHD Quinn 2014 [P].

2. Why tests can look "normal"

Assessments sometimes include tests of executive function (EF), the brain's management system: planning, holding things in mind, switching tasks and stopping yourself. In bright adults, these test scores can look fine even when daily life is not.

  • In a small study, 51 adults with ADHD who had not yet received treatment were split by IQ (110 or above, versus below 110) and compared with 33 healthy adults Milioni 2016 [P]. The standard-IQ group was impaired on many EF tests. The higher-IQ group differed from controls on only one measure Milioni 2016 [P]. The authors suggest a higher IQ may compensate and make diagnosis harder Milioni 2016 [P]. Note that 110 is above average, not a "gifted" cut-off.
  • High-IQ adults with ADHD scored worse than high-IQ adults without ADHD on several EF tests, but their scores were still in the average range Antshel 2010 [S]. Compared with everyone else they looked "normal". Compared with people like them, they were behind.
  • In 157 adults with ADHD and an IQ of 120 or more, 73% were significantly impaired on at least 5 of 8 EF measures Brown 2009 [S]. Those measures combined test scores with self-reports of daily life. There was no comparison group.

Part of the explanation is that tests and daily life measure different things. Across 20 studies, EF tests and EF rating scales of everyday behaviour agreed poorly: the median correlation was r = .19 Toplak 2012 [E]. The authors concluded that tests capture cognitive efficiency, while ratings capture success at pursuing goals in real life Toplak 2012 [E]. In a study of work, problems in adults with ADHD mostly took the form of not meeting their own standards and potential, and less often job loss Fuermaier 2021 [S]. Test scores did not predict how well people functioned at work Fuermaier 2021 [S].

That last point matters if you are a high achiever. "Not meeting your own potential" doesn't show up as failure on paper.

3. Compensation and its cost

"Compensation" means getting to a normal-looking result by a different, often more effortful, route.

What research shows. In interviews, 32 adult outpatients with ADHD described many strategies they had used before diagnosis Canela 2017 [P]. These were organisational, motor, attentional, social and psychopharmacological Canela 2017 [P]. Several were seen as helpful, and the authors suggest this coping may help explain late diagnosis Canela 2017 [P]. A UK expert consensus lists compensatory strategies that mask symptoms as one barrier to recognising ADHD in women Young 2020 [E].

There are early hints about cost. In 19 young adults with ADHD, listening took more mental effort than in 20 controls, although no clear difference was found in speech perception itself Taitelbaum-Swead 2019 [P]. The authors suggest this may make people with ADHD more prone to fatigue Taitelbaum-Swead 2019 [P]. But a small study of 31 adolescent boys found no clear sign of mental-effort problems in ADHD Mies 2019 [P], so this is far from settled. In another study, 62% of adults with ADHD met the threshold for significant fatigue Rogers 2016 [P].

What many women describe (lived experience, not research findings). Over-preparing: rereading an email ten times, or starting the essay three weeks early because you know what happens otherwise. Avoiding situations where the cracks would show. Staying late to catch up. Collapsing at weekends. We did not find a study in our search that measured these costs in hours or energy in women with ADHD, so we keep this separate from the evidence above.

4. Camouflaging: borrowed from autism research

Coping (section 3) and camouflaging are not the same thing. Coping strategies are ways of getting tasks done; camouflaging, as researchers measure it, is mainly about hiding or compensating for social differences. Feeling that you "mask" is real, but it is not the same as a measured camouflaging score.

"Masking" or "camouflaging" was first studied in autism. Researchers there define compensation as a mismatch between behaviour that looks fine on the surface and underlying differences that persist Livingston 2017 [P]. In 92 autistic adults, camouflaging had three parts: motivations (fitting in), techniques (masking and compensating), and consequences, including exhaustion Hull 2017 [P]. In 136 adults, people without a diagnosis but with social difficulties used similar strategies to diagnosed autistic adults Livingston 2019 [P]. The authors suggest this may keep some people below the diagnostic threshold Livingston 2019 [P].

In autism research, females used more camouflaging strategies than males Cruz 2025 [S]. A systematic review of 29 studies reached the preliminary conclusion that more camouflaging goes with worse mental health Cook 2021 [P].

And in ADHD? One study compared camouflaging in 105 adults with ADHD, 105 autistic adults and 105 comparison adults van der Putten 2024 [P]. The ADHD group camouflaged more than the comparison group, but less than the autistic group van der Putten 2024 [P]. Autism traits predicted camouflaging, but ADHD traits did not van der Putten 2024 [P]. That could mean the autism questionnaire misses ADHD-style masking. It could also mean some of what feels like masking reflects autistic traits, which often co-occur with ADHD. We don't know which. Even in autism, measurement is shaky. In 113 autistic adults with social anxiety, camouflaging scores did not predict depression, distress or disability once social anxiety and social responsiveness were accounted for; the authors suggest current measures may capture overlapping constructs Roisenberg 2026 [P].

The bottom line. We did not find a validated questionnaire for measuring masking in ADHD in our search. The main camouflaging questionnaire was built and validated for autistic traits.

Practical tip. Be wary of commercial "ADHD masking" questionnaires that have no peer-reviewed validation. Treat any score as a description of your experience, not a measurement.

5. Claim check: "High IQ masks ADHD"

What the research supports: in small studies, high-IQ adults with ADHD often scored in the average range on thinking tests despite having ADHD. Evidence: preliminary.

  • The direction is plausible. Bright adults with ADHD can score in the normal range on tests Antshel 2010 [S]. In one small study, higher-IQ adults with ADHD differed from controls on only one test measure Milioni 2016 [P].
  • The evidence is thin. These studies are small and cross-sectional (a single snapshot), so they can't show that IQ causes later diagnosis. We did not find a study that followed bright girls over time to test this.
  • "Masks" can mislead. High IQ doesn't hide ADHD from your life. It may hide it from tests, and perhaps from teachers while school is easy. Brown's group still found impairment in most high-IQ adults with ADHD once daily-life measures were included Brown 2009 [S].

Better wording: "In bright adults, test scores can look normal even when ADHD is present, so real-life impairment matters more than test results."

6. What this means for assessment

The practical message is to weight real-world impairment over test scores. Everyday EF problems predicted impairment in daily life and work better than EF tests did, in people followed from childhood hyperactivity to a mean age of 27 Barkley 2011 [S].

Practical tip. Things that help an assessor see past high performance: - examples of the cost of your results, such as all-nighters, missed meals and weekends lost to recovery; - a rating of everyday executive problems, filled in by you and by someone who knows you well; - a childhood informant, or school reports that say "capable but…", if you have them (not having them doesn't rule out an assessment); - asking how your results compare with people of similar ability, not just the population average. This is our suggestion, based on the high-IQ comparison studies above.

What you can do

Practical tips 1. Keep a two-week "cost log". Write down what a normal week takes: the hours, the workarounds, the recovery time. 2. Collect "bright but…" evidence from school reports, appraisals or supervisor feedback. 3. Bring this guide's key point to your assessor: normal test scores don't rule ADHD out in bright adults. 4. If social masking feels central to your experience, ask about autism too. The two often co-occur. 5. Notice your own success stories. Coping strategies that work are worth keeping; the aim is to make them cost less.

Talk to a clinician about…

  • Whether your assessment will weigh everyday functioning and the cost of compensating, not just test scores.
  • How your test results compare with people of similar ability.
  • Whether autism should be considered alongside ADHD.
  • Exhaustion, low mood or anxiety that come with keeping up appearances. These deserve care in their own right.
  • Any thoughts of self-harm or suicide.

What we don't know yet

  • How to measure masking in ADHD. We did not find a validated ADHD-specific measure. The main camouflaging questionnaire was validated for autistic traits only.
  • Whether high IQ causes later diagnosis. The studies are small and cross-sectional, with no long-term follow-up of bright girls.
  • The real cost of compensating. We did not find a study that measured the hours, energy or health cost of compensation in women with ADHD. The effort studies are small and conflicting Taitelbaum-Swead 2019 [P] Mies 2019 [P].
  • Screener cut-offs for gifted adults. The main screeners were validated in general and clinic samples, and we did not find a study validating their cut-offs in gifted adults.
  • Whether camouflaging harms mental health in ADHD, as it appears to in autism. In an online survey of 329 Polish women with a self-reported ADHD diagnosis, camouflaging (measured with new items adapted from an autism questionnaire) went with lower life satisfaction and more depressive symptoms Wicherkiewicz 2024 [P]. It was a single cross-sectional, self-report study, so it can't show cause.
  • Which supports help twice-exceptional adults. Little is known even for students Foley Nicpon 2010 [P].

Popular claims checked

  • You may have heard: "A high IQ protects you from ADHD." What the research supports: ADHD occurs at every IQ level, and high-IQ people with ADHD have more difficulties than equally bright people without it. Evidence: supported. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "A high IQ masks ADHD, so it doesn't show up on testing." What the research supports: in small studies, high-IQ adults with ADHD often scored in the average range on thinking tests despite having ADHD. Evidence: preliminary. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "If you did well at school, you can't have ADHD." What the research supports: doing well at school does not rule out ADHD; it occurs in high-IQ people, whose coping can delay diagnosis. Evidence: supported. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "Women with ADHD mask their symptoms, and that's why they're diagnosed so late." What the research supports: coping strategies that hide symptoms are a recognised barrier to diagnosing ADHD in women, according to expert consensus and interview studies. Evidence: preliminary. Masking in ADHD hasn't been properly measured. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "Masking is something only autistic women do." What the research supports: camouflaging is reported across genders and in ADHD as well as autism, though autistic women report it more than autistic men. Evidence: supported. More in Claim check
  • You may have heard: "Masking causes autistic burnout and suicide." What the research supports: masking is linked to poorer mental health in autistic people, but it hasn't been shown to cause burnout or suicide. Evidence: preliminary. More in Claim check
