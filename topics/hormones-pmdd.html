Topics / Hormones & PMDD

Hormones & PMDD

PMDD is a different brain response to normal hormone changes. Women with ADHD screen positive for it more often.

What is solid

  • Hormone levels are normal in PMDD. The brain responds differently to normal changes (Schmidt 1998).
  • Two cycles of daily ratings are the standard way to confirm the pattern; C-PASS is one standardised scoring method (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). A clinician can assess and offer care while you track.
  • Oral contraceptives carried a higher depression risk in young women with ADHD (Lundin 2023).
  • Premenstrual disorders are linked to higher suicide mortality (Opatowski 2024).

What is thin or missing

  • Women with ADHD screen positive for PMDD more often in several studies (reported as odds ratios or prevalence ratios that differ by study), but we did not find a study confirming this with daily ratings (search last updated October 2026).
  • Adjusting stimulant dose across the cycle rests on a 9-woman case series.
  • The estrogen–dopamine explanation has not been tested in ADHD.

How it connects

  • ADHD in women: Women with ADHD screen positive for PMDD more often than women without ADHD (not yet confirmed with daily ratings) Supported
  • Emotion & rejection sensitivity: Mood can worsen before periods: PMDD, or premenstrual exacerbation of another condition Supported
  • Body & daily function: POTS symptoms may vary across the menstrual cycle Preliminary
  • Life stages & populations: A history of premenstrual disorders is linked with perinatal depression Supported
  • Immune system & allergy: Asthma and eczema often worsen before periods; inflammation markers linked with PMS in small studies Preliminary

  • PMDD treatments: what the evidence supports

    Treatments grouped into evidence tiers, anchored to the ACOG 2023 guideline and the 2024 Cochrane review. Strongest: SSRIs, with both continuous and luteal-phase dosing helping and continuous dosing favoured on average. Moderate: drospirenone pills and short-term GnRH agonists, with more trade-offs; a small 2026 network meta-analysis found SSRIs and pills both effective but cannot rank them. Limited: CBT, surgery, and calcium, B6 and vitex, which have some PMS evidence but insufficient evidence in confirmed PMDD. No eligible trial was found in women with ADHD or autism.

  • Starting and adjusting ADHD medication as a woman

    Stimulants and atomoxetine are the medicines with consistent adult evidence, but Cochrane rates much of it low certainty and the trials skew male. Appetite, sleep and heart-rate effects are common and need monitoring. About half of adults stop within a year, and women are no more likely to stop. Evidence on sex differences and on premenstrual dosing is thin.

  • Living with PMDD: relationships and getting taken seriously

    Self-selected surveys and interviews consistently describe years of dismissal and misdiagnosis, a 'dual identity' and strain on relationships. Daily ratings, the right clinician and partner-inclusive CBT are the practical levers. The evidence is consistent but mostly qualitative.

What the research says about common claims

PMDD is confirmed by daily symptom ratings over at least two cycles, not by a blood test or a one-off questionnaire.

Established evidence Several good studies or reviews agree.

Who was studied: Validation of daily-ratings scoring against expert diagnosis in 200 women; prevalence meta-analysis; DSM-5 workgroup review; one PMS hormone study

Blood tests and one-off questionnaires can't confirm PMDD; prospective daily tracking over at least two cycles confirms the pattern, and a clinician can assess you and offer care while you track.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“A hormone blood test (or a one-off quiz) can tell you if you have PMDD.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs the other way: one-off reports overestimate PMDD and hormone levels are usually normal.

What the studies found

PMDD is confirmed with prospective daily symptom ratings over at least two cycles; the C-PASS, one standard scoring system, matched expert diagnosis with an estimated 98% correct classification in 200 women, and retrospective reports of premenstrual worsening poorly predicted the prospective diagnosis (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). Provisional (one-off) diagnosis roughly doubles prevalence (7.7% vs 3.2% confirmed; Reilly 2024). Hormone levels are typically normal in PMDD; symptoms reflect an abnormal response to normal hormone change (Schmidt 1998), so blood tests cannot diagnose it. A clinician can still assess someone provisionally and discuss care while they track, and urgent help never waits for a chart.

Link to this claim

Combined pills containing drospirenone may modestly reduce PMDD symptoms, more for overall than mood symptoms.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Cochrane review of 5 RCTs (most participants with PMDD) and RCTs of drospirenone pills; network meta-analysis in PMS or PMDD

Some combined pills, especially those containing drospirenone, may reduce PMDD symptoms modestly, more for overall than mood symptoms; they aren't safe for everyone (clot risk, migraine with aura, smoking at 35+), and evidence is of low quality.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“The pill treats PMDD.”

The popular version goes further: only some pills have evidence, the effect is modest, and they are usually second line after SSRIs.

What the studies found

Drospirenone-containing combined pills have the best evidence: the 2023 Cochrane review (5 RCTs, 858 women, most with PMDD) found they may improve overall premenstrual symptoms (SMD -0.41; low-quality evidence) and functional impairment, with effects that may be small to moderate; placebo also had a significant effect, and they may increase adverse effects (OR 2.31) and withdrawal for adverse effects (OR 3.41). No included trial reported rare serious harms such as venous thromboembolism, and it is unknown whether the pill works after three cycles or beats other pills (Ma 2023). In the larger trial the number needed to treat was 8 (Yonkers 2005; also Pearlstein 2005). Across 9 trials, combined pills improved overall premenstrual symptoms (SMD 0.41) but not depressive symptoms specifically (de Wit 2021). A continuous levonorgestrel pill gave mixed results (Halbreich 2012; Freeman 2012). Clinical guidance places the pill second line after SSRIs (Wagner-Schuman 2023). Combined pills carry a small blood-clot risk and are not suitable for everyone (for example migraine with aura, or age 35 or over and smoking), per NHS information.

Link to this claim

In large Danish and Swedish registry studies, hormonal contraception, especially in teenagers, was linked with more later antidepressant use and depression diagnoses.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Danish and Swedish national registries of women and girls aged 15-34; observational, not trials

Large registry studies link hormonal contraception, especially in teens, with a higher chance of later depression; young women with ADHD have a higher depression risk with or without the pill. These are associations, not proof the pill causes depression.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“The pill causes depression, especially if you have ADHD.”

The popular version states cause; the studies show associations, and much of the higher risk with ADHD is present whether or not the pill is used.

What the studies found

In over 1 million Danish women, hormonal contraception users had higher rates of first antidepressant use than non-users (combined pill adjusted incidence rate ratio 1.23; teenagers 1.8) (Skovlund 2016), and current or recent use was linked to first suicide attempt compared with never-use (hazard ratio 1.97) (Skovlund 2018). In Swedish women aged 15-24, women with ADHD had a higher depression risk than women without ADHD whatever their contraception (adjusted hazard ratio 3.69); combined-pill users with ADHD had aHR 5.19 compared with non-users without ADHD and 6.10 compared with pill users without ADHD (Lundin 2023). Because every comparison uses women without ADHD as the reference, much of the excess reflects ADHD itself rather than the pill. These are observational associations, not proof the pill causes depression.

Link to this claim

In self-selected surveys and interviews, many people who report PMDD describe being dismissed or misdiagnosed by clinicians, often over years.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Self-selected surveys and qualitative interviews of people reporting PMDD; few had diagnoses made with daily ratings

Many people with PMDD report being dismissed or misdiagnosed, often for years; these surveys are self-selected, so they show the problem is common, not how often it happens overall.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Doctors dismiss PMDD.”

The popular version goes further: it treats a commonly reported experience as true of all doctors, and not every clinical disagreement is dismissal.

What the studies found

In an Australian survey of 267 people with PMDD, participants had seen an average of 5.1 providers seeking a diagnosis; 54% reported medical gaslighting, 56% misdiagnosis, and only 19% were diagnosed in line with DSM recommendations (Border 2026). Interviews with 27 women in nine countries described medical gas-lighting and diagnostic odysseys (Mosalisa 2026), and UK women described 'lost decades' of misdiagnosis (Osborn 2020). In 2,512 survey respondents, gynaecologists and psychiatrists were rated highest on PMDD knowledge (Hantsoo 2022). A scoping review lists limited provider knowledge and guideline non-adherence among barriers (Nayak 2025). All samples are self-selected and may over-represent people with poor experiences, so they show the problem is common, not how often it happens overall. Not every clinical disagreement is dismissal: a clinician may reasonably ask for prospective ratings, or consider PME or another diagnosis.

Link to this claim

In small hormone experiments, normal hormone changes brought back symptoms in women with PMS or PMDD but not in other women, pointing to brain sensitivity rather than abnormal hormone levels.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Small hormone-suppression and add-back experiments in 20 women with PMS and 12 with PMDD, plus a review

In experiments, the same normal hormone levels brought back symptoms in women with PMS or PMDD but not in other women, and the change in levels seemed to matter more than the level itself.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“PMDD is caused by low progesterone or a hormone imbalance.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs the other way: the problem appears to be sensitivity to normal hormone shifts, not low levels.

What the studies found

When ovarian hormones were switched off and then added back at normal levels, symptoms returned in women with PMS but not in women without it (Schmidt 1998). In 12 women with PMDD, symptoms came back when hormone levels rose but not once levels were steady, pointing to the change rather than the level (Schmidt 2017). Reviews describe PMDD as an abnormal brain response to normal hormone fluctuations (Hantsoo 2020).

Link to this claim

In women with mood disorders, premenstrual worsening is common and is often premenstrual exacerbation of the existing condition, which differs from PMDD.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Review of mostly small studies of women with mood disorders; daily-ratings validation study; one small RCT in suicidal outpatients

Premenstrual worsening of an existing condition is often premenstrual exacerbation (PME), which differs from PMDD. Most research is in mood disorders rather than ADHD; daily tracking plus a clinician's judgement helps tell them apart, and care or urgent help doesn't need to wait.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“If your ADHD, anxiety or depression gets worse before your period, that's PMDD.”

The popular version treats all premenstrual worsening as PMDD, when it is often a separate pattern called premenstrual exacerbation.

What the studies found

Worsening of an existing condition before menstruation is called premenstrual exacerbation (PME); around 60% of women with mood disorders report it, and treatments that help PMDD show less or no benefit for it, from sparse and mostly small studies (Kuehner 2021). Prospective daily ratings, for example DRSP ratings scored with the C-PASS, establish whether the PMDD pattern is met; telling PMDD from PME, or recognising both, still needs a clinician's judgement (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). In 28 outpatients with suicidal thoughts, 93% had their peak around menstruation, yet none met full PMDD criteria (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022).

Link to this claim

On average, premenstrual symptoms did not worsen across the menopause transition, but women with PMS were more likely to report low mood and poor sleep.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Midlife women with self-reported PMS in US population cohorts and daily diaries; no studies of confirmed PMDD

Women with a history of premenstrual symptoms appear more vulnerable to low mood and other symptoms in perimenopause, and irregular cycles can make patterns hard to see. We did not find a study following confirmed PMDD through the late transition (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“PMDD gets worse in perimenopause.”

This has been tested for premenstrual symptoms in general and the evidence mostly runs the other way; PMDD itself has not been followed through the transition.

What the studies found

In a population cohort of 436 women followed for 5 years, premenstrual syndrome decreased across the menopause transition, but women with PMS were more likely to report hot flushes (odds ratio 2.09), depressed mood (odds ratio 2.34) and poor sleep (odds ratio 1.72) (Freeman 2004, Obstet Gynecol). In daily diaries from 290 women, no clear effect of late reproductive vs early menopausal-transition stage on premenstrual symptom severity was found (Mitchell 2022). Among depressed perimenopausal women, 21% met criteria for premenstrual dysphoria vs 3% of non-depressed women (Richards 2006), and depressive symptoms are more likely during the transition (Freeman 2004, Arch Gen Psychiatry). We did not find a study following prospectively confirmed PMDD through the late transition (search last updated October 2026).

Link to this claim

SSRIs help PMDD whether taken daily or only in the luteal phase, often within the first cycle, though daily dosing was probably more effective on average.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: RCTs in women with PMS or PMDD pooled by Cochrane 2024, and large RCTs in women with DSM-defined PMDD

Benefit often appears in the first treatment cycle, which is why luteal-only dosing can work. The 2024 Cochrane review found daily dosing probably works better on average, and starting only when symptoms begin has weaker evidence. Which schedule suits someone is decided with their prescriber.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Taking an SSRI only in the two weeks before your period (or only on symptom days) works as well as taking it every day.”

Both schedules help, but pooled trials suggest daily dosing works better on average.

“SSRIs work for PMDD within days, unlike for depression.”

Benefit appears within the first cycle, but we did not find a trial reporting onset day by day (search last updated October 2026).

What the studies found

Both schedules have evidence. In the 2024 Cochrane review (34 RCTs), SSRIs beat placebo when taken continuously (SMD -0.69; 7 studies, 1055 participants) and in the luteal phase only (SMD -0.39; 6 studies, 687 participants), both moderate-certainty evidence; the review concluded SSRIs are probably more effective when taken continuously (P = 0.03 for subgroup difference) (Jespersen 2024). That is a between-subgroup comparison of placebo-controlled trials, so it describes an average, not what suits each person. In a three-arm RCT that compared schedules directly, luteal-phase sertraline did not differ from continuous dosing, both beat placebo with improvement appearing swiftly in the first month, placebo had caught up by the third month, and women with symptoms after their period ended did worse on either schedule (Freeman 2004). Luteal sertraline beat placebo in 281 women with PMDD, already by the first cycle (responders 50% vs 26%) (Halbreich 2002). Symptom-onset dosing missed its primary outcome (P = .06), though it helped on depression and irritability scales (Yonkers 2015). The 2013 version of the Cochrane review had found no clear difference between schedules (Marjoribanks 2013); it is superseded by the 2024 update. We did not find a trial reporting a day-by-day onset (search last updated October 2026).

Link to this claim

Studies of PMDD and other premenstrual disorders report more suicidal thoughts and attempts than comparison groups, and a Swedish registry found more deaths by suicide in women with diagnosed premenstrual disorders.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Women with PMDD or broader premenstrual disorders (some identified by questionnaire) in observational studies, two systematic reviews and a Swedish national registry

These are group-level associations from observational studies of uneven quality, so they cannot predict any one person's risk. They are a reason to ask about suicidal thoughts directly and to get help early. If you are having suicidal thoughts, see Crisis support now.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“PMDD seriously raises suicide risk.”

The popular version matches the evidence well, though the figures describe groups and cannot predict an individual's risk.

What the studies found

A 2026 systematic review of 18 studies (about 2.6 million people) found suicide-related outcomes associated with "PMDD and/or premenstrual disorders" in eight ways, with very wide prevalence ranges between studies (for example 26% to 86% for suicidal ideation), quality concerns in 70% of studies, no pooled estimate, and no studies testing treatments for suicidality (Zhitnik 2026). An earlier systematic review (13 studies; 10 meta-analysed) found that, compared with women without premenstrual disturbances, women with PMDD had higher odds of suicide attempt (odds ratio 6.97, 95% CI 2.98-16.29) and suicidal ideation (odds ratio 3.95, 2.97-5.24); the authors support routine suicide-risk assessment in moderate-to-severe premenstrual disturbance (Prasad 2021). In a Swedish nationwide matched cohort, 67,748 women with clinically diagnosed premenstrual disorders (a broader group than PMDD alone) (identified from inpatient/outpatient diagnoses and drug dispensing) were compared with 338,740 matched women over a mean 6.2 years: all-cause mortality was not raised (adjusted HR 0.91, 0.82-1.02) but death by suicide was (HR 1.92, 1.43-2.60), and higher in those diagnosed before age 25 (HR 3.84, 1.18-12.45, a wide interval) (Opatowski 2024). These are group-level associations from observational data and cannot predict an individual's risk.

Link to this claim

Women with ADHD or high ADHD traits screen positive for PMDD more often than other women in several surveys.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Cross-sectional screening surveys of women with ADHD or high ADHD traits (online, Japanese and clinic-recruited samples); none confirmed PMDD with daily ratings

Several studies find women with ADHD are considerably more likely to screen positive for PMDD, with estimates that vary by measure and sample; none has yet confirmed this with prospective daily ratings.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Women with ADHD are 3x more likely to have PMDD.”

The popular version gives one fixed figure, but estimates vary by measure and sample and rest on screening rather than confirmed diagnosis.

What the studies found

Every study we found (search last updated October 2026) finds women with ADHD more likely to screen positive for (provisional) PMDD, but the figures use different measures and populations. In 715 young women recruited online, provisional PMDD was 31.4% with self-reported clinical ADHD and 41.1% with questionnaire-defined ADHD vs 9.8% without (the '3x' is a crude ratio of these percentages); the highest risk was in questionnaire-defined ADHD plus depression/anxiety (risk ratio 4.53, 95% CI 3.10-6.61) (Broughton 2025). In 2,000 employed Japanese women, ADHD traits had an adjusted odds ratio of 6.49 (4.10-10.2) for PMDD on a DSM-IV-TR-based scale (Tsuji 2026). Odds ratios and risk ratios are not interchangeable and should not be merged into one range. None used prospective daily ratings; provisional diagnosis roughly doubles PMDD prevalence (7.7% vs 3.2% confirmed; Reilly 2024). An often-quoted earlier figure (Dorani 2021) came from an ADHD clinic sample with no control group.

Link to this claim

ADHD symptoms may shift across the menstrual cycle with hormone changes, according to small early studies.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Who was studied: 32 young women not selected for ADHD tracked daily for 35 days; healthy women; systematic review of 11 small studies

Some women notice ADHD symptoms shift across the cycle, and early research links this to lower oestrogen alongside other hormone changes, but we did not find a study testing the oestrogen-dopamine mechanism in ADHD (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Estrogen boosts dopamine, so ADHD gets worse whenever estrogen drops in your cycle.”

The popular version goes further: it presents an untested mechanism as fact and says symptoms worsen whenever oestrogen drops.

What the studies found

In 32 young women tracked daily for 35 days, lower estradiol together with higher progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the next day, with rises in the early follicular and early luteal phases (Roberts 2018). In healthy women, estradiol's effect on working memory depended on baseline dopamine (Jacobs 2011), but we did not find a study testing the estrogen-dopamine mechanism directly in ADHD (search last updated October 2026). A review of 11 studies found only suggestive, heterogeneous evidence (Osianlis 2025).

Link to this claim

Blocking allopregnanolone eased PMDD in a small trial, but a larger trial of a drug countering it missed its main outcome.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Who was studied: Two RCTs in women with PMDD (one of 16 women, one of 206 prospectively confirmed), plus theoretical reviews

Allopregnanolone is a leading candidate for why the brain reacts unusually to normal hormone changes in PMDD, but treatments built on this theory are still experimental.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“PMDD is caused by allopregnanolone, the brain-active breakdown product of progesterone.”

The popular version goes further: it treats a leading theory, in which allopregnanolone levels are normal, as a settled cause.

What the studies found

A leading theory is that in PMDD the brain's GABA-A receptors react paradoxically to normal luteal-phase allopregnanolone levels (Backstrom 2014; Hantsoo 2020). In a small trial (16 women with PMDD), blocking allopregnanolone production with high-dose dutasteride reduced irritability, sadness and anxiety, while a low dose did not (Martinez 2015). A drug designed to counter allopregnanolone (sepranolone, 206 women) did not beat placebo on its main outcome (Backstrom 2021).

Link to this claim

Calcium and vitamin B6 eased premenstrual symptoms in trials of women with PMS, though most trials were low quality and none we found was in diagnosed PMDD.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Who was studied: Women with PMS (not diagnosed PMDD) in one large calcium RCT and reviews of mostly low-quality B6 and nutrition trials

Calcium and vitamin B6 have some of the better supplement evidence for PMS, but they haven't been tested in diagnosed PMDD and are not substitutes for proven treatments; high-dose B6 can damage nerves.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Calcium and vitamin B6 help PMDD.”

The popular version goes further: the evidence is for PMS, not PMDD.

What the studies found

In a multicentre RCT of 466 analysed women with moderate-to-severe PMS, calcium reduced luteal symptoms by 48% vs 30% with placebo by the third cycle (Thys-Jacobs 1998). A systematic review of 9 B6 trials (940 patients) found benefit (OR 2.32) but low trial quality (Wyatt 1999). A review of 31 nutrition trials found consistent benefits for B6, calcium and zinc on psychological PMS symptoms, though only 1 trial had low risk of bias (Robinson 2024). All of this is PMS, not confirmed PMDD; experts list calcium as warranting further exploration (Nevatte 2013). Safety: NHS advice says large amounts of supplemental vitamin B6 can cause peripheral neuropathy (loss of feeling in arms and legs), usually reversible on stopping but occasionally permanent.

Who was studied: Mostly low-quality RCTs in women with PMS (not confirmed PMDD), pooled in meta-analyses and reviews

Chasteberry may ease PMS symptoms, but the trials are mostly poor quality and in PMS rather than PMDD; it is not a proven PMDD treatment.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Vitex (chasteberry) helps PMDD.”

The popular version goes further: the evidence is mainly in PMS, not PMDD, and the pooled effect is likely overestimated.

What the studies found

Trials are almost all in PMS, not confirmed PMDD. Pooled placebo-controlled trials showed a large effect (Hedges g -1.21), but with high risk of bias, extreme heterogeneity (I² 91%) and likely publication bias, so the authors say the effect is at best an overestimate (Verkaik 2017). In the 3 best-reported trials (520 women), vitex users were 2.57 times more likely to have PMS remission (Csupor 2019). A review of 8 RCTs was positive but noted varying diagnostic criteria, preparations and outcomes (Cerqueira 2017). Experts say vitex warrants further exploration (Nevatte 2013).

Evidence on whether women with ADHD have a harder perimenopause is mixed: one large cohort found more severe symptoms, while another survey found no clear difference.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Who was studied: Women with self-reported ADHD in a population cohort and an online survey of women aged 45-60; cross-sectional comparisons

A harder perimenopause is possible for some women with ADHD but is not a consistent finding. No study has followed ADHD symptoms in the same women through the transition, and treatment at this stage has barely been studied.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Perimenopause makes ADHD much worse.”

The popular version states as settled something that has barely been studied and where findings disagree.

“Menopause is always worse for women with ADHD.”

The popular version treats a pattern seen in one cohort, and not in another survey, as universal.

What the studies found

The evidence is mixed. In a population-based cohort, women with self-reported ADHD more often had severe perimenopausal symptoms than women without (535 vs 4,857 women; 54.2% vs 30.1%; prevalence ratio 1.80), across psychological, somatic and urogenital symptoms, with the gap largest at ages 35-39, which the authors read as an earlier onset of perimenopause (Jakobsdottir Smari 2025). In a survey of 656 women aged 45 to 60 (245 with an ADHD diagnosis), women with ADHD did not report more menopausal complaints at any stage after correction for multiple testing (Chapman 2025). A systematic review of 11 studies found only suggestive evidence linking ADHD symptoms to sex hormones, mainly in puberty and across the menstrual cycle, and called for menopause research (Osianlis 2025). An expert review concludes that hormonal transitions worsen ADHD and mood but that treatment research is lacking (Kooij 2025). Neither cohort followed ADHD symptoms within the same women across the transition.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Who was studied: Questionnaire study of 65 women with POTS and two lab studies of 10 women each

Small studies suggest symptoms and standing tolerance are worst during or just after the start of the period, not the days before, though one study found no phase difference. We did not find a study of the premenstrual days specifically (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“POTS gets worse before your period.”

The popular version points to the wrong phase: the small studies so far point to the period itself rather than the days before.

What the studies found

The menstrual cycle does seem to affect POTS symptoms, but the small studies point to the period itself, not the days before. Women with POTS (65) reported more lightheadedness than controls in every phase, worst during menses (Peggs 2012). In 10 women with POTS, standing tolerance was poorer and presyncope more common in the early follicular phase than the mid-luteal phase (Fu 2010). Another study of 10 women found no phase difference in heart rate, sympathetic nerve activity or presyncope (Stickford 2015). We did not find a study of the premenstrual (late-luteal) days specifically (search last updated October 2026). These studies describe association, not cause.

Magnesium alone did not ease premenstrual symptoms more than placebo in a small trial, and reviews find insufficient evidence for it.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Who was studied: One small crossover trial in women with mild premenstrual symptoms; reviews of PMS trials and observational studies; no PMDD trials

We did not find good evidence that magnesium treats PMDD: small PMS studies are inconclusive, women with PMS don't reliably have low magnesium, and we did not find a trial in confirmed PMDD (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Magnesium helps (or fixes) PMDD.”

This has been tested in PMS and the evidence does not support it; it has not been tested in PMDD.

What the studies found

In the most-cited trial, 44 women with mild premenstrual symptoms took magnesium, B6, both or placebo for one cycle each: magnesium alone did not differ from placebo, and only the combination slightly reduced anxiety-type symptoms (De Souza 2000). A review of 31 nutrition RCTs found insufficient evidence for magnesium (Robinson 2024), and a meta-analysis of 13 observational studies found no overall difference in serum magnesium in PMS (Moslehi 2019). We did not find a trial testing magnesium in confirmed PMDD (search last updated October 2026).

The popular version generalises from one small, specific study; larger studies give mixed results.

What the studies found

The 92% figure comes from 26 women with autism and learning disability rated by observers, using a loose threshold (Obaydi 2008). In a community study, PMDD was not significantly more common in autistic women (14.3% vs 9.5%; Groenman 2022), while another found raised provisional PMDD rates in both ADHD and autistic women (Groenman 2026) and a third found no link with autistic traits (Tsuji 2026). Removing the ovaries ended cyclical symptoms in small, uncontrolled studies of women with severe PMS whom other treatments had failed.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Who was studied: Small uncontrolled series of women with severe, treatment-resistant PMS; an RCT in women with heavy periods; expert consensus

Removing the ovaries (usually with the womb, followed by HRT) ended cyclical symptoms in small series without control groups; a hysterectomy that keeps the ovaries has not been shown to treat PMDD, and surgery is a last resort.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“A hysterectomy (or having your ovaries removed) cures PMDD.”

The popular version goes further: it applies to removing the ovaries, not the womb alone, and rests on small uncontrolled series.

What the studies found

Only removing the ovaries, not the womb alone, stops the hormonal cycle. In 14 women with severe, treatment-resistant PMS, hysterectomy with oophorectomy plus oestrogen ended the cyclic pattern (Casper 1990); in a retrospective series of 47 women, 93.6% reported complete resolution, all on HRT afterwards (Cronje 2004). Neither had a control group. In women treated for heavy periods, hysterectomy with the ovaries kept reduced premenstrual symptoms no more than a hormonal IUS (Leminen 2012). Experts consider oophorectomy as one of the ovulation-suppressing options (Nevatte 2013); clinical guidance puts SSRIs and the pill first and GnRH analogues with add-back next (Wagner-Schuman 2023), and GnRH agonists work but long-term use is limited by menopausal side effects (Naheed 2025).

Key research

118 papers. A selection, listed by study design (reviews and trials first), then newest first. The label is the kind of study, not a quality score. See all in the library.

  • Fourth consensus of the International Society for Premenstrual Disorders (ISPMD): auditable standards for diagnosis and management of premenstrual disorder

    Experts set standards for good PMDD care: two months of symptom diaries before diagnosis, SSRIs as the first treatment, and clear explanation of side effects.

    Ismaili 2016, Archives of Women's Mental Health · Consensus statement

  • ISPMD consensus on the management of premenstrual disorders

    International experts agree that PMDD treatment starts with a careful diagnosis, and that the main options either act on serotonin or switch off ovulation; CBT, calcium and chasteberry need more study.

    Nevatte 2013, Archives of Women's Mental Health · Consensus statement

  • Towards a consensus on diagnostic criteria, measurement and trial design of the premenstrual disorders: the ISPMD Montreal consensus

    An international group of experts agreed on shared rules for diagnosing and studying premenstrual disorders.

    O'Brien 2011, Archives of Women's Mental Health · Consensus statement

  • Comparison of premenstrual dysphoric disorder treatment with antidepressants and combined oral contraceptives: a systematic review with network meta-analysis

    When studies were compared indirectly, both SSRIs and the pill helped PMDD; there are no direct head-to-head trials yet.

    Berni 2026, Journal of Psychiatric Research · Meta-analysis

  • Efficacy of psychological interventions for premenstrual syndrome: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials

    Across 20 trials, talking therapies reduced premenstrual symptoms and related low mood, anxiety and irritability, though most trials were in PMS rather than confirmed PMDD.

    Liu 2026, Journal of Affective Disorders · Meta-analysis

  • Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues for premenstrual syndrome (PMS)

    Medicines that switch off the ovaries work well for severe premenstrual symptoms but cause menopause-like side effects; adding hormones back to protect the body is under-tested and progestogen may bring symptoms back.

    Naheed 2025, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews · Meta-analysis

  • A systematic review and meta-analysis on the comorbidity of premenstrual dysphoric disorder or premenstrual syndrome with mood disorders: prevalence, clinical and neurobiological correlates

    Around four in ten people with depression or bipolar disorder also have premenstrual disorders in these studies.

    Bengi 2025, The British Journal of Psychiatry · Meta-analysis

  • The prevalence of premenstrual dysphoric disorder: Systematic review and meta-analysis

    When PMDD is confirmed with daily symptom tracking over two cycles, 3.2% of women have it (1.6% in community samples); studies without that tracking give 7.7%, likely an overestimate.

    Reilly 2024, Journal of Affective Disorders · Meta-analysis

  • Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors for premenstrual syndrome and premenstrual dysphoric disorder

    SSRI antidepressants probably ease premenstrual symptoms. On average, trials of daily dosing showed a larger benefit than trials of luteal-only dosing, but both beat placebo; side effects such as nausea, low energy and sleepiness are more common than on placebo.

    Jespersen 2024, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews · Meta-analysis

  • Oral contraceptives containing drospirenone for premenstrual syndrome

    A combined pill containing drospirenone may ease PMDD symptoms a little to moderately over three cycles, but placebo helped too, side effects were more common, and the evidence is low quality.

    Ma 2023, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews · Meta-analysis

  • Suicidal Risk in Women with Premenstrual Syndrome and Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

    Women with PMDD are more likely to have suicidal thoughts and attempts, which is why doctors should ask about this.

    Prasad 2021, Journal of Women's Health · Meta-analysis

  • Efficacy of combined oral contraceptives for depressive symptoms and overall symptomatology in premenstrual syndrome: pairwise and network meta-analysis of randomized trials

    Across 9 trials, the combined pill helped premenstrual symptoms overall but did not clearly help low mood specifically, and no one brand stood out as better than the rest.

    de Wit 2021, American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology · Meta-analysis
