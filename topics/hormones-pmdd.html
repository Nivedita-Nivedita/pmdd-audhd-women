Topics / Hormones & PMDD

Hormones & PMDD

PMDD is a different brain response to normal hormone changes. Women with ADHD screen positive for it more often.

What is solid

  • Hormone levels are normal in PMDD. The brain responds differently to normal changes (Schmidt 1998).
  • Two cycles of daily ratings are the standard way to confirm the pattern; C-PASS is one standardised scoring method (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). A clinician can assess and offer care while you track.
  • Oral contraceptives carried a higher depression risk in young women with ADHD (Lundin 2023).
  • Premenstrual disorders are linked to higher suicide mortality (Opatowski 2024).

What is thin or missing

  • Women with ADHD screen positive for PMDD more often in several studies (reported as odds ratios or prevalence ratios that differ by study), but no study has confirmed this with daily ratings.
  • Adjusting stimulant dose across the cycle rests on a 9-woman case series.
  • The estrogen–dopamine explanation has not been tested in ADHD.

How it connects

  • Emotion & rejection sensitivity: Cyclical mood worsening (PMDD or premenstrual exacerbation) Supported
  • ADHD in women: Estrogen may modulate dopamine; ADHD symptoms vary with the cycle Preliminary
  • Life stages & populations: Premenstrual disorder history predicts perinatal depression Supported
  • Body & daily function: POTS symptoms vary across the menstrual cycle Preliminary

Claims checked

False   Established

Popular claim, paraphrased
A hormone blood test (or a one-off quiz) can tell you if you have PMDD.

PMDD is confirmed with prospective daily symptom ratings over at least two cycles; the C-PASS, one standard scoring system, matched expert diagnosis with an estimated 98% correct classification in 200 women, and retrospective reports of premenstrual worsening poorly predicted the prospective diagnosis (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). Provisional (one-off) diagnosis roughly doubles prevalence (7.7% vs 3.2% confirmed; Reilly 2024). Hormone levels are typically normal in PMDD; symptoms reflect an abnormal response to normal hormone change (Schmidt 1998), so blood tests cannot diagnose it. A clinician can still assess someone provisionally and discuss care while they track, and urgent help never waits for a chart.

Closer to the evidence: Blood tests and one-off questionnaires can't confirm PMDD; prospective daily tracking over at least two cycles confirms the pattern, and a clinician can assess you and offer care while you track.

Sources
  • Eisenlohr-Moul 2017
  • Reilly 2024
  • Epperson 2012
  • Schmidt 1998

False   Established

Popular claim, paraphrased
PMDD is caused by low progesterone or a hormone imbalance.

When ovarian hormones were switched off and then added back at normal levels, symptoms returned in women with PMS but not in women without it (Schmidt 1998). In 12 women with PMDD, symptoms came back when hormone levels rose but not once levels were steady, pointing to the change rather than the level (Schmidt 2017). Reviews describe PMDD as an abnormal brain response to normal hormone fluctuations (Hantsoo 2020).

Closer to the evidence: In PMDD hormone levels are usually normal; the brain reacts unusually to the normal monthly rise and fall of hormones.

  • Schmidt 1998
  • Schmidt 2017
  • Hantsoo 2020
  • Dubey 2017

Unsupported   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Magnesium helps (or fixes) PMDD.

In the most-cited trial, 44 women with mild premenstrual symptoms took magnesium, B6, both or placebo for one cycle each: magnesium alone did not differ from placebo, and only the combination slightly reduced anxiety-type symptoms (De Souza 2000). A review of 31 nutrition RCTs found insufficient evidence for magnesium (Robinson 2024), and a meta-analysis of 13 observational studies found no overall difference in serum magnesium in PMS (Moslehi 2019). No trial tests magnesium in confirmed PMDD.

Closer to the evidence: There is no good evidence that magnesium treats PMDD; small PMS studies are inconclusive, and women with PMS don't reliably have low magnesium.

Sources
  • De Souza 2000
  • Robinson 2024
  • Moslehi 2019

Unsupported   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Most autistic women have PMDD; 92% have severe PMS.

The 92% figure comes from 26 women with autism and learning disability rated by observers, using a loose threshold (Obaydi 2008). In a community study, PMDD was not significantly more common in autistic women (14.3% vs 9.5%; Groenman 2022), while another found raised provisional PMDD rates in both ADHD and autistic women (Groenman 2026) and a third found no link with autistic traits (Tsuji 2026). Autistic people do describe cyclical worsening of sensory and emotional difficulties (Steward 2018).

Closer to the evidence: Many autistic women report that sensory and emotional difficulties worsen before their period, but whether PMDD itself is more common in autism is unresolved.

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
A hysterectomy (or having your ovaries removed) cures PMDD.

Only removing the ovaries, not the womb alone, stops the hormonal cycle. In 14 women with severe, treatment-resistant PMS, hysterectomy with oophorectomy plus oestrogen ended the cyclic pattern (Casper 1990); in a retrospective series of 47 women, 93.6% reported complete resolution, all on HRT afterwards (Cronje 2004). Neither had a control group. In women treated for heavy periods, hysterectomy with the ovaries kept reduced premenstrual symptoms no more than a hormonal IUS (Leminen 2012). Experts consider oophorectomy as one of the ovulation-suppressing options (Nevatte 2013); clinical guidance puts SSRIs and the pill first and GnRH analogues with add-back next (Wagner-Schuman 2023), and GnRH agonists work but long-term use is limited by menopausal side effects (Naheed 2025).

Closer to the evidence: Removing the ovaries (usually with the womb, followed by HRT) ended cyclical symptoms in small, uncontrolled series of carefully selected women whom other treatments had failed; a hysterectomy that keeps the ovaries has not been shown to treat PMDD, and surgery is a last resort.

Sources
  • Casper 1990
  • Cronje 2004
  • Leminen 2012
  • Nevatte 2013
  • Wagner-Schuman 2023
  • Naheed 2025

Overstated   Established

Popular claim, paraphrased
If your ADHD, anxiety or depression gets worse before your period, that's PMDD.

Worsening of an existing condition before menstruation is called premenstrual exacerbation (PME); around 60% of women with mood disorders report it, and treatments that help PMDD show less or no benefit for it, from sparse and mostly small studies (Kuehner 2021). Prospective daily ratings, for example DRSP ratings scored with the C-PASS, establish whether the PMDD pattern is met; telling PMDD from PME, or recognising both, still needs a clinician's judgement (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). In 28 outpatients with suicidal thoughts, 93% had their peak around menstruation, yet none met full PMDD criteria (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Premenstrual worsening of ADHD, anxiety or depression is often premenstrual exacerbation (PME), which differs from PMDD; daily tracking plus a clinician's judgement helps tell them apart, and care or urgent help doesn't need to wait for it.

Sources
  • Kuehner 2021
  • Eisenlohr-Moul 2017
  • Eisenlohr-Moul 2022
  • O'Brien 2011

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Menopause is always worse for women with ADHD.

The evidence is mixed. In a population-based cohort, women with self-reported ADHD more often had severe perimenopausal symptoms (54.2% vs 30.1%; Jakobsdottir Smari 2025). In a survey of 656 women aged 45 to 60, no clear difference in menopausal complaints was found between women with and without an ADHD diagnosis at any stage after correction for multiple testing (Chapman 2025). 'Always' is not supported.

Closer to the evidence: Some women with ADHD have a harder perimenopause, but studies disagree and it is not inevitable.

Sources
  • Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025
  • Chapman 2025

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
PMDD gets worse in perimenopause.

In a population cohort of 436 women followed for 5 years, premenstrual syndrome decreased across the menopause transition, but women with PMS were more likely to report hot flushes (odds ratio 2.09), depressed mood (odds ratio 2.34) and poor sleep (odds ratio 1.72) (Freeman 2004, Obstet Gynecol). In daily diaries from 290 women, no clear effect of late reproductive vs early menopausal-transition stage on premenstrual symptom severity was found (Mitchell 2022). Among depressed perimenopausal women, 21% met criteria for premenstrual dysphoria vs 3% of non-depressed women (Richards 2006), and depressive symptoms are more likely during the transition (Freeman 2004, Arch Gen Psychiatry). We did not find a study following prospectively confirmed PMDD through the late transition (search last updated October 2026).

Closer to the evidence: On average, premenstrual symptoms don't seem to worsen in the run-up to menopause, but women with a history of premenstrual symptoms are more vulnerable to low mood and other symptoms in perimenopause, and irregular cycles can make patterns hard to see.

Sources
  • Freeman 2004
  • Mitchell 2022
  • Richards 2006
  • Freeman 2004

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Vitex (chasteberry) helps PMDD.

Trials are almost all in PMS, not confirmed PMDD. Pooled placebo-controlled trials showed a large effect (Hedges g -1.21), but with high risk of bias, extreme heterogeneity (I² 91%) and likely publication bias, so the authors say the effect is at best an overestimate (Verkaik 2017). In the 3 best-reported trials (520 women), vitex users were 2.57 times more likely to have PMS remission (Csupor 2019). A review of 8 RCTs was positive but noted varying diagnostic criteria, preparations and outcomes (Cerqueira 2017). Experts say vitex warrants further exploration (Nevatte 2013).

Closer to the evidence: Chasteberry may ease PMS symptoms, but the trials are mostly poor quality and in PMS rather than PMDD; it is not a proven PMDD treatment.

Sources
  • Verkaik 2017
  • Csupor 2019
  • Cerqueira 2017
  • Nevatte 2013

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Calcium and vitamin B6 help PMDD.

In a multicentre RCT of 466 analysed women with moderate-to-severe PMS, calcium reduced luteal symptoms by 48% vs 30% with placebo by the third cycle (Thys-Jacobs 1998). A systematic review of 9 B6 trials (940 patients) found benefit (OR 2.32) but low trial quality (Wyatt 1999). A review of 31 nutrition trials found consistent benefits for B6, calcium and zinc on psychological PMS symptoms, though only 1 trial had low risk of bias (Robinson 2024). All of this is PMS, not confirmed PMDD; experts list calcium as warranting further exploration (Nevatte 2013). Safety: NHS advice says large amounts of supplemental vitamin B6 can cause peripheral neuropathy (loss of feeling in arms and legs), usually reversible on stopping but occasionally permanent.

Closer to the evidence: Calcium and vitamin B6 have some of the better supplement evidence for PMS, but they haven't been tested in diagnosed PMDD and are not substitutes for proven treatments; high-dose B6 can damage nerves, so check with a pharmacist or clinician.

Sources
  • Thys-Jacobs 1998
  • Wyatt 1999
  • Robinson 2024
  • Nevatte 2013

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Estrogen boosts dopamine, so ADHD gets worse whenever estrogen drops in your cycle.

In 32 young women tracked daily for 35 days, lower estradiol together with higher progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the next day, with rises in the early follicular and early luteal phases (Roberts 2018). In healthy women, estradiol's effect on working memory depended on baseline dopamine (Jacobs 2011), but the estrogen-dopamine mechanism has not been tested directly in ADHD. A review of 11 studies found only suggestive, heterogeneous evidence (Osianlis 2025).

Closer to the evidence: Some women notice ADHD symptoms shift across the cycle, and early research links this to falling estrogen, but the mechanism hasn't been tested in ADHD.

Sources
  • Roberts 2018
  • Jacobs 2011
  • Osianlis 2025
  • Kooij 2025

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
PMDD is caused by allopregnanolone, the brain-active breakdown product of progesterone.

A leading theory is that in PMDD the brain's GABA-A receptors react paradoxically to normal luteal-phase allopregnanolone levels (Backstrom 2014; Hantsoo 2020). In a small trial (16 women with PMDD), blocking allopregnanolone production with high-dose dutasteride reduced irritability, sadness and anxiety, while a low dose did not (Martinez 2015). A drug designed to counter allopregnanolone (sepranolone, 206 women) did not beat placebo on its main outcome (Backstrom 2021).

Closer to the evidence: One leading theory is that in PMDD the brain reacts unusually to normal changes in allopregnanolone; treatments built on this are still experimental.

Sources
  • Bäckström 2014
  • Hantsoo 2020
  • Martinez 2015
  • Bäckström 2021

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
POTS gets worse before your period.

The menstrual cycle does seem to affect POTS symptoms, but the small studies point to the period itself, not the days before. Women with POTS (65) reported more lightheadedness than controls in every phase, worst during menses (Peggs 2012). In 10 women with POTS, standing tolerance was poorer and presyncope more common in the early follicular phase than the mid-luteal phase (Fu 2010). Another study of 10 women found no phase difference in heart rate, sympathetic nerve activity or presyncope (Stickford 2015). We did not find a study of the premenstrual (late-luteal) days specifically (search last updated October 2026). These studies describe association, not cause.

Closer to the evidence: Many women with POTS notice symptoms change across the cycle; the small studies so far suggest lightheadedness tends to be worst during the period itself.

Sources
  • Peggs 2012
  • Fu 2010
  • Stickford 2015

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Perimenopause makes ADHD much worse.

In a population-based cohort, severe perimenopausal symptoms were more common in 535 women with self-reported ADHD than in 4,857 without (54.2% vs 30.1%; prevalence ratio 1.80), with the gap largest at ages 35-39 (Jakobsdottir Smari 2025). In a survey of 656 women aged 45 to 60 (245 with an ADHD diagnosis), no clear difference in menopausal complaints was found at any stage after correction for multiple testing (Chapman 2025). A systematic review of 11 studies found only suggestive evidence linking ADHD symptoms to hormones and called for menopause research (Osianlis 2025). An expert review concludes that hormonal transitions worsen ADHD and mood but that treatment research is lacking (Kooij 2025). Neither cohort followed ADHD symptoms within the same women across the transition.

Closer to the evidence: Some women with ADHD report more severe perimenopause symptoms, but studies disagree, and how menopause changes ADHD itself, and how best to treat it, has barely been studied.

Sources
  • Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025
  • Chapman 2025
  • Osianlis 2025
  • Kooij 2025

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Taking an SSRI only in the two weeks before your period (or only on symptom days) works as well as taking it every day.

Both schedules have evidence. In the 2024 Cochrane review (34 RCTs), SSRIs beat placebo when taken continuously (SMD -0.69; 7 studies, 1055 participants) and in the luteal phase only (SMD -0.39; 6 studies, 687 participants), both moderate-certainty evidence; the review concluded SSRIs are probably more effective when taken continuously (P = 0.03 for subgroup difference) (Jespersen 2024). That is a between-subgroup comparison of placebo-controlled trials, so it describes an average, not what suits each person. In a three-arm RCT that compared schedules directly, luteal-phase sertraline did not differ from continuous dosing, and both beat placebo; women with symptoms after their period ended did worse on either schedule (Freeman 2004). Luteal sertraline beat placebo in 281 women with PMDD (responders 50% vs 26% by cycle 1) (Halbreich 2002). Symptom-onset dosing missed its primary outcome (P = .06), though it helped on depression and irritability scales (Yonkers 2015). The 2013 version of the Cochrane review had found no clear difference between schedules (Marjoribanks 2013); it is superseded by the 2024 update.

Closer to the evidence: Taking an SSRI only in the luteal phase does help PMDD, but the 2024 Cochrane review found daily dosing probably works better on average; starting only when symptoms begin has weaker evidence. Which schedule suits you is decided with your prescriber.

Sources
  • Jespersen 2024
  • Freeman 2004
  • Halbreich 2002
  • Yonkers 2015
  • Marjoribanks 2013

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
The pill causes depression, especially if you have ADHD.

In over 1 million Danish women, hormonal contraception users had higher rates of first antidepressant use than non-users (combined pill adjusted incidence rate ratio 1.23; teenagers 1.8) (Skovlund 2016), and current or recent use was linked to first suicide attempt compared with never-use (hazard ratio 1.97) (Skovlund 2018). In Swedish women aged 15-24, women with ADHD had a higher depression risk than women without ADHD whatever their contraception (adjusted hazard ratio 3.69); combined-pill users with ADHD had aHR 5.19 compared with non-users without ADHD and 6.10 compared with pill users without ADHD (Lundin 2023). Because every comparison uses women without ADHD as the reference, much of the excess reflects ADHD itself rather than the pill. These are observational associations, not proof the pill causes depression.

Closer to the evidence: Large registry studies link hormonal contraception, especially in teens, with a higher chance of later depression; young women with ADHD have a higher depression risk with or without the pill. It's worth monitoring mood after starting a method, not avoiding contraception by default.

Sources
  • Skovlund 2016
  • Skovlund 2018
  • Lundin 2023

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
The pill treats PMDD.

Drospirenone-containing combined pills have the best evidence: the 2023 Cochrane review (5 RCTs, 858 women, most with PMDD) found they may improve overall premenstrual symptoms (SMD -0.41; low-quality evidence) and functional impairment, with effects that may be small to moderate; placebo also had a significant effect, and they may increase adverse effects (OR 2.31) and withdrawal for adverse effects (OR 3.41). No included trial reported rare serious harms such as venous thromboembolism, and it is unknown whether the pill works after three cycles or beats other pills (Ma 2023). In the larger trial the number needed to treat was 8 (Yonkers 2005; also Pearlstein 2005). Across 9 trials, combined pills improved overall premenstrual symptoms (SMD 0.41) but not depressive symptoms specifically (de Wit 2021). A continuous levonorgestrel pill gave mixed results (Halbreich 2012; Freeman 2012). Clinical guidance places the pill second line after SSRIs (Wagner-Schuman 2023). Combined pills carry a small blood-clot risk and are not suitable for everyone (for example migraine with aura, or age 35 or over and smoking), per NHS information.

Closer to the evidence: Some combined pills, especially those containing drospirenone, may reduce PMDD symptoms modestly, more for overall than mood symptoms; they aren't safe for everyone (clot risk, migraine with aura, smoking at 35+), and a pill can also worsen mood for some women.

Sources
  • Ma 2023
  • Yonkers 2005
  • Pearlstein 2005
  • de Wit 2021
  • Halbreich 2012
  • Freeman 2012
  • Wagner-Schuman 2023

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Women with ADHD are 3x more likely to have PMDD.

Every study so far finds women with ADHD more likely to screen positive for (provisional) PMDD, but the figures use different measures and populations. In 715 young women recruited online, provisional PMDD was 31.4% with self-reported clinical ADHD and 41.1% with questionnaire-defined ADHD vs 9.8% without (the '3x' is a crude ratio of these percentages); the highest risk was in questionnaire-defined ADHD plus depression/anxiety (risk ratio 4.53, 95% CI 3.10-6.61) (Broughton 2025). In 2,000 employed Japanese women, ADHD traits had an adjusted odds ratio of 6.49 (4.10-10.2) for PMDD on a DSM-IV-TR-based scale (Tsuji 2026). Odds ratios and risk ratios are not interchangeable and should not be merged into one range. None used prospective daily ratings; provisional diagnosis roughly doubles PMDD prevalence (7.7% vs 3.2% confirmed; Reilly 2024). An often-quoted earlier figure (Dorani 2021) came from an ADHD clinic sample with no control group.

Closer to the evidence: Several studies find women with ADHD are considerably more likely to screen positive for PMDD, with estimates that vary by measure and sample; none has yet confirmed this with prospective daily ratings.

Sources
  • Broughton 2025
  • Tsuji 2026
  • Groenman 2026
  • Reilly 2024
  • Dorani 2021
  • Jaholkowski 2023

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Your ADHD meds stop working before your period, so you should increase the dose that week.

The only study we found is an uncontrolled case series of 9 consecutive women at one adult ADHD clinic, treated for ADHD and co-occurring conditions (including depression and PMDD), who described premenstrual worsening or an insufficient effect of their established dose; raising the stimulant dose in the premenstrual week was followed by improved ADHD and mood symptoms with minimal adverse events over 6-24 months, and all chose to continue (de Jong 2023). With no comparison group or blinding, it cannot show the dose increase caused the improvement, and it did not measure drug efficacy across the cycle. In 32 regularly cycling women not selected for ADHD, hormone patterns predicted next-day ADHD symptoms (Roberts 2018). We did not find a randomised trial of cycle-adjusted dosing in our search (last updated October 2026).

Closer to the evidence: Some women report their ADHD symptoms get worse before their period; raising the dose that week helped in one 9-person case series without a comparison group. Any change to medication is made only with your prescriber.

Sources
  • de Jong 2023
  • Roberts 2018

Mostly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Doctors dismiss PMDD.

In an Australian survey of 267 people with PMDD, participants had seen an average of 5.1 providers seeking a diagnosis; 54% reported medical gaslighting, 56% misdiagnosis, and only 19% were diagnosed in line with DSM recommendations (Border 2026). Interviews with 27 women in nine countries described medical gas-lighting and diagnostic odysseys (Mosalisa 2026), and UK women described 'lost decades' of misdiagnosis (Osborn 2020). In 2,512 survey respondents, gynaecologists and psychiatrists were rated highest on PMDD knowledge (Hantsoo 2022). A scoping review lists limited provider knowledge and guideline non-adherence among barriers (Nayak 2025). All samples are self-selected and may over-represent people with poor experiences, so they show the problem is common, not how often it happens overall. Not every clinical disagreement is dismissal: a clinician may reasonably ask for prospective ratings, or consider PME or another diagnosis.

Closer to the evidence: Many people with PMDD report being dismissed or misdiagnosed, often for years. Bringing daily symptom ratings, asking what the clinician would need to confirm or rule out PMDD, asking for their reasoning to be recorded, and seeking a second opinion or referral to a gynaecologist or psychiatrist who knows PMDD can help.

Sources
  • Border 2026
  • Mosalisa 2026
  • Osborn 2020
  • Hantsoo 2022
  • Nayak 2025

Mostly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
PMDD seriously raises suicide risk.

A systematic review (13 studies; 10 meta-analysed) found that, compared with women without premenstrual disturbances, women with PMDD had higher odds of suicide attempt (odds ratio 6.97, 95% CI 2.98-16.29) and suicidal ideation (odds ratio 3.95, 2.97-5.24); the authors support routine suicide-risk assessment in moderate-to-severe premenstrual disturbance (Prasad 2021). In a Swedish nationwide matched cohort, 67,748 women with premenstrual disorders (identified from inpatient/outpatient diagnoses and drug dispensing) were compared with 338,740 matched women over a mean 6.2 years: all-cause mortality was not raised (adjusted HR 0.91, 0.82-1.02) but death by suicide was (HR 1.92, 1.43-2.60), and higher in those diagnosed before age 25 (HR 3.84, 1.18-12.45, a wide interval) (Opatowski 2024). These are group-level associations from observational data and cannot predict an individual's risk.

Closer to the evidence: PMDD is linked to substantially higher odds of suicidal thoughts and attempts at a group level; that can't predict any one person's risk, but it is a reason to ask directly and get help early. If you are having suicidal thoughts, get help now (see ../help.html).

Sources
  • Prasad 2021
  • Opatowski 2024

Mostly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
SSRIs work for PMDD within days, unlike for depression.

Sertraline's benefit appeared swiftly in the first month of treatment (Freeman 2004), and luteal-only sertraline already separated from placebo by the first treatment cycle (50% vs 26% responders) (Halbreich 2002). The 2024 Cochrane review found luteal-phase-only SSRIs beat placebo (SMD -0.39, moderate certainty), consistent with a fast onset, though continuous dosing was probably more effective on average (Jespersen 2024). No trial reports a day-by-day onset, and in Freeman 2004 placebo had caught up by the third month.

Closer to the evidence: SSRIs often start helping PMDD within the first cycle, faster than is usual for depression, which is why luteal-only dosing can work; daily dosing was more effective on average, and the schedule is decided with a prescriber.

Sources
  • Freeman 2004
  • Halbreich 2002
  • Jespersen 2024

Key research

114 papers, strongest evidence first. See all in the library.

  • Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors for premenstrual syndrome and premenstrual dysphoric disorder

    SSRI antidepressants probably ease premenstrual symptoms. On average, trials of daily dosing showed a larger benefit than trials of luteal-only dosing, but both beat placebo; side effects such as nausea, low energy and sleepiness are more common than on placebo.

    Jespersen 2024, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews · Established

  • Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors for premenstrual syndrome

    SSRI antidepressants reduce premenstrual symptoms whether taken every day or only in the luteal phase, though side effects such as nausea and low energy are relatively frequent.

    Marjoribanks 2013, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews · Established

  • Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder: Evidence for a New Category for DSM-5

    Experts reviewed the research and agreed PMDD is a real, well-defined condition that deserves its own place in the main psychiatric diagnostic manual.

    Epperson 2012, American Journal of Psychiatry · Established

  • Premenstrual syndrome

    Mild premenstrual changes are common; a smaller group have severe symptoms, mostly mood-related, that disrupt daily life.

    Yonkers 2008, The Lancet · Established

  • Towards a consensus on diagnostic criteria, measurement and trial design of the premenstrual disorders: the ISPMD Montreal consensus

    An international group of experts agreed on shared rules for diagnosing and studying premenstrual disorders.

    O'Brien 2011, Archives of Women's Mental Health · Established

  • Daily Record of Severity of Problems (DRSP): reliability and validity

    The DRSP daily symptom diary gives consistent, valid ratings of premenstrual symptoms and impairment and can show change with treatment.

    Endicott 2005, Archives of Women's Mental Health · Established

  • Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues for premenstrual syndrome (PMS)

    Medicines that switch off the ovaries work well for severe premenstrual symptoms but cause menopause-like side effects; adding hormones back to protect the body is under-tested and progestogen may bring symptoms back.

    Naheed 2025, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews · Supported

  • A systematic review and meta-analysis on the comorbidity of premenstrual dysphoric disorder or premenstrual syndrome with mood disorders: prevalence, clinical and neurobiological correlates

    Around four in ten people with depression or bipolar disorder also have premenstrual disorders in these studies.

    Bengi 2025, The British Journal of Psychiatry · Supported

  • Mortality Risk Among Women With Premenstrual Disorders in Sweden

    Women with premenstrual disorders do not die earlier overall, but they do have a higher risk of suicide, especially if diagnosed young.

    Opatowski 2024, JAMA Network Open · Supported

  • Associations Between Symptoms of Premenstrual Disorders and Polygenic Liability for Major Psychiatric Disorders

    Women whose genes carry more risk for ADHD and other mental health conditions were somewhat more likely to report premenstrual mood symptoms, suggesting a shared genetic background.

    Jaholkowski 2023, JAMA Psychiatry · Supported

  • Hormonal Contraceptive Use and Risk of Depression Among Young Women With Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder

    Young women with ADHD who used the contraceptive pill were more likely to be diagnosed with depression than pill users without ADHD, which is worth discussing when choosing contraception.

    Lundin 2023, Journal of the American Academy of Child & Adolescent Psychiatry · Supported

  • Oral contraceptives containing drospirenone for premenstrual syndrome

    A combined pill containing drospirenone may ease PMDD symptoms a little to moderately over three cycles, but placebo helped too, side effects were more common, and the evidence is low quality.

    Ma 2023, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews · Supported
