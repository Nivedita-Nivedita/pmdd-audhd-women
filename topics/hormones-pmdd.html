Topics / Hormones & PMDD

Hormones & PMDD

PMDD is a different brain response to normal hormone changes. Women with ADHD screen positive for it more often.

What is solid

  • Hormone levels are normal in PMDD. The brain responds differently to normal changes (Schmidt 1998).
  • Two cycles of daily ratings are the standard way to confirm the pattern; C-PASS is one standardised scoring method (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). A clinician can assess and offer care while you track.
  • Oral contraceptives carried a higher depression risk in young women with ADHD (Lundin 2023).
  • Premenstrual disorders are linked to higher suicide mortality (Opatowski 2024).

What is thin or missing

  • Women with ADHD screen positive for PMDD more often in several studies (reported as odds ratios or prevalence ratios that differ by study), but no study has confirmed this with daily ratings.
  • Adjusting stimulant dose across the cycle rests on a 9-woman case series.
  • The estrogen–dopamine explanation has not been tested in ADHD.

How it connects

  • Emotion & rejection sensitivity: Cyclical mood worsening (PMDD or premenstrual exacerbation) Supported
  • ADHD in women: Estrogen may modulate dopamine; ADHD symptoms vary with the cycle Preliminary
  • Life stages & populations: Premenstrual disorder history predicts perinatal depression Supported
  • Body & daily function: POTS symptoms vary across the menstrual cycle Preliminary

Claims checked

False   Established

“A hormone blood test (or a one-off quiz) can tell you if you have PMDD.”

PMDD is diagnosed from daily symptom ratings over at least two cycles; a standard scoring system (C-PASS) matched expert diagnosis with about 98% accuracy in 200 women, and retrospective reports of premenstrual worsening poorly predicted the prospective diagnosis (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). Retrospective screening roughly doubles prevalence (7.7% vs 3.2% with confirmed diagnosis; Reilly 2024). Hormone levels are typically normal in PMDD, so blood tests cannot diagnose it.

Closer to the evidence: PMDD is diagnosed by tracking symptoms daily for at least two cycles; blood tests and one-off questionnaires cannot confirm it.

Sources
  • Eisenlohr-Moul 2017
  • Reilly 2024
  • Epperson 2012
  • Schmidt 1998

False   Established

“PMDD is caused by low progesterone or a hormone imbalance.”

When ovarian hormones were switched off and then added back at normal levels, symptoms returned in women with PMS but not in women without it (Schmidt 1998). In 12 women with PMDD, symptoms came back when hormone levels rose but not once levels were steady, pointing to the change rather than the level (Schmidt 2017). Reviews describe PMDD as an abnormal brain response to normal hormone fluctuations (Hantsoo 2020).

Closer to the evidence: In PMDD hormone levels are usually normal; the brain reacts unusually to the normal monthly rise and fall of hormones.

Sources
  • Schmidt 1998
  • Schmidt 2017
  • Hantsoo 2020
  • Dubey 2017

Unsupported   Preliminary

“Magnesium helps (or fixes) PMDD.”

In the most-cited trial, 44 women with mild premenstrual symptoms took magnesium, B6, both or placebo for one cycle each: magnesium alone did not differ from placebo, and only the combination slightly reduced anxiety-type symptoms (De Souza 2000). A review of 31 nutrition RCTs found insufficient evidence for magnesium (Robinson 2024), and a meta-analysis of 13 observational studies found no overall difference in serum magnesium in PMS (Moslehi 2019). No trial tests magnesium in confirmed PMDD.

Closer to the evidence: There is no good evidence that magnesium treats PMDD; small PMS studies are inconclusive, and women with PMS don't reliably have low magnesium.

Sources
  • De Souza 2000
  • Robinson 2024
  • Moslehi 2019

Unsupported   Preliminary

“Most autistic women have PMDD; 92% have severe PMS.”

The 92% figure comes from 26 women with autism and learning disability rated by observers, using a loose threshold (Obaydi 2008). In a community study, PMDD was not significantly more common in autistic women (14.3% vs 9.5%; Groenman 2022), while another found raised provisional PMDD rates in both ADHD and autistic women (Groenman 2026) and a third found no link with autistic traits (Tsuji 2026). Autistic people do describe cyclical worsening of sensory and emotional difficulties (Steward 2018).

Closer to the evidence: Many autistic women report that sensory and emotional difficulties worsen before their period, but whether PMDD itself is more common in autism is unresolved.

Sources
  • Obaydi 2008
  • Groenman 2022
  • Groenman 2026
  • Tsuji 2026
  • Steward 2018

Overstated   Preliminary

“A hysterectomy (or having your ovaries removed) cures PMDD.”

Only removing the ovaries, not the womb alone, stops the hormonal cycle. In 14 women with severe, treatment-resistant PMS, hysterectomy with oophorectomy plus oestrogen ended the cyclic pattern (Casper 1990); in a retrospective series of 47 women, 93.6% reported complete resolution, all on HRT afterwards (Cronje 2004). Neither had a control group. In women treated for heavy periods, hysterectomy with the ovaries kept reduced premenstrual symptoms no more than a hormonal IUS (Leminen 2012). Experts consider oophorectomy as one of the ovulation-suppressing options (Nevatte 2013); clinical guidance puts SSRIs and the pill first and GnRH analogues with add-back next (Wagner-Schuman 2023), and GnRH agonists work but long-term use is limited by menopausal side effects (Naheed 2025).

Closer to the evidence: Removing the ovaries (usually with the womb, followed by HRT) ended cyclical symptoms in small, uncontrolled series of carefully selected women whom other treatments had failed; a hysterectomy that keeps the ovaries has not been shown to treat PMDD, and surgery is a last resort.

Sources
  • Casper 1990
  • Cronje 2004
  • Leminen 2012
  • Nevatte 2013
  • Wagner-Schuman 2023
  • Naheed 2025

Overstated   Established

“If your ADHD, anxiety or depression gets worse before your period, that's PMDD.”

Worsening of an existing condition before menstruation is called premenstrual exacerbation (PME); around 60% of women with mood disorders report it, and treatments that help PMDD show less or no benefit for it (Kuehner 2021). Daily ratings scored with the C-PASS separate PMDD from PME (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). In 28 outpatients with suicidal thoughts, 93% had their peak around menstruation, yet none met full PMDD criteria (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Premenstrual worsening of ADHD, anxiety or depression is often premenstrual exacerbation (PME), which differs from PMDD; daily tracking helps tell them apart.

Sources
  • Kuehner 2021
  • Eisenlohr-Moul 2017
  • Eisenlohr-Moul 2022
  • O'Brien 2011

Overstated   Preliminary

“Menopause is always worse for women with ADHD.”

The evidence is mixed. In a Swedish population cohort, women with ADHD had more severe perimenopausal symptoms (54.2% vs 30.1% severe; Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025). In another study, women with ADHD did not report more menopausal complaints at any stage after correction for multiple testing (Chapman 2025). 'Always' is not supported.

Closer to the evidence: Some women with ADHD have a harder perimenopause, but studies disagree and it is not inevitable.

Sources
  • Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025
  • Chapman 2025

Overstated   Preliminary

“PMDD gets worse in perimenopause.”

In a population cohort of 436 women followed for 5 years, premenstrual syndrome decreased across the menopause transition, but women with PMS were more likely to have hot flushes (OR 2.09), depressed mood (OR 2.34) and poor sleep (OR 1.72) (Freeman 2004, Obstet Gynecol). In daily diaries from 290 women, reproductive stage had no effect on premenstrual symptom severity (Mitchell 2022). Among depressed perimenopausal women, 21% met criteria for premenstrual dysphoria vs 3% of non-depressed women (Richards 2006), and depressive symptoms rise during the transition (Freeman 2004, Arch Gen Psychiatry). No study follows confirmed PMDD through the late transition.

Closer to the evidence: On average premenstrual symptoms don't worsen in the run-up to menopause, but women with a history of premenstrual symptoms are more vulnerable to depression and other symptoms in perimenopause, and irregular cycles can make patterns hard to see.

Sources
  • Freeman 2004
  • Mitchell 2022
  • Richards 2006
  • Freeman 2004

Overstated   Preliminary

“Vitex (chasteberry) helps PMDD.”

Trials are almost all in PMS, not confirmed PMDD. Pooled placebo-controlled trials showed a large effect (Hedges g -1.21), but with high risk of bias, extreme heterogeneity (I² 91%) and likely publication bias, so the authors say the effect is at best an overestimate (Verkaik 2017). In the 3 best-reported trials (520 women), vitex users were 2.57 times more likely to have PMS remission (Csupor 2019). A review of 8 RCTs was positive but noted varying diagnostic criteria, preparations and outcomes (Cerqueira 2017). Experts say vitex warrants further exploration (Nevatte 2013).

Closer to the evidence: Chasteberry may ease PMS symptoms, but the trials are mostly poor quality and in PMS rather than PMDD; it is not a proven PMDD treatment.

Sources
  • Verkaik 2017
  • Csupor 2019
  • Cerqueira 2017
  • Nevatte 2013

Partly true   Preliminary

“Calcium and vitamin B6 help PMDD.”

In a multicentre RCT of 466 analysed women with moderate-to-severe PMS, 1200 mg/day calcium reduced luteal symptoms by 48% vs 30% with placebo by the third cycle (Thys-Jacobs 1998). A systematic review of 9 B6 trials (940 patients) found benefit (OR 2.32) but low trial quality, at doses up to 100 mg/day (Wyatt 1999). A review of 31 nutrition trials found consistent benefits for B6, calcium and zinc on psychological PMS symptoms, though only 1 trial had low risk of bias (Robinson 2024). All of this is PMS, not confirmed PMDD; experts list calcium as warranting further exploration (Nevatte 2013).

Closer to the evidence: Calcium and vitamin B6 have some of the better supplement evidence for PMS, but they haven't been tested in diagnosed PMDD and are not substitutes for proven treatments.

Sources
  • Thys-Jacobs 1998
  • Wyatt 1999
  • Robinson 2024
  • Nevatte 2013

Partly true   Preliminary

“Estrogen boosts dopamine, so ADHD gets worse whenever estrogen drops in your cycle.”

In 32 young women tracked daily for 35 days, lower estradiol together with higher progesterone or testosterone predicted more ADHD symptoms the next day, with rises in the early follicular and early luteal phases (Roberts 2018). In healthy women, estradiol's effect on working memory depended on baseline dopamine (Jacobs 2011), but the estrogen-dopamine mechanism has not been tested directly in ADHD. A review of 11 studies found only suggestive, heterogeneous evidence (Osianlis 2025).

Closer to the evidence: Some women notice ADHD symptoms shift across the cycle, and early research links this to falling estrogen, but the mechanism hasn't been tested in ADHD.

Sources
  • Roberts 2018
  • Jacobs 2011
  • Osianlis 2025
  • Kooij 2025

Partly true   Preliminary

“PMDD is caused by allopregnanolone, the brain-active breakdown product of progesterone.”

A leading theory is that in PMDD the brain's GABA-A receptors react paradoxically to normal luteal-phase allopregnanolone levels (Backstrom 2014; Hantsoo 2020). In a small trial (16 women with PMDD), blocking allopregnanolone production with high-dose dutasteride reduced irritability, sadness and anxiety, while a low dose did not (Martinez 2015). A drug designed to counter allopregnanolone (sepranolone, 206 women) did not beat placebo on its main outcome (Backstrom 2021).

Closer to the evidence: One leading theory is that in PMDD the brain reacts unusually to normal changes in allopregnanolone; treatments built on this are still experimental.

Sources
  • Bäckström 2014
  • Hantsoo 2020
  • Martinez 2015
  • Bäckström 2021

Partly true   Preliminary

“POTS gets worse before your period.”

The menstrual cycle does seem to affect POTS symptoms, but the small studies point to the period itself, not the days before. Women with POTS (65) reported more lightheadedness than controls in every phase, worst during menses (Peggs 2012). In 10 women with POTS, standing tolerance was poorer and presyncope more common in the early follicular phase than the mid-luteal phase (Fu 2010). Another study of 10 women found no phase difference in heart rate, sympathetic nerve activity or presyncope (Stickford 2015). No study has looked specifically at the premenstrual (late-luteal) days.

Closer to the evidence: Many women with POTS notice symptoms change across the cycle; the small studies so far suggest lightheadedness tends to be worst during the period itself.

Sources
  • Peggs 2012
  • Fu 2010
  • Stickford 2015

Partly true   Preliminary

“Perimenopause makes ADHD much worse.”

In 5,392 Icelandic women, severe perimenopausal symptoms were more common with self-reported ADHD (54.2% vs 30.1%), with the gap largest at ages 35-39 (Jakobsdottir Smari 2025). A systematic review of 11 studies found only suggestive evidence linking ADHD symptoms to hormones and called for menopause research (Osianlis 2025). An expert review concludes that hormonal transitions worsen ADHD and mood but that treatment research is lacking (Kooij 2025).

Closer to the evidence: Women with ADHD report more severe perimenopause symptoms, but how menopause changes ADHD itself, and how best to treat it, is barely studied.

Sources
  • Jakobsdóttir Smári 2025
  • Osianlis 2025
  • Kooij 2025

Partly true   Supported

“The pill causes depression, especially if you have ADHD.”

In over 1 million Danish women, hormonal contraception was linked to more first antidepressant use (combined pill RR 1.23; teenagers RR 1.8) (Skovlund 2016) and to first suicide attempt (HR 1.97) (Skovlund 2018). In Swedish women aged 15-24, ADHD alone carried about a 3.7-fold depression risk, and combined-pill users with ADHD had the highest risk (aHR 5.19 vs non-users without ADHD) (Lundin 2023). These are observational associations, not proof the pill causes depression.

Closer to the evidence: Large registry studies link hormonal contraception, especially in teens, to a higher chance of depression, and young women with ADHD on the pill had the highest risk; it's worth monitoring mood, not avoiding contraception by default.

Sources
  • Skovlund 2016
  • Skovlund 2018
  • Lundin 2023

Partly true   Supported

“The pill treats PMDD.”

One specific pill has the best evidence: drospirenone 3 mg with ethinyl estradiol 20 μg in a 24/4 regimen beat placebo in two placebo-controlled PMDD trials (Cochrane: MD -7.92), though placebo also had a large effect and nausea, spotting and breast pain were more common (Lopez 2012). In the larger trial the number needed to treat was 8 (Yonkers 2005; also Pearlstein 2005). Across 9 trials, combined pills improved overall premenstrual symptoms (SMD 0.41) but not depressive symptoms specifically, and no pill beat another (de Wit 2021). A continuous no-break levonorgestrel pill gave mixed results (Halbreich 2012; Freeman 2012). Clinical guidance places the pill second line after SSRIs (Wagner-Schuman 2023).

Closer to the evidence: Some combined pills, especially drospirenone 24/4, can reduce PMDD symptoms modestly, more for physical and overall symptoms than for low mood; they are a second-line option, and a pill can also worsen mood for some women.

Sources
  • Lopez 2012
  • Yonkers 2005
  • Pearlstein 2005
  • de Wit 2021
  • Halbreich 2012
  • Freeman 2012
  • Wagner-Schuman 2023

Partly true   Preliminary

“Women with ADHD are 3x more likely to have PMDD.”

Every study so far finds women with ADHD more likely to screen positive for PMDD: 31.4% with self-reported clinical ADHD (41.1% with questionnaire-defined ADHD) vs 9.8% without in 715 young women (Broughton 2025), and an adjusted OR of 6.49 for ADHD traits in 2,000 Japanese women (Tsuji 2026). But none used the prospective daily ratings a PMDD diagnosis requires, one-off questionnaires roughly double PMDD rates (7.7% vs 3.2% confirmed; Reilly 2024), and estimates range from about 2.4 to 6.5 times depending on method. An often-quoted earlier figure (Dorani 2021) came from an ADHD clinic sample with no control group.

Closer to the evidence: Several studies find women with ADHD are considerably more likely to screen positive for PMDD, roughly 2-6 times in surveys; none has yet confirmed this with the daily tracking needed for a diagnosis.

Sources
  • Broughton 2025
  • Tsuji 2026
  • Groenman 2026
  • Reilly 2024
  • Dorani 2021
  • Jaholkowski 2023

Partly true   Preliminary

“Your ADHD meds stop working before your period, so you should increase the dose that week.”

In a case series of 9 women with premenstrual worsening, raising the stimulant dose in the premenstrual week improved inattention, irritability and energy with minimal side effects, and all chose to continue (de Jong 2023). There is no randomised trial of cycle-adjusted dosing, and no data on whether stimulants worsen or improve PMDD.

Closer to the evidence: Some women find their ADHD medication seems weaker before their period; adjusting the dose that week is a promising idea from a 9-person case series, to be tried only with a prescriber.

Sources
  • de Jong 2023
  • Roberts 2018

Mostly true   Preliminary

“Doctors dismiss PMDD.”

In an Australian survey of 267 people, participants had seen an average of 5.1 providers seeking a PMDD diagnosis, 54% reported medical gaslighting, 56% misdiagnosis, and only 19% were diagnosed in line with DSM recommendations (Border 2026). Interviews with 27 women in nine countries described medical gas-lighting and diagnostic odysseys regardless of health system (Mosalisa 2026), and UK women described 'lost decades' of misdiagnosis (Osborn 2020). Provider knowledge varies: in 2,512 IAPMD survey respondents, gynaecologists and psychiatrists were rated highest on PMDD knowledge (Hantsoo 2022). A scoping review lists limited provider knowledge and guideline non-adherence among barriers (Nayak 2025). All samples are self-selected.

Closer to the evidence: Many people with PMDD report being dismissed or misdiagnosed, often for years; bringing two months of daily symptom ratings and seeing a clinician who knows PMDD can help.

Sources
  • Border 2026
  • Mosalisa 2026
  • Osborn 2020
  • Hantsoo 2022
  • Nayak 2025

Mostly true   Supported

“PMDD seriously raises suicide risk.”

A meta-analysis of 10 studies found higher odds of suicide attempt (OR 6.97) and suicidal ideation (OR 3.95) in women with PMDD, though nearly all used provisional diagnoses (Prasad 2021). In Swedish registers, 67,748 women with clinically diagnosed premenstrual disorders had no higher all-cause mortality but higher suicide death (HR 1.92), highest when diagnosed before age 25 (HR 3.84) (Opatowski 2024).

Closer to the evidence: PMDD is linked to a substantially higher risk of suicidal thoughts and attempts, so it deserves to be taken seriously and asked about directly.

Sources
  • Prasad 2021
  • Opatowski 2024

Mostly true   Supported

“SSRIs work for PMDD within days, unlike for depression.”

Sertraline's benefit appeared swiftly in the first month of treatment (Freeman 2004), and luteal-only sertraline already separated from placebo by the first treatment cycle (50% vs 26% responders) (Halbreich 2002). That luteal-only dosing works at all implies a fast onset (Marjoribanks 2013). No trial reports a day-by-day onset, and in Freeman 2004 placebo had caught up by the third month.

Closer to the evidence: SSRIs usually start helping PMDD within the first cycle, much faster than for depression, which is why taking them only in the luteal phase can work.

Sources
  • Freeman 2004
  • Halbreich 2002
  • Marjoribanks 2013

Mostly true   Supported

“Taking an SSRI only in the two weeks before your period (or only on symptom days) works as well as taking it every day.”

In a three-arm RCT, luteal-phase sertraline did not differ from continuous dosing, and both beat placebo; women with symptoms after their period ended did worse on either schedule (Freeman 2004). Luteal sertraline beat placebo in 281 women with PMDD (responders 50% vs 26% by cycle 1) (Halbreich 2002), and a Cochrane review found no clear difference between luteal and continuous dosing (Marjoribanks 2013). The shortest schedule, starting at symptom onset, is weaker: it missed its primary outcome (P = .06), though it helped on depression and irritability scales and caused no discontinuation symptoms (Yonkers 2015). ISPMD standards name SSRIs as first-line treatment (Ismaili 2016).

Closer to the evidence: For PMDD, taking an SSRI only in the luteal phase works about as well as daily dosing; starting only when symptoms begin has weaker evidence, and daily dosing may suit you better if symptoms spill outside the premenstrual weeks.

Sources
  • Freeman 2004
  • Halbreich 2002
  • Marjoribanks 2013
  • Yonkers 2015
  • Ismaili 2016

Key research

112 papers, strongest evidence first. See all in the library.

  • Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors for premenstrual syndrome

    SSRI antidepressants reduce premenstrual symptoms whether taken every day or only in the luteal phase, though side effects such as nausea and low energy are relatively frequent.

    Marjoribanks 2013, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews · Established

  • Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder: Evidence for a New Category for DSM-5

    Experts reviewed the research and agreed PMDD is a real, well-defined condition that deserves its own place in the main psychiatric diagnostic manual.

    Epperson 2012, American Journal of Psychiatry · Established

  • Premenstrual syndrome

    Mild premenstrual changes are common; a smaller group have severe symptoms, mostly mood-related, that disrupt daily life.

    Yonkers 2008, The Lancet · Established

  • Towards a consensus on diagnostic criteria, measurement and trial design of the premenstrual disorders: the ISPMD Montreal consensus

    An international group of experts agreed on shared rules for diagnosing and studying premenstrual disorders.

    O'Brien 2011, Archives of Women's Mental Health · Established

  • Daily Record of Severity of Problems (DRSP): reliability and validity

    The DRSP daily symptom diary gives consistent, valid ratings of premenstrual symptoms and impairment and can show change with treatment.

    Endicott 2005, Archives of Women's Mental Health · Established

  • Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues for premenstrual syndrome (PMS)

    Medicines that switch off the ovaries work well for severe premenstrual symptoms but cause menopause-like side effects; adding hormones back to protect the body is under-tested and progestogen may bring symptoms back.

    Naheed 2025, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews · Supported

  • A systematic review and meta-analysis on the comorbidity of premenstrual dysphoric disorder or premenstrual syndrome with mood disorders: prevalence, clinical and neurobiological correlates

    Around four in ten people with depression or bipolar disorder also have premenstrual disorders in these studies.

    Bengi 2025, The British Journal of Psychiatry · Supported

  • Mortality Risk Among Women With Premenstrual Disorders in Sweden

    Women with premenstrual disorders do not die earlier overall, but they do have a higher risk of suicide, especially if diagnosed young.

    Opatowski 2024, JAMA Network Open · Supported

  • Associations Between Symptoms of Premenstrual Disorders and Polygenic Liability for Major Psychiatric Disorders

    Women whose genes carry more risk for ADHD and other mental health conditions were somewhat more likely to report premenstrual mood symptoms, suggesting a shared genetic background.

    Jaholkowski 2023, JAMA Psychiatry · Supported

  • Hormonal Contraceptive Use and Risk of Depression Among Young Women With Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder

    Young women with ADHD who used the contraceptive pill were more likely to be diagnosed with depression than pill users without ADHD, which is worth discussing when choosing contraception.

    Lundin 2023, Journal of the American Academy of Child & Adolescent Psychiatry · Supported

  • Efficacy of combined oral contraceptives for depressive symptoms and overall symptomatology in premenstrual syndrome: pairwise and network meta-analysis of randomized trials

    Across 9 trials, the combined pill helped premenstrual symptoms overall but did not clearly help low mood specifically, and no one brand stood out as better than the rest.

    de Wit 2021, American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology · Supported

  • Exacerbation of Psychosis During the Perimenstrual Phase of the Menstrual Cycle: Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

    People with psychotic disorders were admitted to hospital more often around their periods than would be expected by chance.

    Reilly 2019, Schizophrenia Bulletin · Supported
