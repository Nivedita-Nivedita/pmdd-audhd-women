Topics / Autism overlap, sensory & burnout

Autism overlap, sensory & burnout

ADHD and autism often co-occur, and women and girls with either are under-identified.

What is solid

  • About 28% of autistic people also have ADHD (Lai 2019).
  • The two conditions share a large genetic component (Ghirardi 2017).
  • The true male-to-female ratio in autism is about 3:1, which points to under-diagnosis in girls (Loomes 2017).
  • Deliberate social compensation is linked to late diagnosis and poorer mental health (Livingston 2019).

What is thin or missing

  • Autistic burnout is described mainly in qualitative work and early measurement studies. Separately, in general occupational research, work burnout overlaps heavily with depression (Bianchi 2021).
  • Research on ADHD burnout is limited to two small studies.
  • Self-screening questionnaires miss many autistic women (Ashwood 2016).

How it connects

  • ADHD in women: Frequent co-occurrence and shared genetics Established
  • Giftedness & masking: Compensation and camouflaging may lead to exhaustion Preliminary
  • Body & daily function: Sensory sensitivity in both ADHD and autism Supported

Claims checked

False   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Masking is something only autistic women do.

Undiagnosed adults with social difficulties described compensation strategies similar to those of diagnosed autistic adults (Livingston 2019), and adults with ADHD also camouflage, though less than autistic adults (van der Putten 2024). Females used more camouflaging strategies than males in a meta-analysis (Cruz 2025), so it is more common in women but not exclusive to them. In autistic adults, camouflaging was one predictor of suicide risk (Cassidy 2018).

Closer to the evidence: Camouflaging happens across genders and diagnoses; it is more common in autistic women and is linked to poorer mental health.

Sources
  • Livingston 2019
  • van der Putten 2024
  • Cruz 2025
  • Cassidy 2018

Unsupported   Unsupported

Popular claim, paraphrased
ADHD burnout is a recognised condition.

There is no validated 'ADHD burnout' construct and no prevalence data. In 171 employees, those with ADHD reported more job burnout, explained by executive-function difficulties (Turjeman-Levi 2024), and 62% of adults with ADHD in a clinic study met criteria for significant fatigue (Rogers 2016). ICD-11 classes burn-out as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition.

Closer to the evidence: Many adults with ADHD describe cycles of exhaustion and job burnout, but 'ADHD burnout' isn't a defined or diagnosable condition.

Sources
  • Turjeman-Levi 2024
  • Rogers 2016
  • Maslach 2016
  • Bianchi 2021

Unsupported   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
AuDHD is its own distinct condition.

'AuDHD' is a community word for having both autism and ADHD. That combination is real and common: in pooled studies of autistic people of all ages, 28% had ADHD (Lai 2019) or 38.5% currently (Rong 2021), and among children and adolescents with ADHD, 21% met the threshold for autism (Hollingdale 2019). The word needs no separate validation to describe that combination accurately. What has not been shown is the stronger claim that having both is a distinct third condition with its own profile: we did not find a validated research definition of one (search last updated October 2026), and a review concludes the two overlap heavily but remain distinct enough to warrant separate diagnoses (Antshel 2019). Having both matters: autistic adults with more ADHD symptoms reported less independence and lower quality of life (Yerys 2022), and in one survey an earlier ADHD diagnosis delayed autism diagnosis in children, by 2.6 years on average in girls (Kentrou 2019).

Closer to the evidence: 'AuDHD' accurately describes having both autism and ADHD, a real and common combination that can delay diagnosis; it is recorded as two diagnoses rather than a separate third one. 'Not a separate diagnosis' does not mean 'not real'.

Sources
  • Lai 2019
  • Rong 2021
  • Hollingdale 2019
  • Antshel 2019
  • Yerys 2022
  • Kentrou 2019

Unsupported   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Existing in a neurotypical world costs ADHD and autistic adults more energy; your cells keep the score.

No study has measured energy expenditure in neurodivergent adults. An energetic model of allostatic load proposes that chronic stress adds an energetic burden that competes with repair, as a testable model (Bobba-Alves 2022). A hypothesis paper extends this to autism, proposing camouflaging as chronic stress leading to mitochondrial allostatic load and burnout, and notes these mechanisms are largely unexplored (Mahony 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Researchers have proposed that chronic stress has an energy cost and that this may apply to neurodivergent people in environments that don't fit them, but it hasn't been measured.

Sources
  • Bobba-Alves 2022
  • Mahony 2022

Unsupported   Unsupported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Mitochondria under strain (more lactate, fewer antioxidants) are central to neurodivergent burnout.

No study has measured mitochondria in autistic, ADHD or occupational burnout. A systematic review found 23 experimental stress-mitochondria studies, all in male laboratory animals, and only 6 observational human studies (Picard & McEwen 2018). In humans, mothers caring for an autistic child had lower leukocyte mitochondrial health than control mothers (Picard 2018). Linking this to autistic burnout is a hypothesis (Mahony 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Chronic stress may affect mitochondria, but no study has yet looked at mitochondria in burnout.

  • Picard 2018

Unsupported   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Predictive-processing neuroscience shows that neurodivergent and gifted brains run faster, higher-resolution, more confident prediction models.

Predictive processing is a leading framework in neuroscience. But the autism versions of it propose something different from 'stronger predictions'. They suggest weaker priors, so that perception leans less on expectation (Pellicano 2012); or that prediction errors are given an inflexibly high weight, so sameness feels safer (Van de Cruys 2014); or that sensory evidence is over-weighted relative to prior beliefs (Lawson 2014). A review of 47 empirical studies mainly found difficulty learning predictive relationships, especially when cues were faint or inconsistent (Cannon 2021). We did not find an eligible review applying the framework to ADHD or giftedness.

Closer to the evidence: Predictive-processing theories suggest autistic perception may rely less on prior expectations or weigh surprises too heavily, not that neurodivergent brains predict better; for ADHD and giftedness the theory is untested.

Sources
  • Pellicano 2012
  • Van de Cruys 2014
  • Lawson 2014
  • Cannon 2021

Unsupported   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
The average life expectancy of autistic people is just 54 years.

The Swedish registry study usually linked to this figure compared 27,122 autistic people with 2,672,185 matched controls and found that 2.60% vs 0.91% died during follow-up (odds ratio 2.56, 95% CI 2.38-2.76), with raised mortality across almost all cause categories (Hirvikoski 2016). Its abstract reports odds of death during follow-up, not a life expectancy; an average age at death among people who died during a limited follow-up is not the same thing. Premature mortality is real and largely linked to medical conditions that can be looked for and treated; the figure of 54 is not a measured life expectancy, and no group figure predicts an individual's lifespan.

Closer to the evidence: Autistic people die earlier than average from a range of causes; the often-quoted '54 years' is not a measured life expectancy.

Sources
  • Hirvikoski 2016

Overstated   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic children are many times (sometimes quoted as 28 times) more likely than other children to think about or attempt suicide.

We did not find the single-clinic, parent-report study usually given for the '28 times' figure among the studies we checked, so we can't confirm it. A meta-analysis of 47 papers on autistic young people aged 25 or under found pooled prevalence of 25.2% for suicidal ideation, 8.3% for attempts and 0.2% for suicide deaths; self-reports gave higher estimates than parent reports, heterogeneity was substantial, adverse childhood experiences were strong risk factors and resilience was protective (O'Halloran 2022). Risk is clearly raised, but a single multiplier from one study overstates how precise the evidence is, and these group figures do not predict what will happen to any child. If you are struggling now, see Crisis support (../help.html); don't wait for thoughts to pass on their own.

Closer to the evidence: About one in four autistic young people have had suicidal thoughts in pooled studies; childhood adversity raises the risk and resilience lowers it, so support and direct questions matter.

Sources
  • O'Halloran 2022

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Masking causes autistic burnout and suicide.

A systematic review of 29 studies called the link between camouflaging and worse mental health 'preliminary' (Cook 2021). In 164 autistic adults, camouflaging was one of several predictors of suicide risk (Cassidy 2018), but the autistic burnout measure did not track masking in 141 adults (Arnold 2023). No longitudinal study shows masking leading to burnout and then suicidality.

Closer to the evidence: Masking is linked to poorer mental health in autistic people, but research hasn't shown that it directly causes burnout or suicide.

Sources
  • Cook 2021
  • Cassidy 2018
  • Arnold 2023
  • Cassidy 2020

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
1 in 3 autistic children show elevated blood lactate and pyruvate with low carnitine.

A 2012 meta-analysis found mitochondrial disease in 5.0% of autistic children, and abnormal mitochondrial biomarkers more often. Its full-text table gives separate pooled rates: elevated lactate 31.1% (6 studies, n = 479), elevated pyruvate 13.6% (2 studies, n = 110), low total carnitine 90% (1 study, n = 30). These are separate markers, not one combined profile, from small studies with referral bias (Rossignol 2012).

Closer to the evidence: About 31% of autistic children in small studies had raised blood lactate, and about 5% had mitochondrial disease; other markers come from even smaller studies.

Sources
  • Rossignol 2012

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic adults are 6x more likely to have alexithymia.

We did not find a source for the '6x' figure. A meta-analysis of 15 studies using the TAS-20 questionnaire found that 49.93% of autistic participants met alexithymia criteria, against 4.89% of neurotypical participants, and concluded that alexithymia is common but not universal in autism (Kinnaird 2019). Those percentages come from autistic and comparison samples in questionnaire studies, so they describe those samples rather than all autistic adults, and a multiplier depends on which comparison group is used.

Closer to the evidence: In questionnaire studies, about half of autistic participants (49.93%) met criteria for alexithymia, compared with 4.89% of neurotypical participants; it is common in autism but not universal.

Sources
  • Kinnaird 2019

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic burnout is a distinct condition, different from depression.

Autistic adults consistently describe burnout as chronic exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance to stimuli, arising from life's demands plus barriers to support (Raymaker 2020; Higgins 2021), and a systematic review of 48 studies describes the same pattern (Ali 2025). That makes it a recognised lived experience. Whether it can be told apart from depression is a separate, measurement question: in autistic adults, the main autistic burnout questionnaire correlated strongly with depression and could not reliably distinguish current from past burnout (Mantzalas 2024; Arnold 2023). In general working populations (not autistic samples), occupational burnout's core feature, exhaustion, correlated with depressive symptoms at r = .80 (Bianchi 2021); that finding is about work burnout and should not be read as evidence about autistic burnout. Autistic burnout is not a diagnosis in DSM-5 or ICD-11; ICD-11's 'burn-out' (QD85) refers only to chronic workplace stress. Lack of a validated way to separate the two does not mean autistic burnout is 'just depression'.

Closer to the evidence: Autistic burnout is a real, consistently described experience; current measures can't yet reliably tell it apart from depression, so both are worth assessing, alongside reducing demands and sensory load.

Sources
  • Raymaker 2020
  • Higgins 2021
  • Ali 2025
  • Mantzalas 2024
  • Arnold 2023
  • Bianchi 2021

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic inertia (being stuck unable to start, or unable to stop) is a real autistic experience.

A qualitative analysis of 501 Reddit posts with 9,955 comments described inertia as 'all or nothing' extremes of staying at rest or in motion, ranging from joyful to highly disabling and linked with fatigue; it corroborated earlier interview studies, and the authors note the topic is scarcely acknowledged in academic research (Ward 2026). There is no measure, prevalence estimate or comparison with non-autistic people or ADHD.

Closer to the evidence: Many autistic people describe inertia, being stuck unable to start or to stop, and early qualitative research takes it seriously; it hasn't yet been measured or compared with ADHD-related difficulty starting tasks.

Sources
  • Ward 2026

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Every adult mental-health assessment should screen for autism with standard questionnaires (such as the AQ-10, CAT-Q and RAADS-R), and doing so would reduce suicide.

The rationale is sound: suicidality is very common in autistic and possibly autistic adults (Newell 2023), and many adults, especially women, are missed (Lai 2015). But the tools are weaker in real clinical settings than in their validation studies. In 476 adults referred for assessment, the AQ had specificity 0.29, and 64% of those below cut-off were in fact autistic; anxiety inflated scores (Ashwood 2016). The AQ-10 and RAADS-R were validated mainly by comparing known cases with controls (Booth 2013; Ritvo 2010). The CAT-Q measures camouflaging and its validation reports good reliability but no diagnostic cut-off (Hull 2018). We did not find a study testing whether universal screening reduces suicide (search last updated October 2026).

Closer to the evidence: Clinicians should keep autism in mind in adult mental-health assessments; questionnaires can prompt a referral but cannot confirm or rule out autism, and no study shows screening alone prevents suicide.

Sources
  • Newell 2023
  • Lai 2015
  • Ashwood 2016
  • Booth 2013
  • Ritvo 2010
  • Hull 2018

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Masking is exhausting and, over time, wears down the ability to notice one's own hunger, fatigue and distress signals.

The exhaustion part is well described. Autistic adults report exhaustion as a consequence of camouflaging (Hull 2017), more camouflaging is associated with worse mental health in a review that called the conclusion preliminary (Cook 2021), and in 160 undergraduates camouflaging autistic traits was associated with lifetime suicidality (Cassidy 2020). Interoceptive and emotional-awareness differences are common in autistic people (alexithymia in 49.93% vs 4.89% in questionnaire studies; Kinnaird 2019), and some small studies find reduced interoceptive accuracy in ADHD (Bruton 2025). But we did not find a study testing whether masking causes poorer body awareness. The direction could run the other way, or both could reflect autistic traits.

Closer to the evidence: Masking is linked to exhaustion and poorer mental health, and many autistic and ADHD people have trouble reading body signals; whether masking causes that is not known.

Sources
  • Hull 2017
  • Cook 2021
  • Cassidy 2020
  • Kinnaird 2019
  • Bruton 2025

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Neuroimaging shows that masking is compensatory brain activity that doesn't switch off, even at rest.

In 110 healthy young adults without a diagnosis (mostly women), more camouflaging was associated with stronger default mode-cingulo-opercular resting-state connectivity only among those with higher autistic traits, and this mediated links with depression and anxiety; the design was cross-sectional (Guo 2026). In 119 adults, more camouflaging was associated with a heightened vmPFC self-representation response in autistic women only, on a task-based scan (Lai 2019). Camouflaging did not predict distress beyond social anxiety in 113 autistic adults (Roisenberg 2026).

Closer to the evidence: One study of students with autistic traits found more masking went with stronger links between brain networks at rest, tied to anxiety and low mood; it can't show masking causes this.

Sources
  • Guo 2026
  • Lai 2019
  • Roisenberg 2026
  • Cook 2021

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Recovering from neurodivergent burnout needs not rest or less stimulation, but permission to do things the neurodivergent way.

Autistic adults linked recovery to acceptance and social support, time off/reduced expectations, and doing things in an autistic way/unmasking (Raymaker 2020). A systematic review of 48 studies found rest, solitude and sensory relief, self-understanding and support helped (Ali 2025). Stimming was described as self-regulation (Kapp 2019), and motivated special interests went with higher wellbeing while very intense engagement went with lower wellbeing (Grove 2018). No intervention trial exists.

Closer to the evidence: Autistic adults say recovery takes real rest and sensory relief plus permission to do things their way, including unmasking, stimming and interests they enjoy.

Sources
  • Raymaker 2020
  • Ali 2025
  • Ali 2026
  • Kapp 2019
  • Grove 2018

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Up to two-thirds (66%) of autistic adults have considered suicide.

The 66% figure comes from 374 adults newly diagnosed with Asperger syndrome at one English specialist clinic between 2004 and 2013: 66% (243/367) reported lifetime suicidal ideation, 35% (127/365) plans or attempts and 31% depression; lifetime ideation was more likely than in a general UK population sample (odds ratio 9.6, 95% CI 7.6-11.9), and the authors identify depression as an important potential risk factor (Cassidy 2014). A meta-analysis of 36 studies (48,186 autistic and possibly autistic participants without intellectual disability) found pooled prevalence of 34.2% for lifetime suicidal ideation, 21.9% for plans and 24.3% for attempts and behaviours, with high heterogeneity (Newell 2023). 'Up to 66%' is accurate for that late-diagnosed clinic sample; across studies the pooled figure is about a third. These are rates in groups, not predictions for an individual. If you are struggling now, see Crisis support (../help.html); don't wait for thoughts to pass on their own.

Closer to the evidence: In pooled studies, about a third of autistic and possibly autistic adults without intellectual disability report having thought about suicide; it was two-thirds in one sample of adults newly diagnosed at a specialist clinic. Asking directly and treating depression matter.

Sources
  • Cassidy 2014
  • Newell 2023

Mostly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
A brain-imaging study found elevated lactate in 1 in 10 autistic adults even with normal blood lactate, so the strain was brain-specific.

In 64 autistic adults vs 58 controls, 9.4% of the autistic group had elevated posterior cingulate lactate vs 0% of controls (p = 0.028), and serum lactate did not correlate with brain lactate; the authors urge caution (Maier 2023). An earlier study found brain lactate in 13% of 75 autistic participants vs 1% of controls, and in 20% of adults (Goh 2014). Both interpret this as a possible mitochondrial subgroup, not burnout or strain.

Closer to the evidence: About 1 in 10 autistic adults in one study had raised lactate in one brain area, unrelated to blood lactate; it may mark a metabolic subgroup and wasn't studied in relation to burnout.

Sources
  • Maier 2023
  • Goh 2014

Mostly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic people are several times more likely to die by suicide than the general population, with the highest figures (around seven to nine times) quoted for autistic people without a learning disability.

National registry studies agree that suicide is more common in autistic people, but the size depends on the country, the measure and co-occurring conditions, and we did not find a peer-reviewed source for the specific 7x and 9x figures. In Denmark (6,559,266 residents, 35,020 autistic), autistic people had a higher rate of suicide death (adjusted incidence rate ratio 3.75, 95% CI 2.85-4.92) and of suicide attempt (3.19); autism without another psychiatric diagnosis gave an attempt rate ratio of 1.33 (0.99-1.78), against 9.27 with another diagnosis, and more than 90% of those who attempted or died had another diagnosis (Kõlves 2021). In Swedish registers (54,168 autistic people and 270,840 matched controls), risk was highest in autism without intellectual disability plus ADHD (suicide odds ratio 13.09, 95% CI 8.54-20.08; attempt odds ratio 7.25), and adjusting for psychiatric comorbidity reduced the risks (Hirvikoski 2020). In 372 English coroner inquest records, evidence of autism was found in 10.8% of suicide deaths against 1.1% expected (odds ratio 11.08, 95% CI 3.92-31.31) (Cassidy 2022). These are group associations from registry and record studies; they do not predict what will happen to any individual, and they point to co-occurring depression, anxiety and ADHD as conditions to recognise and treat. If you are struggling now, see Crisis support (../help.html); don't wait for thoughts to pass on their own.

Closer to the evidence: Suicide is several times more common in autistic people than in the general population in registry studies, especially in those without intellectual disability and with co-occurring conditions such as depression or ADHD; recognising and treating those conditions, and asking directly about suicidal thoughts, matters.

Sources
  • Kõlves 2021
  • Hirvikoski 2020
  • Cassidy 2022

Mostly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic people make up about 1% of the population but about 11% of people who die by suicide.

One English study reviewed 372 coroner inquest records of suicide deaths and found evidence of autism in 10.8%, against 1.1% expected (odds ratio 11.08, 95% CI 3.92-31.31). In a subsample of 29 next-of-kin interviews, adding possible undiagnosed autism raised this to 41.4% (odds ratio 19.76, 95% CI 2.36-165.84) (Cassidy 2022). The figures match that study, but it is one study of one region's records, the confidence intervals are wide, and the outcome is suicide deaths, not individual risk. It describes a group pattern, not what will happen to any autistic person. If you are struggling now, see Crisis support (../help.html); don't wait for thoughts to pass on their own.

Closer to the evidence: In one English study of coroner records, evidence of autism was found in about 11% of suicide deaths, far more than the 1% expected; it is one study with wide uncertainty, and a reason to make support easy to reach.

Sources
  • Cassidy 2022

True   Established

Popular claim, paraphrased
Lots of people with ADHD are also autistic, and vice versa.

Meta-analyses estimate ADHD in 28% (Lai 2019) to 38.5% (Rong 2021) of autistic people, and autism in 21% of children and adolescents with ADHD (Hollingdale 2019). In 1.9 million Swedes, autism carried far higher odds of ADHD (OR 22.33), with a twin and sibling pattern consistent with shared genetics (Ghirardi 2017). There is still no meta-analysis of autism in adults with ADHD.

Closer to the evidence: Autism and ADHD often occur together and share genetic roots, so if you have one, it's reasonable to ask about the other.

Sources
  • Lai 2019
  • Rong 2021
  • Hollingdale 2019
  • Ghirardi 2017
