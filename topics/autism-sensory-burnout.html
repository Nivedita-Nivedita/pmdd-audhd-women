Autism overlap, sensory & burnout

ADHD and autism often co-occur, and women with both are the most under-identified.

What is solid

  • About 28% of autistic people also have ADHD (Lai 2019).
  • The two conditions share a large genetic component (Ghirardi 2017).
  • The true male-to-female ratio in autism is about 3:1, which points to under-diagnosis in girls (Loomes 2017).
  • Deliberate social compensation is linked to late diagnosis and poorer mental health (Livingston 2019).

What is thin or missing

  • Autistic burnout is described in qualitative work; it overlaps heavily with depression (Bianchi 2021).
  • Research on ADHD burnout is limited to two small studies.
  • Self-screening questionnaires miss many autistic women (Ashwood 2016).

How it connects

  • ADHD in women: Frequent co-occurrence and shared genetics Established
  • Giftedness & masking: Compensation and camouflaging may lead to exhaustion Preliminary
  • Body & daily function: Sensory sensitivity in both ADHD and autism Supported

Claims checked

False Supported

“Adrenal fatigue is a real part of what happens in burnout.”

A systematic review of 58 studies found almost systematically conflicting cortisol results and concluded there is no substantiation that 'adrenal fatigue' is a medical condition (Cadegiani 2016). It is not recognised by endocrinology societies. Burnout, including autistic burnout, is real (Raymaker 2020), but 'adrenal fatigue' is not an explanation for it.

A more accurate way to say it: Burnout is real, but 'adrenal fatigue' is not a recognised condition and adrenal or saliva cortisol tests don't diagnose burnout.

Sources
  • Cadegiani 2016
  • Raymaker 2020

False Supported

“Masking is something only autistic women do.”

Undiagnosed adults with social difficulties described compensation strategies similar to those of diagnosed autistic adults (Livingston 2019), and adults with ADHD also camouflage, though less than autistic adults (van der Putten 2024). Females used more camouflaging strategies than males in a meta-analysis (Cruz 2025), so it is more common in women but not exclusive to them. In autistic adults, camouflaging was one predictor of suicide risk (Cassidy 2018).

A more accurate way to say it: Camouflaging happens across genders and diagnoses; it is more common in autistic women and is linked to poorer mental health.

Sources
  • Livingston 2019
  • van der Putten 2024
  • Cruz 2025
  • Cassidy 2018

Unsupported Popular, unsupported

“ADHD burnout is a recognised condition.”

There is no validated 'ADHD burnout' construct and no prevalence data. In 171 employees, those with ADHD reported more job burnout, explained by executive-function difficulties (Turjeman-Levi 2024), and 62% of adults with ADHD in a clinic study met criteria for significant fatigue (Rogers 2016). ICD-11 classes burn-out as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition.

A more accurate way to say it: Many adults with ADHD describe cycles of exhaustion and job burnout, but 'ADHD burnout' isn't a defined or diagnosable condition.

Sources
  • Turjeman-Levi 2024
  • Rogers 2016
  • Maslach 2016
  • Bianchi 2021

Unsupported Supported

“Anorexia is the female form of autism.”

Autistic traits are higher in anorexia (g = 0.88; Inal-Kaleli 2024), but in Danish registry data the link was no stronger than anorexia's links with depression or any psychiatric disorder, suggesting a non-specific relationship (Koch 2015). In Swedish twins, children who later developed anorexia did not have raised autistic traits at age 9, and raised traits at 18 were confined to acute illness (Dinkler 2020).

A more accurate way to say it: Autism and anorexia overlap more than chance and autistic traits should be considered in eating-disorder care, but anorexia is not a female form of autism.

Sources
  • Koch 2015
  • Dinkler 2020
  • Inal‐Kaleli 2024

Unsupported Popular, unsupported

“AuDHD is its own distinct condition.”

There is no validated research definition of a combined 'AuDHD' profile. A review concludes autism and ADHD overlap heavily but remain distinct enough to warrant separate diagnoses (Antshel 2019). Having both matters: autistic adults with more ADHD symptoms reported less independence and lower quality of life (Yerys 2022), and an earlier ADHD diagnosis delayed autism diagnosis, by 2.6 years on average in girls (Kentrou 2019).

A more accurate way to say it: 'AuDHD' is a useful community word for having both autism and ADHD, which is common and can delay diagnosis, but it isn't a separate diagnosis.

Sources
  • Antshel 2019
  • Yerys 2022
  • Kentrou 2019
  • Lai 2019

Unsupported Preliminary

“Existing in a neurotypical world costs ADHD and autistic adults more energy; your cells keep the score.”

No study has measured energy expenditure in neurodivergent adults. An energetic model of allostatic load proposes that chronic stress adds an energetic burden that competes with repair, as a testable model (Bobba-Alves 2022). A hypothesis paper extends this to autism, proposing camouflaging as chronic stress leading to mitochondrial allostatic load and burnout, and notes these mechanisms are largely unexplored (Mahony 2022).

A more accurate way to say it: Researchers have proposed that chronic stress has an energy cost and that this may apply to neurodivergent people in environments that don't fit them, but it hasn't been measured.

Sources
  • Bobba-Alves 2022
  • Mahony 2022

Unsupported Popular, unsupported

“Mitochondria under strain (more lactate, fewer antioxidants) are central to neurodivergent burnout.”

No study has measured mitochondria in autistic, ADHD or occupational burnout. A systematic review found 23 experimental stress-mitochondria studies, all in male laboratory animals, and only 6 observational human studies (Picard & McEwen 2018). In humans, mothers caring for an autistic child had lower leukocyte mitochondrial health than control mothers (Picard 2018). Linking this to autistic burnout is a hypothesis (Mahony 2022).

A more accurate way to say it: Chronic stress may affect mitochondria, but no study has yet looked at mitochondria in burnout.

Sources
  • Picard 2018
  • Picard 2018
  • Picard 2018
  • Mahony 2022

Unsupported Popular, unsupported

“Pathological demand avoidance is really a form of death anxiety, a fear of losing the self and autonomy; in values-driven teens the PDA or ODD label is often a misdiagnosis.”

The existential reading is usually offered as a hypothesis, which is fair, but it has not been studied. PDA itself is not in DSM-5 or ICD-11. A systematic review of 13 studies found definitions based on Newson's original descriptions, reliance on parent report, and little testing of alternative explanations such as anxiety (Kildahl 2021). The measurable evidence points to anxiety and intolerance of uncertainty. PDA-type features in a clinic sample included anxiety, and almost all of those children were autistic (O'Nions 2015). Demand avoidance was predicted by intolerance of uncertainty (Stuart 2019). Parent-rated PDA traits can be measured (O'Nions 2013). No study has examined whether existentially minded teenagers are misdiagnosed.

A more accurate way to say it: Extreme demand avoidance is best understood, on current evidence, as linked to anxiety and difficulty tolerating uncertainty, mostly in autistic young people; existential explanations are untested.

Sources
  • Kildahl 2021
  • O'Nions 2015
  • Stuart 2019
  • O'Nions 2013

Unsupported Preliminary

“Predictive-processing neuroscience shows that neurodivergent and gifted brains run faster, higher-resolution, more confident prediction models.”

Predictive processing is a leading framework, and popular accounts usually say so. But the autism versions propose the opposite of 'stronger predictions'. They suggest weaker priors, so that perception leans less on expectation (Pellicano 2012). Alternatively, prediction errors may be given an inflexibly high weight, so sameness feels safer (Van de Cruys 2014), or sensory evidence may be over-weighted relative to prior beliefs (Lawson 2014). A review of 47 empirical studies mainly found difficulty learning predictive relationships, especially when cues were faint or inconsistent (Cannon 2021). No eligible review on ADHD or giftedness was found.

A more accurate way to say it: Predictive-processing theories suggest autistic perception may rely less on prior expectations or weigh surprises too heavily, not that neurodivergent brains predict better; for ADHD and giftedness the theory is untested.

Sources
  • Pellicano 2012
  • Van de Cruys 2014
  • Lawson 2014
  • Cannon 2021

Unsupported Popular, unsupported

“Some 'easy', quiet babies were actually overwhelmed and in physiological freeze, which goes unnoticed and later turns into people-pleasing (fawning) and unexplained adult distress.”

This is usually offered openly as a hypothesis, and it has not been tested. Measured infant temperament does predict later outcomes. Behavioural inhibition raises the odds of later anxiety (OR 2.80; Sandstrom 2019), especially social anxiety (OR 7.59; Clauss 2012). Infants later diagnosed autistic were unusually passive and inactive at 6 months, then showed extreme distress by 12 months (Zwaigenbaum 2004), which partly fits the idea that 'easy' is not always fine. 'Fawn' has no validated measure. The polyvagal physiology behind 'freeze/shutdown' language is disputed (Grossman 2023).

A more accurate way to say it: Early temperament, including unusual passivity in some infants later found to be autistic, does predict later difficulties, but there is no evidence for a hidden infant 'freeze' that leads to adult fawning.

Sources
  • Sandstrom 2019
  • Clauss 2012
  • Zwaigenbaum 2004
  • Grossman 2023

Unsupported Supported

“The average life expectancy of autistic people is just 54 years.”

She repeats this as fact from a charity summary. The Swedish registry study usually cited compared 27,122 autistic people with matched controls and found 2.60% vs 0.91% died during follow-up (OR 2.56), from many medical causes (Hirvikoski 2016). Its abstract reports odds of death, not a life expectancy. An average age at death among people who died during a limited follow-up is not the same as life expectancy. Premature mortality is real; the figure of 54 is not a life expectancy.

A more accurate way to say it: Autistic people die earlier than average from a range of causes; the often-quoted '54 years' is not a measured life expectancy.

Sources
  • Hirvikoski 2016

Overstated Supported

“Autistic children are 28 times more likely to think about or attempt suicide (15% vs 0.5% in one study).”

She passes this on from a charity statistics page. The '28 times' figure comes from a single clinic study using parent report, which is not in this library. A meta-analysis of 47 papers on autistic youth found pooled prevalence of 25.2% for suicidal ideation and 8.3% for attempts. Self-reports gave higher estimates than parent reports, and heterogeneity was substantial (O'Halloran 2022). Risk is clearly raised, but a single multiplier from one study overstates how precise the evidence is.

A more accurate way to say it: About one in four autistic young people have had suicidal thoughts, well above non-autistic peers; childhood adversity raises the risk and resilience lowers it.

Sources
  • O'Halloran 2022

Overstated Preliminary

“High-capacity neurodivergent people don't burn out from long hours or intensity, which they thrive on, but from misalignment, meaningless work and under-use.”

Values mismatch is one recognised driver of occupational burnout (Maslach 2016), so misalignment matters. But autistic burnout research identifies cumulative life load, sensory and social overwhelm and camouflaging as contributors (Raymaker 2020; Ali 2025), and conceptual models treat demands and resources together (Mantzalas 2022). No evidence suggests any group is immune to load. The same writing elsewhere attributes burnout to accumulated sensory overload and over-adaptation, so the 'intensity is fuel' version is internally inconsistent.

A more accurate way to say it: Burnout comes from both too much load and the wrong kind of load; meaningful, well-fitting work helps, but no one is immune to overload.

Sources
  • Maslach 2016
  • Raymaker 2020
  • Ali 2025
  • Mantzalas 2022

Overstated Preliminary

“Masking causes autistic burnout and suicide.”

A systematic review of 29 studies called the link between camouflaging and worse mental health 'preliminary' (Cook 2021). In 164 autistic adults, camouflaging was one of several predictors of suicide risk (Cassidy 2018), but the autistic burnout measure did not track masking in 141 adults (Arnold 2023). No longitudinal study shows masking leading to burnout and then suicidality.

A more accurate way to say it: Masking is linked to poorer mental health in autistic people, but research hasn't shown that it directly causes burnout or suicide.

Sources
  • Cook 2021
  • Cassidy 2018
  • Arnold 2023
  • Cassidy 2020

Overstated Preliminary

“Nine in ten autistic women experience sexual violence.”

The figure comes from a self-selected French online survey of 225 autistic women: 88.4% on a standardised questionnaire and 68.9% on an open question (Cazalis 2022). A meta-analysis of 34 studies found pooled sexual victimisation of 40% in autistic people (Trundle 2022). Both figures are high, but the 90% figure is not a population prevalence.

A more accurate way to say it: Autistic women face a high risk of sexual violence; estimates range from about 40% in pooled studies to higher in some surveys.

Sources
  • Cazalis 2022
  • Trundle 2022

Partly true Preliminary

“1 in 3 autistic children show elevated blood lactate and pyruvate with low carnitine.”

A 2012 meta-analysis found mitochondrial disease in 5.0% of autistic children, and abnormal mitochondrial biomarkers more often. Its full-text table gives separate pooled rates: elevated lactate 31.1% (6 studies, n = 479), elevated pyruvate 13.6% (2 studies, n = 110), low total carnitine 90% (1 study, n = 30). These are separate markers, not one combined profile, from small studies with referral bias (Rossignol 2012).

A more accurate way to say it: About 31% of autistic children in small studies had raised blood lactate, and about 5% had mitochondrial disease; other markers come from even smaller studies.

Sources
  • Rossignol 2012

Partly true Supported

“Autistic adults are 6x more likely to have alexithymia.”

No source for '6x' was found. A meta-analysis of 15 studies found 49.93% of autistic people vs 4.89% of neurotypical people met TAS-20 alexithymia criteria, a ratio of about 10 (Kinnaird 2019). Alexithymia contributed to autistic burnout in a systematic review of 48 studies (Ali 2025).

A more accurate way to say it: About half of autistic adults have alexithymia, roughly ten times the rate in non-autistic people, and it may make burnout harder to notice.

Sources
  • Kinnaird 2019
  • Ali 2025

Partly true Preliminary

“Autistic burnout is a distinct condition, different from depression.”

Autistic adults consistently describe burnout as chronic exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance to stimuli (Raymaker 2020; Higgins 2021). But the main burnout measure correlated strongly with depression and could not tell current from past burnout (Mantzalas 2024; Arnold 2023), and general burnout overlaps heavily with depression (exhaustion-depression r = .80; Bianchi 2021). Autistic burnout is not in DSM-5 or ICD-11.

A more accurate way to say it: Autistic burnout is a real and consistently described experience, but research hasn't yet shown how it differs from depression, so both are worth assessing.

Sources
  • Raymaker 2020
  • Higgins 2021
  • Mantzalas 2024
  • Arnold 2023
  • Bianchi 2021

Partly true Preliminary

“Autistic inertia (being stuck unable to start, or unable to stop) is a real autistic experience.”

A qualitative analysis of 501 Reddit posts with 9,955 comments described inertia as 'all or nothing' extremes of staying at rest or in motion, ranging from joyful to highly disabling and linked with fatigue; it corroborated earlier interview studies, and the authors note the topic is scarcely acknowledged in academic research (Ward 2026). There is no measure, prevalence estimate or comparison with non-autistic people or ADHD.

A more accurate way to say it: Many autistic people describe inertia, being stuck unable to start or to stop, and early qualitative research takes it seriously; it hasn't yet been measured or compared with ADHD-related difficulty starting tasks.

Sources
  • Ward 2026

Partly true Preliminary

“Every adult mental-health assessment should screen for autism using the AQ-10 (6+), CAT-Q (100+) and RAADS-R (65+), and this will reduce suicide.”

She frames this as a clinical proposal. The rationale is sound: suicidality is very common in autistic and possibly autistic adults (Newell 2023), and many adults are missed (Lai 2015). But the tools are weaker in real clinical settings than in their validation studies. In 476 adults referred for assessment, the AQ had specificity 0.29, and 64% of those below cut-off were in fact autistic; anxiety inflated scores (Ashwood 2016). The AQ-10 and RAADS-R were validated mainly by comparing known cases with controls (Booth 2013; Ritvo 2011). The CAT-Q validation reports good reliability but no diagnostic cut-off (Hull 2018). No study has tested whether universal screening reduces suicide.

A more accurate way to say it: Clinicians should keep autism in mind in adult mental-health assessments; questionnaires can prompt a referral but cannot confirm or rule out autism, and no study shows screening alone prevents suicide.

Sources
  • Newell 2023
  • Lai 2015
  • Ashwood 2016
  • Booth 2013
  • Ritvo 2010
  • Hull 2018

Partly true Preliminary

“Masking is exhausting and over time erodes the ability to notice one's own hunger, fatigue and distress signals.”

The exhaustion part is well described. Autistic adults report exhaustion as a consequence of camouflaging (Hull 2017), and more camouflaging is associated with worse mental health (Cook 2021) and with suicidality (Cassidy 2020). Interoceptive and emotional-awareness differences are real in many autistic people (alexithymia 49.93% vs 4.89%; Kinnaird 2019), and some studies find reduced interoception in ADHD (Bruton 2025). But no study has tested whether masking causes poorer body awareness. The direction could run the other way, or both could reflect autistic traits.

A more accurate way to say it: Masking is linked to exhaustion and poorer mental health, and many autistic and ADHD people have trouble reading body signals; whether masking causes that is not known.

Sources
  • Hull 2017
  • Cook 2021
  • Cassidy 2020
  • Kinnaird 2019
  • Bruton 2025

Partly true Preliminary

“Neuroimaging shows that masking is compensatory brain activity that doesn't switch off, even at rest.”

In 110 healthy young adults without a diagnosis (mostly women), more camouflaging was associated with stronger default mode-cingulo-opercular resting-state connectivity only among those with higher autistic traits, and this mediated links with depression and anxiety; the design was cross-sectional (Guo 2026). In 119 adults, more camouflaging was associated with a heightened vmPFC self-representation response in autistic women only, on a task-based scan (Lai 2019). Camouflaging did not predict distress beyond social anxiety in 113 autistic adults (Roisenberg 2026).

A more accurate way to say it: One study of students with autistic traits found more masking went with stronger links between brain networks at rest, tied to anxiety and low mood; it can't show masking causes this.

Sources
  • Guo 2026
  • Lai 2019
  • Roisenberg 2026
  • Cook 2021

Partly true Preliminary

“Recovering from neurodivergent burnout needs not rest or less stimulation, but permission to do things the neurodivergent way.”

Autistic adults linked recovery to acceptance and social support, time off/reduced expectations, and doing things in an autistic way/unmasking (Raymaker 2020). A systematic review of 48 studies found rest, solitude and sensory relief, self-understanding and support helped (Ali 2025). Stimming was described as self-regulation (Kapp 2019), and motivated special interests went with higher wellbeing while very intense engagement went with lower wellbeing (Grove 2018). No intervention trial exists.

A more accurate way to say it: Autistic adults say recovery takes real rest and sensory relief plus permission to do things their way, including unmasking, stimming and interests they enjoy.

Sources
  • Raymaker 2020
  • Ali 2025
  • Ali 2026
  • Kapp 2019
  • Grove 2018

Partly true Supported

“Up to 66% of autistic adults have considered suicide.”

The 66% comes from 374 adults newly diagnosed at one English specialist clinic, where 66% reported lifetime suicidal ideation and 35% plans or attempts (OR 9.6 for ideation vs the general population) (Cassidy 2014). A meta-analysis of 36 studies of autistic and possibly autistic people without intellectual disability found pooled lifetime ideation of 34.2% and attempts/behaviours of 24.3% (Newell 2023). 'Up to' is accurate for late-diagnosed clinic samples, but the typical figure is closer to one in three.

A more accurate way to say it: About a third of autistic adults without intellectual disability report having thought about suicide; rates reach two-thirds in some samples of adults diagnosed late.

Sources
  • Cassidy 2014
  • Newell 2023

Mostly true Preliminary

“A brain-imaging study found elevated lactate in 1 in 10 autistic adults even with normal blood lactate, so the strain was brain-specific.”

In 64 autistic adults vs 58 controls, 9.4% of the autistic group had elevated posterior cingulate lactate vs 0% of controls (p = 0.028), and serum lactate did not correlate with brain lactate; the authors urge caution (Maier 2023). An earlier study found brain lactate in 13% of 75 autistic participants vs 1% of controls, and in 20% of adults (Goh 2014). Both interpret this as a possible mitochondrial subgroup, not burnout or strain.

A more accurate way to say it: About 1 in 10 autistic adults in one study had raised lactate in one brain area, unrelated to blood lactate; it may mark a metabolic subgroup and wasn't studied in relation to burnout.

Sources
  • Maier 2023
  • Goh 2014

Mostly true Supported

“Autistic people are up to seven times more likely to die by suicide than the general population, and nine times if they have no learning disability.”

She states this as fact, citing the charity Autistica. Registry studies agree suicide risk is clearly raised, but the size depends on the country and on co-occurring conditions. In Denmark, autistic people had 3.75 times the suicide rate, and more than 90% of those who attempted or died by suicide had another psychiatric diagnosis (Kõlves 2021). In Sweden, risk was highest in autism without intellectual disability plus ADHD (suicide OR 13.09), and adjusting for psychiatric comorbidity reduced the risks (Hirvikoski 2020). In England, evidence of autism was found in 10.8% of suicide deaths against 1.1% expected (Cassidy 2022).

A more accurate way to say it: Autistic people, especially those without intellectual disability, have a several-fold higher suicide risk, much of it tied to co-occurring depression, anxiety and ADHD.

Sources
  • Kõlves 2021
  • Hirvikoski 2020
  • Cassidy 2022

Mostly true Preliminary

“Autistic people make up about 1% of the population but about 11% of suicides.”

One English study of suicide deaths found evidence of autism in 10.8% against 1.1% expected (OR 11.08, 95% CI 3.92-31.31), rising to 41.4% in an interview subsample when possible undiagnosed autism was counted (Cassidy 2022). The figure is accurate for that study, but it is one study with wide confidence intervals.

A more accurate way to say it: In one English study, evidence of autism was about ten times more common among people who died by suicide than expected.

Sources
  • Cassidy 2022

True Established

“Lots of people with ADHD are also autistic, and vice versa.”

Meta-analyses estimate ADHD in 28% (Lai 2019) to 38.5% (Rong 2021) of autistic people, and autism in 21% of children and adolescents with ADHD (Hollingdale 2019). In 1.9 million Swedes, autism carried far higher odds of ADHD (OR 22.33), with a twin and sibling pattern consistent with shared genetics (Ghirardi 2017). There is still no meta-analysis of autism in adults with ADHD.

A more accurate way to say it: Autism and ADHD often occur together and share genetic roots, so if you have one, it's reasonable to ask about the other.

Sources
  • Lai 2019
  • Rong 2021
  • Hollingdale 2019
  • Ghirardi 2017
