Topics / Autism overlap, sensory & burnout

Autism overlap, sensory & burnout

ADHD and autism often co-occur, and women and girls with either are under-identified.

What is solid

  • About 28% of autistic people also have ADHD (Lai 2019).
  • The two conditions share a large genetic component (Ghirardi 2017).
  • The true male-to-female ratio in autism is about 3:1, which points to under-diagnosis in girls (Loomes 2017).
  • Deliberate social compensation is linked to late diagnosis and poorer mental health (Livingston 2019).

What is thin or missing

  • Autistic burnout is described mainly in qualitative work and early measurement studies. Separately, in general occupational research, work burnout overlaps heavily with depression (Bianchi 2021).
  • Research on ADHD burnout is limited to two small studies.
  • Self-screening questionnaires miss many autistic women (Ashwood 2016).

What the research says about common claims

Autism and ADHD often occur together: roughly 28–39% of autistic people have ADHD, and about 21% of children with ADHD are autistic.

Established evidence Several good studies or reviews agree.

Autism and ADHD often occur together and appear to share genetic roots, so if you have one, it's reasonable to ask about the other.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Lots of people with ADHD are also autistic, and vice versa.”

The popular version matches the evidence well.

What the studies found

Meta-analyses estimate ADHD in 28% (Lai 2019) to 38.5% (Rong 2021) of autistic people, and autism in 21% of children and adolescents with ADHD (Hollingdale 2019). In 1.9 million Swedes, autism carried far higher odds of ADHD (OR 22.33), with a twin and sibling pattern consistent with shared genetics (Ghirardi 2017). We did not find a meta-analysis of autism in adults with ADHD (search last updated October 2026).

You may have heard

“Autistic children are many times (sometimes quoted as 28 times) more likely than other children to think about or attempt suicide.”

The popular version goes further: it gives one precise multiplier that we could not confirm, while the pooled evidence shows raised risk without that precision.

What the studies found

We did not find the single-clinic, parent-report study usually given for the '28 times' figure among the studies we checked (search last updated October 2026), so we can't confirm it. A meta-analysis of 47 papers on autistic young people aged 25 or under found pooled prevalence of 25.2% for suicidal ideation, 8.3% for attempts and 0.2% for suicide deaths; self-reports gave higher estimates than parent reports, heterogeneity was substantial, adverse childhood experiences were strongly associated with higher risk and resilience with lower risk (O'Halloran 2022). Risk is clearly raised, but a single multiplier from one study overstates how precise the evidence is, and these group figures do not predict what will happen to any child. Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Up to two-thirds (66%) of autistic adults have considered suicide.”

The popular version takes the highest figure from one late-diagnosed clinic sample; the pooled figure is about half that.

What the studies found

The 66% figure comes from 374 adults newly diagnosed with Asperger syndrome at one English specialist clinic between 2004 and 2013: 66% (243/367) reported lifetime suicidal ideation, 35% (127/365) plans or attempts and 31% depression; lifetime ideation was more likely than in a general UK population sample (odds ratio 9.6, 95% CI 7.6-11.9), and the authors identify depression as an important potential risk factor (Cassidy 2014). A meta-analysis of 36 studies (48,186 autistic and possibly autistic participants without intellectual disability) found pooled prevalence of 34.2% for lifetime suicidal ideation, 21.9% for plans and 24.3% for attempts and behaviours, with high heterogeneity (Newell 2023). 'Up to 66%' is accurate for that late-diagnosed clinic sample; across studies the pooled figure is about a third. These are rates in groups, not predictions for an individual. Link to this claim

Autistic adults consistently describe burnout as chronic exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance to sensory input.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Autistic burnout is a consistently described experience across 48 studies. Current questionnaires correlate strongly with depression and cannot yet reliably tell the two apart, so whether it is a separate condition remains a measurement question.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Autistic burnout is a distinct condition, different from depression.”

The popular version goes further: the experience is well described, but research has not yet shown it can be measured separately from depression.

What the studies found

Autistic adults consistently describe burnout as chronic exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced tolerance to stimuli, arising from life's demands plus barriers to support (Raymaker 2020; Higgins 2021), and a systematic review of 48 studies describes the same pattern (Ali 2025). That makes it a recognised lived experience. Whether it can be told apart from depression is a separate, measurement question: in autistic adults, the main autistic burnout questionnaire correlated strongly with depression and could not reliably distinguish current from past burnout (Mantzalas 2024; Arnold 2023). In general working populations (not autistic samples), occupational burnout's core feature, exhaustion, correlated with depressive symptoms at r = .80 (Bianchi 2021); that finding is about work burnout and should not be read as evidence about autistic burnout. Autistic burnout is not a diagnosis in DSM-5 or ICD-11; ICD-11's 'burn-out' (QD85) refers only to chronic workplace stress. Lack of a validated way to separate the two does not mean autistic burnout is 'just depression'.

Camouflaging is reported across genders and in ADHD as well as autism, though autistic women report it more than autistic men.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Undiagnosed adults with social difficulties and adults with ADHD also camouflage, so it is not limited to autistic women, though they report it more often.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Masking is something only autistic women do.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs the other way: masking is more common in autistic women but not exclusive to them.

What the studies found

Undiagnosed adults with social difficulties described compensation strategies similar to those of diagnosed autistic adults (Livingston 2019), and adults with ADHD also camouflage, though less than autistic adults (van der Putten 2024). Females used more camouflaging strategies than males in a meta-analysis (Cruz 2025), so it is more common in women but not exclusive to them. In autistic adults, camouflaging was one predictor of suicide risk (Cassidy 2018).

Link to this claim

Having both autism and ADHD is linked with later autism diagnosis and lower quality of life, and is diagnosed as two conditions.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

'AuDHD' is a community term for this combination. We did not find a validated research definition of it as a distinct third condition (search last updated October 2026); 'not a separate diagnosis' does not mean 'not real'.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“AuDHD is its own distinct condition.”

The popular version goes further: the combination is well documented, but not as a separate condition.

What the studies found

'AuDHD' is a community word for having both autism and ADHD. That combination is real and common: in pooled studies of autistic people of all ages, 28% had ADHD (Lai 2019) or 38.5% currently (Rong 2021), and among children and adolescents with ADHD, 21% met the threshold for autism (Hollingdale 2019). The word needs no separate validation to describe that combination accurately. What has not been shown is the stronger claim that having both is a distinct third condition with its own profile: we did not find a validated research definition of one (search last updated October 2026), and a review concludes the two overlap heavily but remain distinct enough to warrant separate diagnoses (Antshel 2019). Having both matters: autistic adults with more ADHD symptoms reported less independence and lower quality of life (Yerys 2022), and in one survey an earlier ADHD diagnosis delayed autism diagnosis in children, by 2.6 years on average in girls (Kentrou 2019).

Link to this claim

In Swedish registers, autistic people were about two and a half times as likely to die during follow-up, from many different causes.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Autistic people die earlier than average from a range of causes, many medical. The often-quoted '54 years' is not a measured life expectancy, and no group figure predicts an individual's lifespan.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“The average life expectancy of autistic people is just 54 years.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs differently: the study reports raised odds of death, not an average lifespan of 54.

What the studies found

The Swedish registry study usually linked to this figure compared 27,122 autistic people with 2,672,185 matched controls and found that 2.60% vs 0.91% died during follow-up (odds ratio 2.56, 95% CI 2.38-2.76), with raised mortality across almost all cause categories (Hirvikoski 2016). Its abstract reports odds of death during follow-up, not a life expectancy; an average age at death among people who died during a limited follow-up is not the same thing. Premature mortality is real, and the authors attribute it to many different medical conditions; the figure of 54 is not a measured life expectancy, and no group figure predicts an individual's lifespan.

Link to this claim

Suicide is several times more common in autistic people in national registries and coroner records, mostly among those with co-occurring conditions such as depression or ADHD.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Raised risk is consistent across studies, highest in autism without intellectual disability plus ADHD. The '11% of suicides' figure matches one English coroner study; we did not find a peer-reviewed source for the 7x and 9x figures (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Autistic people are several times more likely to die by suicide than the general population, with the highest figures (around seven to nine times) quoted for autistic people without a learning disability.”

The popular version is close, but the multipliers vary by country and measure and depend heavily on co-occurring conditions.

“Autistic people make up about 1% of the population but about 11% of people who die by suicide.”

The figures match one English study of coroner records with wide uncertainty, which the popular version presents as a settled population figure.

What the studies found

National registry and record studies agree that suicide is more common in autistic people, but the size depends on the country, the measure and co-occurring conditions, and we did not find a peer-reviewed source for the specific 7x and 9x figures (search last updated October 2026). In Denmark (6,559,266 residents, 35,020 autistic), autistic people had a higher rate of suicide death (adjusted incidence rate ratio 3.75, 95% CI 2.85-4.92) and of suicide attempt (3.19); autism without another psychiatric diagnosis gave an attempt rate ratio of 1.33 (0.99-1.78), against 9.27 with another diagnosis, and more than 90% of those who attempted or died had another diagnosis (Kõlves 2021). In Swedish registers (54,168 autistic people and 270,840 matched controls), risk was highest in autism without intellectual disability plus ADHD (suicide odds ratio 13.09, 95% CI 8.54-20.08; attempt odds ratio 7.25), and adjusting for psychiatric comorbidity reduced the risks (Hirvikoski 2020). In 372 English coroner inquest records, evidence of autism was found in 10.8% of suicide deaths against 1.1% expected (odds ratio 11.08, 95% CI 3.92-31.31); in a subsample of 29 next-of-kin interviews, adding possible undiagnosed autism raised this to 41.4% (odds ratio 19.76, 95% CI 2.36-165.84) (Cassidy 2022). These are group associations with wide uncertainty in the smaller study; they do not predict what will happen to any individual, and they point to co-occurring depression, anxiety and ADHD as conditions to recognise and treat. Read the evidence

You may have heard

“1 in 3 autistic children show elevated blood lactate and pyruvate with low carnitine.”

The popular version merges separate findings into one profile; only raised lactate reached roughly 1 in 3.

What the studies found

A 2012 meta-analysis found mitochondrial disease in 5.0% of autistic children, and abnormal mitochondrial biomarkers more often. Its full-text table gives separate pooled rates: elevated lactate 31.1% (6 studies, n = 479), elevated pyruvate 13.6% (2 studies, n = 110), low total carnitine 90% (1 study, n = 30). These are separate markers, not one combined profile, from small studies with referral bias (Rossignol 2012).

Link to this claim

Autism questionnaires were poor at identifying autism in adults referred for assessment, so they can prompt referral but cannot confirm or rule it out.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Many adults are missed, so keeping autism in mind makes sense. We did not find a study testing whether universal screening reduces suicide (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Every adult mental-health assessment should screen for autism with standard questionnaires (such as the AQ-10, CAT-Q and RAADS-R), and doing so would reduce suicide.”

The popular version goes further: the reason for concern is sound, but the questionnaires work less well in clinics than in validation studies, and the suicide benefit is untested.

What the studies found

The rationale is sound: suicidality is very common in autistic and possibly autistic adults (Newell 2023), and many adults, especially women, are missed (Lai 2015). But the tools are weaker in real clinical settings than in their validation studies. In 476 adults referred for assessment, the AQ had specificity 0.29, and 64% of those below cut-off were in fact autistic; anxiety inflated scores (Ashwood 2016). The AQ-10 and RAADS-R were validated mainly by comparing known cases with controls (Booth 2013; Ritvo 2010). The CAT-Q measures camouflaging and its validation reports good reliability but no diagnostic cut-off (Hull 2018). We did not find a study testing whether universal screening reduces suicide (search last updated October 2026).

Autistic adults say recovery from burnout involves rest and sensory relief as well as acceptance and doing things in an autistic way.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Recovering from neurodivergent burnout needs not rest or less stimulation, but permission to do things the neurodivergent way.”

What the studies found

Autistic adults linked recovery to acceptance and social support, time off/reduced expectations, and doing things in an autistic way/unmasking (Raymaker 2020). A systematic review of 48 studies found rest, solitude and sensory relief, self-understanding and support helped (Ali 2025). In autism, masking and difficulty reading body and emotional signals are each documented, but they have not been linked in a study.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Masking is exhausting and, over time, wears down the ability to notice one's own hunger, fatigue and distress signals.”

What the studies found

The exhaustion part is well described. Autistic adults report exhaustion as a consequence of camouflaging (Hull 2017), more camouflaging is associated with worse mental health in a review that called the conclusion preliminary (Cook 2021), and in 160 undergraduates camouflaging autistic traits was associated with lifetime suicidality (Cassidy 2020). But we did not find a study testing whether masking causes poorer body awareness. The direction could run the other way, or both could reflect autistic traits.

In one study of young adults, more masking was linked with stronger resting connectivity between brain networks, but only in those with higher autistic traits.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

This connectivity pattern was statistically tied to anxiety and low mood, but the sample was non-clinical and measured at one time point, so it cannot show that masking causes brain changes or that the activity 'doesn't switch off'.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Neuroimaging shows that masking is compensatory brain activity that doesn't switch off, even at rest.”

The popular version goes further: a single cross-sectional association is described as proof of a lasting brain process.

What the studies found

In 110 healthy young adults without a diagnosis, more camouflaging was associated with stronger default mode-cingulo-opercular resting-state connectivity only among those with higher autistic traits, and this mediated links with depression and anxiety; the design was cross-sectional (Guo 2026). In 119 adults, more camouflaging was associated with a heightened vmPFC self-representation response in autistic women only, on a task-based scan (Lai 2019). Camouflaging did not predict distress beyond social anxiety in 113 autistic adults (Roisenberg 2026).

The popular version matches the finding but adds an interpretation (brain-specific strain) the researchers did not make.

What the studies found

In 64 autistic adults vs 58 controls, 9.4% of the autistic group had elevated posterior cingulate lactate vs 0% of controls (p = 0.028), and serum lactate did not correlate with brain lactate; the authors urge caution (Maier 2023). An earlier study found brain lactate in 13% of 75 autistic participants vs 1% of controls, and in 20% of adults (Goh 2014). Both interpret this as a possible mitochondrial subgroup, not burnout or strain.

You may have heard

“Existing in a neurotypical world costs ADHD and autistic adults more energy; your cells keep the score.”

The popular version states as fact an idea that so far exists only as a proposed model.

“Mitochondria under strain (more lactate, fewer antioxidants) are central to neurodivergent burnout.”

The popular version presents a hypothesis as an established mechanism; mitochondria have not been measured in burnout.

What the studies found

We did not find a study measuring energy expenditure in neurodivergent adults, or mitochondria in autistic, ADHD or occupational burnout (search last updated October 2026). An energetic model of allostatic load proposes, as a testable model, that chronic stress adds an energetic burden that competes with repair (Bobba-Alves 2022), and a related review proposes 'mitochondrial allostatic load' as an emerging concept (Picard 2018, narrative review). A hypothesis paper extends this to autism, proposing camouflaging as chronic stress leading to mitochondrial allostatic load and burnout, and notes these mechanisms are largely unexplored (Mahony 2022). A systematic review found 23 experimental stress-mitochondria studies, all in male laboratory animals, and only 6 observational human studies (Picard & McEwen 2018, systematic review). In humans, mothers caring for an autistic child had lower leukocyte mitochondrial health than control mothers (Picard 2018).

Studies of autism mainly find difficulty learning predictive patterns, especially from faint or inconsistent cues, not faster or more confident prediction.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Predictive-processing theories suggest autistic perception may rely less on prior expectations or weigh surprises too heavily, not that neurodivergent brains predict better. We did not find an eligible review applying the framework to ADHD or giftedness (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Predictive-processing neuroscience shows that neurodivergent and gifted brains run faster, higher-resolution, more confident prediction models.”

This has been tested for autism and the evidence runs the other way; for ADHD and giftedness it is untested.

What the studies found

Predictive processing is a leading framework in neuroscience. But the autism versions of it propose something different from 'stronger predictions'. They suggest weaker priors, so that perception leans less on expectation (Pellicano 2012); or that prediction errors are given an inflexibly high weight, so sameness feels safer (Van de Cruys 2014); or that sensory evidence is over-weighted relative to prior beliefs (Lawson 2014). A review of 47 empirical studies mainly found difficulty learning predictive relationships, especially when cues were faint or inconsistent (Cannon 2021). We did not find an eligible review applying the framework to ADHD or giftedness.
