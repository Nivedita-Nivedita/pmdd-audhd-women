Topics / Giftedness & masking

Giftedness & masking

High IQ can hide ADHD. It does not protect against it.

What is solid

  • ADHD is a valid diagnosis at high IQ, with the same impairments and family patterns (Antshel 2007, 2010).
  • Normal or high test scores can mask real executive problems (Brown 2009, Milioni 2017).

What is thin or missing

  • No validated measure of masking in ADHD exists.
  • Nobody has measured how often women with ADHD are misdiagnosed with BPD.
  • Almost no data exist on gifted adult women with ADHD.

How it connects

  • Autism overlap, sensory & burnout: Compensation and camouflaging may lead to exhaustion Preliminary
  • ADHD in women: Higher IQ can mask ADHD on tests, which may delay recognition Preliminary
  • Emotion & rejection sensitivity: Rejection sensitivity and people-pleasing may drive masking Preliminary

Claims checked

Contradicted   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
A high IQ protects you from ADHD.

High-IQ (IQ 120 or more) children with ADHD (49) had more grade repetition, comorbidity and impairment than 92 high-IQ children without ADHD (Antshel 2007). High-IQ adults with ADHD (64 vs 53 high-IQ controls) had lower quality of life, poorer work and family functioning, and more accidents and depression (Antshel 2008). ADHD and IQ are negatively correlated on nearly all constructs, but ADHD occurs at every level (Rommelse 2016).

Closer to the evidence: ADHD occurs at every IQ level, and bright people with ADHD still struggle more than equally bright people without it.

Sources
  • Antshel 2007
  • Antshel 2008
  • Rommelse 2016

Contradicted   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
If you did well at school, you can't have ADHD.

A review of 41 articles on women and girls concluded that satisfactory academic achievement should not rule out ADHD (Quinn 2014). In a population birth cohort, children with ADHD and high IQ had the same comorbidity and treatment patterns as other children with ADHD, and better reading (Katusic 2011). A systematic review found limited but consistent evidence that ADHD is valid in high-IQ people and that their coping can lead to a missed diagnosis (Rommelse 2016).

Closer to the evidence: Good grades don't rule out ADHD; many bright girls cope at school at a high hidden cost.

Sources
  • Quinn 2014
  • Katusic 2011
  • Rommelse 2016

Untested   No direct evidence

Popular claim, paraphrased
Adults who were 'gifted kids' experience a distinctive kind of burnout, different from ordinary overwork burnout.

We did not find a research construct of 'gifted burnout' (search last updated October 2026). Autistic burnout has qualitative and early measurement research (Raymaker 2020; Arnold 2023), and autistic young people who compensate socially and have higher IQ report more anxiety (Livingston 2018). But population studies show high intelligence goes with less anxiety and fewer mental disorders (Williams 2022; Koenen 2009), so being gifted alone is not a known risk factor. Where the pattern fits, unrecognised autism or ADHD in a capable person, plus long-term compensation, is a better-supported explanation than giftedness itself.

Closer to the evidence: Exhaustion in capable adults who were 'gifted kids' may reflect unrecognised autism or ADHD and long-term compensation; giftedness alone is not linked to more burnout or mental illness.

Sources
  • Raymaker 2020
  • Arnold 2023
  • Livingston 2018
  • Williams 2022
  • Koenen 2009

Untested   No direct evidence

Popular claim, paraphrased
Gifted and neurodivergent children understand mortality unusually early, sometimes at age 3 or 4.

Most healthy children understand that death is permanent, ends bodily function and is universal by about 5 to 7 years of age (Speece 1984). We did not find a study comparing this by IQ or neurotype, so 'unusually early' is untested rather than disproven. Many adults do remember worrying about death when very young.

Closer to the evidence: Many adults remember worrying about death young; most children understand death by about age 5–7, and whether gifted or neurodivergent children get there earlier hasn't been studied.

Sources
  • Speece 1984

Untested   No direct evidence

Popular claim, paraphrased
Gifted and neurodivergent people experience a distinct 'existential depression' that reflects accurate perception rather than illness, and that standard treatment misses.

We did not find a peer-reviewed study of 'existential depression' as a construct in gifted or neurodivergent people (search last updated October 2026); the idea comes from gifted-education writing rather than research. Evidence on emotional problems in gifted children is mixed (Tasca 2022), and high intelligence was not associated with more mental disorder in population data (Williams 2022). The risk of the framing is that depression with treatable features goes untreated. Low mood that centres on meaning or mortality is still worth taking seriously.

Closer to the evidence: Some people's low mood centres on meaning and mortality, and that is worth exploring in therapy, but there is no evidence that gifted people have a separate kind of depression that standard treatment can't help.

Sources
  • Tasca 2022
  • Williams 2022

Untested   No direct evidence

Popular claim, paraphrased
Gifted and neurodivergent people have heightened intuition and 'energetic' or metaphysical abilities.

We did not find a study testing 'energetic' or metaphysical abilities in autistic, ADHD or gifted people, and the terms are not defined in a way research can measure. Openness to experience, which is higher in gifted samples (Vuyk 2016), is the nearest measurable trait. Intuition as people describe it is a lived experience worth respecting, but it is not evidence of a measurable ability.

Closer to the evidence: Some neurodivergent people describe strong intuition or a sense of perceiving more; this is a lived-experience description that has not been studied.

Sources
  • Vuyk 2016

Overstated   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Gifted people live with 'overexcitabilities': heightened emotional, sensory and intellectual intensity that shapes their lives.

The idea uses Dabrowski's term 'overexcitability' (OE). A meta-analysis found gifted samples score higher on overexcitability questionnaires, but differences were medium only for intellectual and imaginational OEs, small for emotional and sensual OEs and not significant for psychomotor OE, and the authors note important limitations (Winkler 2016). OEs appear to be largely the personality trait 'openness to experience' (Vuyk 2016). Surveys of high-IQ society members reporting more anxiety, autism and allergies rely on self-selected members (Karpinski 2018; Fries 2022). In UK Biobank, high intelligence went with less anxiety and PTSD but more allergies (Williams 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Gifted people tend to be more intellectually curious and imaginative (high openness); evidence that they are more emotionally or sensorily intense is weak.

Sources
  • Winkler 2016
  • Vuyk 2016
  • Karpinski 2018
  • Fries 2022
  • Williams 2022

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
A high IQ masks ADHD, so it doesn't show up on testing.

In a small study (51 adults with ADHD, 33 controls), the higher-IQ ADHD group differed from controls on only one test measure, while the standard-IQ group was impaired on many (Milioni 2016). High-IQ adults with ADHD scored worse than high-IQ controls but within the 'average' range (Antshel 2010), and 73% of 157 adults with ADHD and IQ 120 or more were impaired on at least 5 of 8 executive-function measures (Brown 2009). The evidence is small and cross-sectional, and lab tests generally agree poorly with everyday functioning (Toplak 2012).

Closer to the evidence: In bright adults, test scores can look normal even when ADHD is present, so real-life impairment matters more than test results.

Sources
  • Milioni 2016
  • Antshel 2010
  • Brown 2009
  • Toplak 2012

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Autistic children with high IQ (120 or more) are several times more likely to have suicidal thoughts, and the same probably applies to autistic adults with high ability.

The child finding comes from one study. In 1,074 young people attending a clinic for gifted and talented youth, parent-reported suicidal ideation was in excess among autistic youth with IQ of 120 or more (odds ratio 5.9); in a comparison of two large child cohorts (combined N = 16,049), autistic children had more suicidal thoughts (odds ratio 6.8), and polygenic scores for cognitive performance were associated with suicidal thoughts in 1,983 children (Casten 2023). The extension to adults has not been tested in the studies we found. In general-population studies, high intelligence went with less anxiety and PTSD (Williams 2022), and lower childhood IQ predicted more adult depression and anxiety in a New Zealand birth cohort (Koenen 2009), so the autism-specific finding is notable but needs replication. These are group associations, not predictions for an individual child. If you are struggling now, see Crisis support (../help.html); don't wait for thoughts to pass on their own.

Closer to the evidence: One study found that autistic children with high IQ were more likely than other autistic children to have had suicidal thoughts (parent report); whether this holds in adults is unknown, and high ability should never be read as a sign that someone is coping.

Sources
  • Casten 2023
  • Williams 2022
  • Koenen 2009

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Giftedness is a form of neurodivergence, alongside autism and ADHD.

Two different questions are mixed here. The first is about language: 'neurodivergence' is a broad community and identity term, not a diagnosis, and whether people include giftedness under it is a choice about terminology that research can't settle. The second is a testable clinical claim: that giftedness is a neurodevelopmental condition like autism or ADHD, with its own clinical features and needs. That claim is untested in the studies we found: giftedness has no diagnostic definition beyond ability or achievement thresholds, and its genetic links point in different directions for the two conditions. Autism polygenic risk is slightly positively associated with cognitive ability (Clarke 2015), while intelligence appears protective against ADHD (Savage 2018). High intelligence was not associated with more mental disorders in population data (Williams 2022; Gale 2010), and a systematic review of gifted children found inconsistent evidence on emotional and behavioural problems (Tasca 2022).

Closer to the evidence: Calling giftedness a neurodivergence is a reasonable identity and terminology choice for some people, but giftedness is not a clinical or neurodevelopmental diagnosis; it can co-occur with autism or ADHD and complicate their recognition.

Sources
  • Clarke 2015
  • Savage 2018
  • Williams 2022
  • Gale 2010
  • Tasca 2022

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Many children seen as 'gifted' are identified as autistic and/or ADHD only in adulthood, often after burnout, because their ability hid traits that were always there.

Parts of this pathway are supported. Autistic adolescents who compensate socially have higher IQ and executive function but more anxiety, and are not 'milder' cases (Livingston 2018). In adults diagnosed late, ability and coping seem to soften rather than remove traits (Happé 2016). Higher IQ can mask ADHD on executive tests (Milioni 2016), and gifted children were over-represented, at 8.8%, in one ADHD clinic sample (Cornoldi 2023). The genetics differ by condition: autism polygenic risk is slightly positively associated with cognitive ability (Clarke 2015), while intelligence appears protective against ADHD (Savage 2018). We did not find a study that followed gifted children into adulthood to test the full pathway.

Closer to the evidence: High ability can delay recognition of autism and ADHD by helping people compensate, sometimes until demands exceed their resources; how often this happens to gifted children has not been measured.

Sources
  • Livingston 2018
  • Happé 2016
  • Milioni 2016
  • Cornoldi 2023
  • Clarke 2015
  • Savage 2018
  • Rommelse 2016

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Women with ADHD 'mask' their symptoms, and that's why they're diagnosed so late.

An expert consensus lists compensatory strategies that mask symptoms as one barrier to recognising ADHD in women (Young 2020), and 32 adult outpatients described many self-made coping strategies before diagnosis (Canela 2017). Adults with ADHD scored higher on a camouflaging questionnaire than comparison adults but lower than autistic adults, and camouflaging tracked autistic traits rather than ADHD traits (van der Putten 2024). No validated ADHD-specific masking measure exists yet.

Closer to the evidence: Many women develop coping strategies that hide ADHD and can delay diagnosis, but 'masking' in ADHD hasn't yet been properly measured.

Sources
  • Young 2020
  • Canela 2017
  • van der Putten 2024
  • Attoe 2023

Mostly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Being identified as autistic or ADHD late in life brings relief and grief together, including an ongoing, often unrecognised grief for the years spent not knowing.

Qualitative studies of late-diagnosed women describe this mix. Diagnosis shifted self-criticism toward self-compassion but was painful so late (Leedham 2020). Women described years of pretending to be normal (Bargiela 2016). A synthesis of women diagnosed with ADHD in adulthood found self-acceptance after diagnosis alongside the costs of years undiagnosed (Attoe 2023). Describing this grief as 'ambiguous' borrows the theory of ambiguous loss (Boss 2016), which was developed for families; we did not find a study applying it to late-diagnosed adults.

Closer to the evidence: Many women diagnosed late describe relief and grief together; this is well documented in qualitative studies, though not yet measured in larger samples.

Sources
  • Leedham 2020
  • Bargiela 2016
  • Attoe 2023
  • Boss 2016

Key research

35 papers. A selection, listed by study design (reviews and trials first), then newest first. The label is the kind of study, not a quality score. See all in the library.

  • Cognitive Profile in Autism and ADHD: A Meta-Analysis of Performance on the WAIS-IV and WISC-V

    Autistic people often score much lower on speed tasks than on reasoning; people with ADHD show a flatter pattern; neither pattern can diagnose the condition.

    Wilson 2023, Archives of Clinical Neuropsychology · Meta-analysis

  • Giftedness and Overexcitability

    Gifted people scored somewhat higher on "overexcitability" questionnaires, mainly for intellectual curiosity and imagination; differences in emotional and sensory intensity were small.

    Winkler 2016, Gifted Child Quarterly · Meta-analysis

  • Miss. Diagnosis: A Systematic Review of ADHD in Adult Women

    Women who learned they had ADHD as adults described strain on emotional wellbeing, difficult relationships and feeling out of control, with self-acceptance after diagnosis emerging as a theme.

    Attoe 2023, Journal of Attention Disorders · Systematic review

  • Behavioral and Socio-Emotional Disorders in Intellectual Giftedness: A Systematic Review

    Studies disagree on whether gifted children have more emotional or behavioural problems; the evidence is too mixed to say.

    Tasca 2022, Child Psychiatry & Human Development · Systematic review

  • An evidenced-based perspective on the validity of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder in the context of high intelligence

    A review found limited but consistent evidence that ADHD is a valid diagnosis in very bright people too; their coping strategies may make it easier to miss.

    Rommelse 2016, Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews · Systematic review

  • The Identification and Performance of Gifted Students With Learning Disability Diagnoses

    Research on "gifted with a learning disability" students used very different definitions, and many of those labelled were not actually struggling academically.

    Lovett 2011, Journal of Learning Disabilities · Systematic review

  • Common polygenic risk for autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is associated with cognitive ability in the general population

    Genes linked to autism were linked, very slightly, to higher thinking-test scores in ordinary people; genes linked to ADHD were not.

    Clarke 2015, Molecular Psychiatry · Cohort study

  • Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder in Children With High Intelligence Quotient: Results From a Population-Based Study

    Children with ADHD and high IQ had much the same ADHD-related problems as other children with ADHD, though they read better.

    Katusic 2011, Journal of Developmental & Behavioral Pediatrics · Cohort study

  • Childhood IQ and Adult Mental Disorders: A Test of the Cognitive Reserve Hypothesis

    In a New Zealand birth cohort, children with lower IQ were more likely to develop depression and anxiety as adults; higher IQ was linked only to more mania.

    Koenen 2009, American Journal of Psychiatry · Cohort study

  • Intelligence in Early Adulthood and Subsequent Hospitalization for Mental Disorders

    In over a million Swedish men, those with higher test scores at conscription were less likely to be hospitalised later for any mental disorder.

    Gale 2010, Epidemiology · Registry study

  • Quantifying and exploring camouflaging in men and women with autism

    Autistic women, on average, appeared less autistic in a clinical assessment than their inner traits would predict, suggesting more masking.

    Lai 2016, Autism · Case-control study

  • Cognitive, Adaptive, and Psychosocial Differences Between High Ability Youth With and Without Autism Spectrum Disorder

    Bright autistic young people were slower on speed tasks and had more everyday and emotional difficulties than equally bright non-autistic peers.

    Doobay 2014, Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders · Case-control study
