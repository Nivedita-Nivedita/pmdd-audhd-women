Topics / Giftedness & masking

Giftedness & masking

High IQ can hide ADHD. It does not protect against it.

What is solid

  • ADHD is a valid diagnosis at high IQ, with the same impairments and family patterns (Antshel 2007, 2010).
  • Normal or high test scores can mask real executive problems (Brown 2009, Milioni 2017).

What is thin or missing

  • No validated measure of masking in ADHD exists.
  • Nobody has measured how often women with ADHD are misdiagnosed with BPD.
  • Almost no data exist on gifted adult women with ADHD.

How it connects

  • ADHD in women: ADHD occurs at every IQ level; bright people with ADHD still have real impairment Supported
  • Autism overlap, sensory & burnout: Camouflaging is common in autism, also reported in ADHD, and linked with poorer mental health Preliminary

What the research says about common claims

ADHD occurs at every IQ level, and high-IQ people with ADHD have more difficulties than equally bright people without it.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: High-IQ children and adults with and without ADHD in two US case-control studies, plus a systematic review

Higher IQ goes with fewer ADHD traits on average, but ADHD still occurs in very bright children and adults, who had more impairment, comorbidity and work and family problems than high-IQ peers without ADHD.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“A high IQ protects you from ADHD.”

The popular version goes further: a lower average rate is not protection, and ADHD is found at every IQ level.

What the studies found

High-IQ (IQ 120 or more) children with ADHD (49) had more grade repetition, comorbidity and impairment than 92 high-IQ children without ADHD (Antshel 2007). High-IQ adults with ADHD (64 vs 53 high-IQ controls) had lower quality of life, poorer work and family functioning, and more accidents and depression (Antshel 2008). ADHD and IQ are negatively correlated on nearly all constructs, but ADHD occurs at every level (Rommelse 2016).

Link to this claim

ADHD occurs in high-IQ children and looks clinically similar to ADHD in other children, so doing well at school does not rule it out.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: US birth cohort including 34 high-IQ children with ADHD, a systematic review of high-IQ ADHD and a review of 41 articles on girls and women

Good grades don't rule out ADHD: bright children with ADHD looked clinically like other children with ADHD, though their coping strategies may make it easier to miss.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“If you did well at school, you can't have ADHD.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs the other way.

What the studies found

A review of 41 articles on women and girls concluded that satisfactory academic achievement should not rule out ADHD (Quinn 2014). In a population birth cohort, children with ADHD and high IQ had the same comorbidity and treatment patterns as other children with ADHD, and better reading (Katusic 2011). A systematic review found limited but consistent evidence that ADHD is valid in high-IQ people and that their coping can lead to a missed diagnosis (Rommelse 2016).

Link to this claim

Gifted people score somewhat higher on intellectual curiosity and imagination, traits that largely overlap with openness to experience; differences in emotional and sensory intensity are small.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Meta-analysis of gifted versus non-gifted samples on overexcitability questionnaires, one structural study, and self-selected Mensa surveys

Evidence that gifted people are more emotionally or sensorily intense is weak. We did not find a study testing 'energetic' or metaphysical abilities in autistic, ADHD or gifted people (search last updated October 2026); strong intuition is a lived experience worth respecting, but it has not been studied as an ability.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Gifted people live with 'overexcitabilities': heightened emotional, sensory and intellectual intensity that shapes their lives.”

The differences found are mainly intellectual and imaginative, and small for emotional and sensory intensity.

“Gifted and neurodivergent people have heightened intuition and 'energetic' or metaphysical abilities.”

This idea has not been tested, and its terms are not defined in a way research can measure.

What the studies found

The idea uses Dabrowski's term 'overexcitability' (OE). A meta-analysis found gifted samples score higher on overexcitability questionnaires, but differences were medium only for intellectual and imaginational OEs, small for emotional and sensual OEs and not significant for psychomotor OE, and the authors note important limitations (Winkler 2016). OEs appear to be largely the personality trait 'openness to experience', the nearest measurable trait (Vuyk 2016). Surveys of high-IQ society members reporting more anxiety, autism and allergies rely on self-selected members (Karpinski 2018; Fries 2022). In UK Biobank, high intelligence went with less anxiety and PTSD but more allergies (Williams 2022). We did not find a study testing 'energetic' or metaphysical abilities in autistic, ADHD or gifted people (search last updated October 2026), and these terms are not defined in a way research can measure. Intuition as people describe it is a lived experience worth respecting, but it is not evidence of a measurable ability.

Link to this claim

In large population studies, high intelligence was linked with fewer mental disorders, not more, and giftedness has no clinical definition.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Swedish male conscript registry, UK Biobank adults and a New Zealand birth cohort; review of gifted children

Calling giftedness a neurodivergence is a terminology choice research can't settle. We did not find peer-reviewed studies of 'existential depression' or 'gifted burnout' (search last updated October 2026). Low mood or exhaustion in capable adults deserves attention and may sometimes reflect unrecognised autism or ADHD, though this is untested.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Giftedness is a form of neurodivergence, alongside autism and ADHD.”

The popular version mixes an identity choice with a clinical claim; the clinical part has not been shown.

“Gifted and neurodivergent people experience a distinct 'existential depression' that reflects accurate perception rather than illness, and that standard treatment misses.”

This idea comes from gifted-education writing and has not been tested as a distinct kind of depression.

“Adults who were 'gifted kids' experience a distinctive kind of burnout, different from ordinary overwork burnout.”

A distinct 'gifted burnout' has not been studied; the nearest evidence concerns autistic burnout and the mental health of highly intelligent people.

What the studies found

Calling giftedness a neurodivergence mixes two questions. 'Neurodivergence' is a broad community and identity term, not a diagnosis, so including giftedness is a terminology choice research can't settle. The clinical claim, that giftedness is a neurodevelopmental condition like autism or ADHD, is untested in the studies we found: giftedness has no diagnostic definition beyond ability or achievement thresholds, and its genetic links point in different directions, with autism polygenic risk slightly positively associated with cognitive ability (Clarke 2015) and Mendelian randomisation suggesting intelligence may protect against ADHD (Savage 2018). High intelligence was not associated with more mental disorders in population data: highly intelligent UK Biobank participants had less anxiety and PTSD (Williams 2022), hospital admission for every category of mental disorder rose as IQ fell in Swedish conscripts (Gale 2010), and lower childhood IQ predicted adult depression and anxiety in a New Zealand cohort, though higher IQ predicted more adult mania (Koenen 2009). A systematic review of gifted children found inconsistent evidence on emotional and behavioural problems (Tasca 2022). We did not find peer-reviewed studies of 'existential depression' or 'gifted burnout' as constructs in gifted or neurodivergent people (search last updated October 2026); the first idea comes from gifted-education writing, and its risk is that depression with treatable features goes untreated, though low mood that centres on meaning or mortality is still worth taking seriously. Autistic burnout has qualitative and early measurement research (Raymaker 2020; Arnold 2023), and autistic young people who compensate socially and have higher IQ report more anxiety (Livingston 2018). Where the pattern fits, unrecognised autism or ADHD in a capable person, plus long-term compensation, may be a more plausible explanation than giftedness itself, though this has not been tested directly.

Most children understand that death is permanent, stops the body working and happens to everyone by about age 5 to 7.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Review of empirical studies of healthy children in urban-industrial societies; no comparison by IQ or neurotype

We did not find a study comparing this by IQ or neurotype (search last updated October 2026), so whether gifted or neurodivergent children understand death earlier is untested rather than disproven.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Gifted and neurodivergent children understand mortality unusually early, sometimes at age 3 or 4.”

The popular idea has not been tested; the research describes typical ages, not differences between groups.

What the studies found

Most healthy children understand that death is permanent, ends bodily function and is universal by about 5 to 7 years of age (Speece 1984). We did not find a study comparing this by IQ or neurotype (search last updated October 2026), so 'unusually early' is untested rather than disproven.

Link to this claim

Coping strategies that hide symptoms are a recognised barrier to diagnosing ADHD in women, according to expert consensus and interview studies.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Who was studied: UK expert consensus, interviews with 32 adult outpatients, and one case-control camouflaging study in adults

Many women develop coping strategies that hide ADHD and can delay diagnosis. ADHD-specific masking has not yet been properly measured.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Women with ADHD 'mask' their symptoms, and that's why they're diagnosed so late.”

The popular version goes further: it treats masking as the main reason for late diagnosis, while it is one of several.

What the studies found

An expert consensus lists compensatory strategies that mask symptoms as one barrier to recognising ADHD in women (Young 2020), and 32 adult outpatients described many self-made coping strategies before diagnosis (Canela 2017). Adults with ADHD scored higher on a camouflaging questionnaire than comparison adults but lower than autistic adults, and camouflaging tracked autistic traits rather than ADHD traits (van der Putten 2024). We did not find a validated ADHD-specific masking measure (search last updated October 2026).

Link to this claim

High ability may help people compensate for autistic or ADHD traits, which can make them harder to recognise.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Who was studied: Cross-sectional studies of autistic adolescents, adults referred for assessment and ADHD clinic samples; genetic studies in general populations

Autistic teenagers who compensated socially had higher IQ but more anxiety. We did not find a study that followed gifted children into adulthood to test the full pathway (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Many children seen as 'gifted' are identified as autistic and/or ADHD only in adulthood, often after burnout, because their ability hid traits that were always there.”

The popular version goes further: parts of the pathway have been studied, but how often it happens to gifted children has not.

What the studies found

Parts of this pathway are supported. Autistic adolescents who compensate socially have higher IQ and executive function but more anxiety, and are not 'milder' cases (Livingston 2018). In adults diagnosed late, ability and coping seem to soften rather than remove traits (Happé 2016). Higher IQ can mask ADHD on executive tests (Milioni 2016), and gifted children were over-represented, at 8.8%, in one ADHD clinic sample (Cornoldi 2023). The genetics differ by condition: autism polygenic risk is slightly positively associated with cognitive ability (Clarke 2015), while intelligence appears protective against ADHD (Savage 2018). We did not find a study that followed gifted children into adulthood to test the full pathway.

Link to this claim

In one study at a clinic for gifted youth, autistic children with IQ of 120 or more had more parent-reported suicidal thoughts.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Who was studied: Parent reports from one US clinic for gifted youth, plus two child cohorts; no adult studies

This is one study using parent reports and needs replication. We did not find a study testing it in adults (search last updated October 2026), and high ability should never be read as a sign that someone is coping.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Autistic children with high IQ (120 or more) are several times more likely to have suicidal thoughts, and the same probably applies to autistic adults with high ability.”

The popular version goes further: it extends a single child study to adults, which has not been tested.

What the studies found

The child finding comes from one study. In 1,074 young people attending a clinic for gifted and talented youth, parent-reported suicidal ideation was in excess among autistic youth with IQ of 120 or more (odds ratio 5.9); in a comparison of two large child cohorts (combined N = 16,049), autistic children had more suicidal thoughts (odds ratio 6.8), and polygenic scores for cognitive performance were associated with suicidal thoughts in 1,983 children (Casten 2023). The extension to adults has not been tested in the studies we found. In general-population studies, high intelligence went with less anxiety and PTSD (Williams 2022), and lower childhood IQ predicted more adult depression and anxiety in a New Zealand birth cohort (Koenen 2009), so the autism-specific finding is notable but needs replication. These are group associations, not predictions for an individual child. If you are struggling now, see Crisis support; don't wait for thoughts to pass on their own.

Link to this claim

In small qualitative studies, women diagnosed late with autism or ADHD described self-acceptance after diagnosis alongside pain about the years without one.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Who was studied: Small qualitative interview studies of women diagnosed with autism or ADHD in adulthood, and a synthesis of 8 qualitative studies

Many women diagnosed late describe relief and grief together; this is documented in qualitative studies, though not yet measured in larger samples. We did not find a study applying the idea of 'ambiguous loss' to late-diagnosed adults (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Being identified as autistic or ADHD late in life brings relief and grief together, including an ongoing, often unrecognised grief for the years spent not knowing.”

The popular version matches what women describe in interviews, but the idea of an ongoing, unrecognised grief has not been studied directly.

What the studies found

Qualitative studies of late-diagnosed women describe this mix. Diagnosis shifted self-criticism toward self-compassion but was painful so late (Leedham 2020). Women described years of pretending to be normal (Bargiela 2016). A synthesis of women diagnosed with ADHD in adulthood found self-acceptance after diagnosis alongside the costs of years undiagnosed (Attoe 2023). Describing this grief as 'ambiguous' borrows the theory of ambiguous loss (Boss 2016), which was developed for families; we did not find a study applying it to late-diagnosed adults.

Link to this claim

In small studies, high-IQ adults with ADHD often scored in the average range on thinking tests despite having ADHD.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Who was studied: Small cross-sectional and case-control studies of high-IQ adults with ADHD, plus a review comparing lab tests with rating scales

In bright adults, test scores can look normal even when ADHD is present, so real-life impairment matters more than test results. The studies are small, and lab tests generally agree poorly with everyday functioning.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“A high IQ masks ADHD, so it doesn't show up on testing.”

The popular version goes further: high IQ blunts test differences in some studies, but many bright adults with ADHD still show impairment on testing.

What the studies found

In a small study (51 adults with ADHD, 33 controls), the higher-IQ ADHD group differed from controls on only one test measure, while the standard-IQ group was impaired on many (Milioni 2016). High-IQ adults with ADHD scored worse than high-IQ controls but within the 'average' range (Antshel 2010), and 73% of 157 adults with ADHD and IQ 120 or more were impaired on at least 5 of 8 executive-function measures (Brown 2009). The evidence is small and cross-sectional, and lab tests generally agree poorly with everyday functioning (Toplak 2012).

Key research

35 papers. A selection, listed by study design (reviews and trials first), then newest first. The label is the kind of study, not a quality score. See all in the library.

  • Cognitive Profile in Autism and ADHD: A Meta-Analysis of Performance on the WAIS-IV and WISC-V

    Autistic people often score much lower on speed tasks than on reasoning; people with ADHD show a flatter pattern; neither pattern can diagnose the condition.

    Wilson 2023, Archives of Clinical Neuropsychology · Meta-analysis

  • Giftedness and Overexcitability

    Gifted people scored somewhat higher on "overexcitability" questionnaires, mainly for intellectual curiosity and imagination; differences in emotional and sensory intensity were small.

    Winkler 2016, Gifted Child Quarterly · Meta-analysis

  • Miss. Diagnosis: A Systematic Review of ADHD in Adult Women

    Women who learned they had ADHD as adults described strain on emotional wellbeing, difficult relationships and feeling out of control, with self-acceptance after diagnosis emerging as a theme.

    Attoe 2023, Journal of Attention Disorders · Systematic review

  • Behavioral and Socio-Emotional Disorders in Intellectual Giftedness: A Systematic Review

    Studies disagree on whether gifted children have more emotional or behavioural problems; the evidence is too mixed to say.

    Tasca 2022, Child Psychiatry & Human Development · Systematic review

  • An evidenced-based perspective on the validity of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder in the context of high intelligence

    A review found limited but consistent evidence that ADHD is a valid diagnosis in very bright people too; their coping strategies may make it easier to miss.

    Rommelse 2016, Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews · Systematic review

  • The Identification and Performance of Gifted Students With Learning Disability Diagnoses

    Research on "gifted with a learning disability" students used very different definitions, and many of those labelled were not actually struggling academically.

    Lovett 2011, Journal of Learning Disabilities · Systematic review

  • Common polygenic risk for autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is associated with cognitive ability in the general population

    Genes linked to autism were linked, very slightly, to higher thinking-test scores in ordinary people; genes linked to ADHD were not.

    Clarke 2015, Molecular Psychiatry · Cohort study

  • Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder in Children With High Intelligence Quotient: Results From a Population-Based Study

    Children with ADHD and high IQ had much the same ADHD-related problems as other children with ADHD, though they read better.

    Katusic 2011, Journal of Developmental & Behavioral Pediatrics · Cohort study

  • Childhood IQ and Adult Mental Disorders: A Test of the Cognitive Reserve Hypothesis

    In a New Zealand birth cohort, children with lower IQ were more likely to develop depression and anxiety as adults; higher IQ was linked only to more mania.

    Koenen 2009, American Journal of Psychiatry · Cohort study

  • Intelligence in Early Adulthood and Subsequent Hospitalization for Mental Disorders

    In over a million Swedish men, those with higher test scores at conscription were less likely to be hospitalised later for any mental disorder.

    Gale 2010, Epidemiology · Registry study

  • Quantifying and exploring camouflaging in men and women with autism

    Autistic women, on average, appeared less autistic in a clinical assessment than their inner traits would predict, suggesting more masking.

    Lai 2016, Autism · Case-control study

  • Cognitive, Adaptive, and Psychosocial Differences Between High Ability Youth With and Without Autism Spectrum Disorder

    Bright autistic young people were slower on speed tasks and had more everyday and emotional difficulties than equally bright non-autistic peers.

    Doobay 2014, Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders · Case-control study
