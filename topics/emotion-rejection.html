Topics / Emotion & rejection sensitivity

Emotion & rejection sensitivity

Emotional dysregulation is a core feature of adult ADHD. 'RSD' is not a validated construct.

What is solid

  • Adults with ADHD show much more emotional dysregulation than controls (Beheshti 2020 meta-analysis).
  • Emotional dysregulation is now treated as part of the ADHD picture (Shaw 2014).

What is thin or missing

  • Rejection sensitivity has not been studied in adult women with ADHD.
  • People-pleasing constructs (sociotropy, self-silencing, fear of negative evaluation) are unstudied in ADHD.

How it connects

  • Hormones & PMDD: Cyclical mood worsening (PMDD or premenstrual exacerbation) Supported
  • Giftedness & masking: Rejection sensitivity and people-pleasing may drive masking Preliminary

Claims checked

Contradicted   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
What gets diagnosed as generalised anxiety in bright or neurodivergent people is often accurate early pattern recognition rather than excessive worry.

It is true that the criteria for generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) do not ask whether a worry turns out to be accurate. But the direct evidence runs the other way. In 29 people with GAD, 91.4% of recorded worries did not come true, and people who saw this most clearly improved most in treatment (LaFreniere 2020). Intolerance of uncertainty, the core process in worry, is shared about equally by GAD, depression and OCD (Gentes 2011) and is linked to anxiety in autistic people about as strongly as in others (Jenkinson 2020). In UK Biobank, high intelligence went with less generalised anxiety, not more (Williams 2022). We did not find a study testing whether anxious gifted, autistic or ADHD adults forecast threats more accurately.

Closer to the evidence: Some worries do pick up real problems early, but in generalised anxiety most predicted bad outcomes don't happen; finding uncertainty hard to bear, rather than unusual foresight, is the better-supported explanation.

Sources
  • LaFreniere 2020
  • Gentes 2011
  • Jenkinson 2020
  • Williams 2022

Untested   No direct evidence

Popular claim, paraphrased
People-pleasing (or 'fawning') is an ADHD trait.

No peer-reviewed study has looked at people-pleasing-type constructs (sociotropy, self-silencing, fear of negative evaluation) in ADHD. What is documented is that anxiety is common with ADHD (pooled OR 5.0; Hartman 2023), that adults with ADHD often feel criticised (162 adults; Beaton 2022), and that self-esteem is lower in most controlled studies (Pedersen 2024).

Closer to the evidence: Some people with ADHD describe people-pleasing, perhaps after years of criticism or alongside anxiety, but it hasn't been studied as an ADHD trait.

Sources
  • Hartman 2023
  • Beaton 2022
  • Pedersen 2024

Untested   No direct evidence

Popular claim, paraphrased
Rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD) is a core part of ADHD that almost everyone with ADHD has.

'Rejection sensitive dysphoria' has no peer-reviewed validation and no questionnaire of its own. Rejection sensitivity is a real, measurable trait (Downey 1996), but ADHD studies are few and mixed: 78 college men with ADHD did not report higher rejection sensitivity (Canu 2007), while among 1,235 German teenagers more ADHD symptoms went with more rejection sensitivity (Bondu 2014). No study has looked at adult women.

Closer to the evidence: Many people with ADHD describe intense pain around rejection and criticism; this fits the well-documented emotional difficulties in ADHD, but 'RSD' itself hasn't been tested as a distinct symptom.

Sources
  • Downey 1996
  • Canu 2007
  • Bondü 2014
  • Beaton 2022

Partly true   Supported

Popular claim, paraphrased
Capable, low-maintenance children are easy to emotionally neglect, and this kind of neglect (an absence of attunement rather than a single event) has lasting effects in adulthood.

The adult-outcome part is well supported. Neglect was associated with depression (odds ratio 2.11) and suicide attempts (odds ratio 1.95) in a meta-analysis of 124 studies (Norman 2012). Emotional neglect was among the forms of maltreatment most strongly linked to depression across 192 samples (Humphreys 2020). The idea that capable children are more often overlooked is a reasonable clinical observation, but we did not find a study that tested it.

Closer to the evidence: Emotional neglect, even without abuse, is linked to adult depression; whether capable children are more often overlooked is plausible but unstudied.

Sources
  • Norman 2012
  • Humphreys 2020

Partly true   Preliminary

Popular claim, paraphrased
Chronic stress disrupts dopamine and dampens 'wanting' (anticipatory pleasure) more than enjoyment itself, which is why high-functioning people can quietly lose desire.

A leading model proposes that anhedonia (loss of pleasure or interest) arises from stress disrupting brain reward systems, based largely on animal evidence that stress impairs dopamine pathways involved in incentive motivation (Pizzagalli 2014). The more specific claim that wanting is affected more than enjoying goes beyond what that review's summary states, and we did not verify it against other studies. Loss of interest is also a core symptom of depression, so it is worth getting checked rather than explained away as stress.

Closer to the evidence: Long-term stress may dampen motivation and the pull of rewards; loss of interest is also a core sign of depression and worth getting checked.

Sources
  • Pizzagalli 2014

Mostly true   Established

Popular claim, paraphrased
Big emotions and emotional dysregulation are part of ADHD.

Across 13 studies (N=2,535), adults with ADHD had much higher emotion dysregulation than controls (g=1.17), strongest for emotional lability (Beheshti 2020); in 77 youth studies the effect for emotional reactivity was d=.95 (Graziano 2016). Emotional symptoms are common and impairing, but there is no consensus on how to define them and they are not an official diagnostic criterion (Faraone 2018). Medication eases emotional lability about half as much as core symptoms (SMD -0.41 vs -0.8; Moukhtarian 2017).

Closer to the evidence: Emotional dysregulation is common and impairing in ADHD, though it isn't an official criterion and medication usually helps it less than it helps attention.

Sources
  • Beheshti 2020
  • Graziano 2016
  • Faraone 2018
  • Moukhtarian 2017
  • Lenzi 2018

Key research

32 papers. A selection, listed by study design (reviews and trials first), then newest first. The label is the kind of study, not a quality score. See all in the library.

  • Emotion dysregulation in adults with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: a meta-analysis

    Pooling 13 studies, adults with ADHD had much more trouble managing emotions than other adults, especially quick mood changes.

    Beheshti 2020, BMC Psychiatry · Meta-analysis

  • Pharmacotherapy of emotional dysregulation in adults with ADHD: A systematic review and meta-analysis

    ADHD medicines had small-to-moderate effects on adults' emotional regulation, and may be less effective for emotions than for core ADHD symptoms.

    Lenzi 2018, Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews · Meta-analysis

  • Effects of stimulants and atomoxetine on emotional lability in adults: A systematic review and meta-analysis

    ADHD medicines eased mood swings in adults, but only about half as much as they eased core ADHD symptoms.

    Moukhtarian 2017, European Psychiatry · Meta-analysis

  • Attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder and children's emotion dysregulation: A meta-analysis

    Across 77 studies, children with ADHD had much bigger and harder-to-manage emotional reactions than other children.

    Graziano 2016, Clinical Psychology Review · Meta-analysis

  • A meta-analysis of the relation of intolerance of uncertainty to symptoms of generalized anxiety disorder, major depressive disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder

    People who find uncertainty hard to bear tend to have more symptoms of generalised anxiety, depression and OCD. The link was similar for all three, a little stronger for generalised anxiety.

    Gentes 2011, Clinical Psychology Review · Meta-analysis

  • Self-Esteem in Adults With ADHD Using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale: A Systematic Review

    Most studies found adults with ADHD have lower self-esteem, and this was similar for women and men.

    Pedersen 2024, Journal of Attention Disorders · Systematic review

  • The lived experiences of adults with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder: A rapid review of qualitative evidence

    Adults with ADHD describe experiences that overlap with other conditions, which can lead to being misdiagnosed or diagnosed late.

    Ginapp 2022, Frontiers in Psychiatry · Systematic review

  • The self-esteem of adults diagnosed with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD): a systematic review of the literature

    Adults with ADHD tend to have lower self-esteem, and therapy may help improve it to some extent, though the evidence is limited.

    Cook 2014, ADHD Attention Deficit and Hyperactivity Disorders · Systematic review

  • Everyday emotional experience of adults with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: evidence for reactive and endogenous emotional lability

    When men with ADHD tracked their moods several times a day, they felt more irritation and frustration, sometimes triggered by events and sometimes not.

    Skirrow 2014, Psychological Medicine · Case-control study

  • Emotional lability, comorbidity and impairment in adults with attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder

    In a small study of men, quick mood changes seemed to come from ADHD itself rather than other conditions and made daily life harder.

    Skirrow 2013, Journal of Affective Disorders · Case-control study

  • The Kind Nature Behind the Unsocial Semblance: ADHD and Justice Sensitivity-A Pilot Study

    In a pilot study, adults with ADHD, especially the inattentive type, were more bothered by unfairness than other adults.

    Schäfer 2012, Journal of Attention Disorders · Case-control study

  • The relationship between death anxiety and severity of mental illnesses

    In 200 people in treatment, those with more fear of death had more severe symptoms across many disorders. The study cannot show which causes which.

    Menzies 2019, British Journal of Clinical Psychology · Cross-sectional study
