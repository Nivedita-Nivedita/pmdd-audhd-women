Topics / Emotion & rejection sensitivity

Emotion & rejection sensitivity

Emotional dysregulation is a core feature of adult ADHD. 'RSD' is not a validated construct.

What is solid

  • Adults with ADHD show much more emotional dysregulation than controls (Beheshti 2020 meta-analysis).
  • Emotional dysregulation is now treated as part of the ADHD picture (Shaw 2014).

What is thin or missing

  • Rejection sensitivity has not been studied in adult women with ADHD.
  • People-pleasing constructs (sociotropy, self-silencing, fear of negative evaluation) are unstudied in ADHD.

How it connects

  • Hormones & PMDD: Cyclical mood worsening (PMDD or premenstrual exacerbation) Supported
  • Giftedness & masking: Rejection sensitivity and people-pleasing may drive masking Preliminary

Claims checked

Unsupported   Unsupported

“People-pleasing (or 'fawning') is an ADHD trait.”

No peer-reviewed study has looked at people-pleasing-type constructs (sociotropy, self-silencing, fear of negative evaluation) in ADHD. What is documented is that anxiety is common with ADHD (pooled OR 5.0; Hartman 2023), that adults with ADHD often feel criticised (162 adults; Beaton 2022), and that self-esteem is lower in most controlled studies (Pedersen 2024).

Closer to the evidence: Some people with ADHD describe people-pleasing, perhaps after years of criticism or alongside anxiety, but it hasn't been studied as an ADHD trait.

Sources
  • Hartman 2023
  • Beaton 2022
  • Pedersen 2024

Unsupported   Unsupported

“Rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD) is a core part of ADHD that almost everyone with ADHD has.”

'Rejection sensitive dysphoria' has no peer-reviewed validation and no questionnaire of its own. Rejection sensitivity is a real, measurable trait (Downey 1996), but ADHD studies are few and mixed: 78 college men with ADHD did not report higher rejection sensitivity (Canu 2007), while among 1,235 German teenagers more ADHD symptoms went with more rejection sensitivity (Bondu 2014). No study has looked at adult women.

Closer to the evidence: Many people with ADHD describe intense pain around rejection and criticism; this fits the well-documented emotional difficulties in ADHD, but 'RSD' itself hasn't been tested as a distinct symptom.

Sources
  • Downey 1996
  • Canu 2007
  • Bondü 2014
  • Beaton 2022

Unsupported   Preliminary

“What gets diagnosed as generalised anxiety in bright or neurodivergent people is often accurate early pattern recognition, not excessive worry.”

It is true that the GAD criteria do not ask whether a worry turns out to be accurate, and the idea is usually offered with fair caveats about false alarms. But the direct evidence runs the other way. In 29 people with GAD, 91.4% of recorded worries did not come true, and people who saw this most clearly improved most in treatment (LaFreniere 2020). Intolerance of uncertainty, the core process in worry, is shared about equally by GAD, depression and OCD (Gentes 2011) and is linked to anxiety in autistic people about as strongly as in others (Jenkinson 2020). In UK Biobank, high intelligence went with less generalised anxiety, not more (Williams 2022). No study has tested whether anxious gifted, autistic or ADHD adults forecast threats more accurately.

Closer to the evidence: Some worries do pick up real problems early, but in generalised anxiety most predicted bad outcomes don't happen; finding uncertainty hard to bear, rather than unusual foresight, is the better-supported explanation.

Sources
  • LaFreniere 2020
  • Gentes 2011
  • Jenkinson 2020
  • Williams 2022

Partly true   Supported

“Capable, low-maintenance children are easy to emotionally neglect, and this kind of neglect, an absence of attunement rather than an event, has lasting adult effects.”

The adult-outcome part is well supported. Neglect was associated with depression (OR 2.11) and suicide attempts (OR 1.95) in a meta-analysis of 124 studies (Norman 2012). Emotional neglect was among the forms of maltreatment most strongly linked to depression across 192 samples (Humphreys 2020). The 'capable children are more often missed' part is a reasonable clinical observation but has not been studied. The term 'childhood emotional neglect' as a popular self-help concept comes from Jonice Webb's books.

Closer to the evidence: Emotional neglect, even without abuse, is linked to adult depression; whether capable children are more often overlooked is plausible but unstudied.

Sources
  • Norman 2012
  • Humphreys 2020

Partly true   Preliminary

“Chronic stress dysregulates dopamine and blunts 'wanting' (anticipatory pleasure) more than enjoyment itself, which is why high-functioning people quietly lose desire.”

A leading model proposes that anhedonia arises from stress disrupting brain reward systems, based largely on animal evidence that stress impairs dopamine pathways for incentive motivation (Pizzagalli 2014). The specific claim that wanting is affected more than enjoying is plausible but was not checked against abstracts in this library. The writing usually pairs this with advice to see a doctor, which is appropriate because anhedonia is a core symptom of depression.

Closer to the evidence: Long-term stress may dampen motivation and the pull of rewards; loss of interest is also a core sign of depression and worth getting checked.

Sources
  • Pizzagalli 2014

Mostly true   Established

“Big emotions and emotional dysregulation are part of ADHD.”

Across 13 studies (N=2,535), adults with ADHD had much higher emotion dysregulation than controls (g=1.17), strongest for emotional lability (Beheshti 2020); in 77 youth studies the effect for emotional reactivity was d=.95 (Graziano 2016). Emotional symptoms are common and impairing, but there is no consensus on how to define them and they are not an official diagnostic criterion (Faraone 2018). Medication eases emotional lability about half as much as core symptoms (SMD -0.41 vs -0.8; Moukhtarian 2017).

Closer to the evidence: Emotional dysregulation is common and impairing in ADHD, though it isn't an official criterion and medication usually helps it less than it helps attention.

Sources
  • Beheshti 2020
  • Graziano 2016
  • Faraone 2018
  • Moukhtarian 2017
  • Lenzi 2018

Key research

32 papers, strongest evidence first. See all in the library.

  • Practitioner Review: Emotional dysregulation in attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder – implications for clinical recognition and intervention

    Experts say emotional difficulties are a common and lasting part of ADHD, and some argue they should count as a core symptom.

    Faraone 2018, Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry · Established

  • Attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder and children's emotion dysregulation: A meta-analysis

    Across 77 studies, children with ADHD had much bigger and harder-to-manage emotional reactions than other children.

    Graziano 2016, Clinical Psychology Review · Established

  • Emotion Dysregulation in Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder

    Strong emotional reactions are common in ADHD at every age and cause real problems, and ADHD treatment often helps with them.

    Shaw 2014, American Journal of Psychiatry · Established

  • Pharmacotherapy of emotional dysregulation in adults with ADHD: A systematic review and meta-analysis

    ADHD medicines had small-to-moderate effects on adults' emotional regulation, and may be less effective for emotions than for core ADHD symptoms.

    Lenzi 2018, Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews · Supported

  • Effects of stimulants and atomoxetine on emotional lability in adults: A systematic review and meta-analysis

    ADHD medicines eased mood swings in adults, but only about half as much as they eased core ADHD symptoms.

    Moukhtarian 2017, European Psychiatry · Supported

  • ADHD and Emotion Dysregulation Among Children and Adolescents

    Early evidence links emotional difficulties in young people with ADHD to social problems and risk-taking, but many basic questions are still open.

    Bunford 2015, Clinical Child and Family Psychology Review · Supported

  • Death anxiety and its role in psychopathology: Reviewing the status of a transdiagnostic construct

    Fear of death seems to sit underneath many anxiety problems, including health anxiety and panic, and may be worth treating directly.

    Iverach 2014, Clinical Psychology Review · Supported

  • The Role of Emotion Regulation in Autism Spectrum Disorder

    Big, hard-to-control emotions are common in autism and may be part of autism itself rather than a separate problem.

    Mazefsky 2013, Journal of the American Academy of Child & Adolescent Psychiatry · Supported

  • A meta-analysis of the relation of intolerance of uncertainty to symptoms of generalized anxiety disorder, major depressive disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder

    People who find uncertainty hard to bear tend to have more symptoms of generalised anxiety, depression and OCD. The link was similar for all three, a little stronger for generalised anxiety.

    Gentes 2011, Clinical Psychology Review · Supported

  • Implications of rejection sensitivity for intimate relationships.

    This classic study (not about ADHD) showed that people who expect rejection tend to see it in ambiguous situations, which can strain relationships.

    Downey 1996, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology · Supported

  • Self-Esteem in Adults With ADHD Using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale: A Systematic Review

    Most studies found adults with ADHD have lower self-esteem, and this was similar for women and men.

    Pedersen 2024, Journal of Attention Disorders · Supported

  • Adult ADHD and romantic relationships: What we know and what we can do to help

    Research suggests adults with ADHD often have shorter and more troubled romantic relationships, and this review looks at why and what might help.

    Wymbs 2021, Journal of Marital and Family Therapy · Supported
