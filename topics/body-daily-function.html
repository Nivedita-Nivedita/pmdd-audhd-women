Topics / Body & daily function

Body & daily function

Daily-life impairment, eating, sensory load and repetitive behaviours are part of the picture.

What is solid

  • Eating disorders, especially binge eating, are much more common in ADHD (Nazar 2016).
  • Sensory over- and under-sensitivity is a feature of adult ADHD and is more often reported by women (Bijlenga 2017).
  • Habit reversal training is the first-line treatment for body-focused repetitive behaviours such as nail biting.
  • POTS mostly affects women (94% in a survey of 4,835), and nearly all report difficulty concentrating (Shaw 2019).

What is thin or missing

  • No study reports how often ADHD and POTS occur together.
  • Interoception in ADHD rests on small studies.
  • N-acetylcysteine for nail biting rests on one small pilot.
  • 'Time blindness' is shown in lab tasks; the link to everyday lateness is inferred.

What the research says about common claims

Most people diagnosed with POTS are young women, often with symptoms starting in adolescence.

Established evidence Several good studies or reviews agree.

Consensus statements put the share of patients who are female at more than 75%, and surveys and trials are mostly women, though men also get POTS. These figures describe people diagnosed by clinicians.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“POTS is mostly a women's condition.”

The popular version matches the evidence well, though men get POTS too and the reason for the sex difference is not known.

What the studies found

More than 75% of patients are female, and most present between 15 and 25 years (Sheldon 2015); a typical patient is a woman of child-bearing age whose symptoms often start in adolescence (Vernino 2021). In 4835 people with diagnosed POTS from an online survey, 94% were female (Shaw 2019), though this sample was self-selected. Trial samples are similarly mostly women (Taub 2021: 95.5%). These figures describe people diagnosed by clinicians. Current consensus criteria require all of: a sustained heart-rate rise of at least 30 bpm (at least 40 bpm at ages 12-19) within 10 minutes of standing or head-up tilt; very frequent orthostatic symptoms, worse upright and easing on lying down, for at least 3 months; no significant orthostatic hypotension (a fall of at least 20/10 mm Hg); and exclusion of other causes of a fast heart rate, such as anaemia, dehydration, hyperthyroidism, prolonged bed rest or heart-rate-raising medicines including stimulants (Raj 2022). A wearable heart-rate reading alone cannot diagnose POTS.

Link to this claim

Children and adolescents with ADHD show higher levels of some oxidative stress markers and lower overall antioxidant capacity.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

A meta-analysis of 20 studies in young people found some, not all, oxidative stress markers raised and total antioxidant status lower. Evidence in adults is limited to one study of 60 adults.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Adults with ADHD show higher markers of oxidative cell damage and lower antioxidant defences.”

The popular version applies to adults a pattern shown mainly in children, and across all markers when only some differ.

What the studies found

The first meta-analysis (6 studies) found an oxidative stress association that lost significance after correction, and normal antioxidant status (Joseph 2015). A 2026 meta-analysis of 20 studies in children and adolescents found higher blood total oxidant status (SMD 0.94) and urinary 8-OHdG (0.21) and lower total antioxidant status (-0.53), but no difference in blood 8-OHdG, MDA, NO or SOD (Suwindi 2026). Adult data are a single study of 60 adults showing lower antioxidant-regulating proteins (Gürbüzer 2026).

Link to this claim

Extra fluids and salt are a weakly recommended part of POTS management, not a cure.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Consensus rates fluids and salt as a weak recommendation and structured exercise higher, and the overall treatment evidence is very limited. Most teenagers improve over years, but few report complete resolution, and extra salt is not suitable for everyone.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Salt and water cure POTS.”

The popular version goes further: these measures may help some people but have not been shown to cure POTS.

What the studies found

People with POTS can have low blood volume without the expected hormone response (Raj 2005), which is the rationale for fluids and salt. But consensus rates 2-3 L water and 10-12 g salt daily as a weak (Class IIb) recommendation, while a structured exercise programme is rated higher (Class IIa) (Sheldon 2015). Across 28 mostly small studies, volume, vascular, heart-rate and exercise treatments each helped 51%-72% of patients, and the evidence was called 'extremely limited' (Wells 2018). In a small trial, exercise training improved quality of life where propranolol did not (Fu 2011). Most adolescents improve over years, but only 19% reported complete resolution (Bhatia 2016). Extra salt and fluid are not suitable for everyone, so they should be agreed with a clinician rather than followed as universal advice. Current consensus criteria require all of: a sustained heart-rate rise of at least 30 bpm (at least 40 bpm at ages 12-19) within 10 minutes of standing or head-up tilt; very frequent orthostatic symptoms, worse upright and easing on lying down, for at least 3 months; no significant orthostatic hypotension (a fall of at least 20/10 mm Hg); and exclusion of other causes of a fast heart rate, such as anaemia, dehydration, hyperthyroidism, prolonged bed rest or heart-rate-raising medicines including stimulants (Raj 2022). A wearable heart-rate reading alone cannot diagnose POTS.

Link to this claim

Hoarding problems are more common in adults with ADHD, especially linked to inattention.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

In small clinic studies, about 20–32% of adults with ADHD had significant hoarding versus 2–4% of controls, and a population survey linked childhood inattention to hoarding. 'Doom piles' themselves have not been studied, and hoarding is more than ordinary clutter.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Doom piles (clutter you can't deal with) are an ADHD thing.”

The popular term has not been studied; the nearest research is on hoarding, which is related but more severe.

What the studies found

'Doom piles' are not studied as such, but hoarding is. About 20% of adults in an ADHD clinic had clinically significant hoarding vs 2% of matched controls, linked specifically to inattention (Morein-Zamir 2022); hoarding disorder affected 32.1% of adults with ADHD vs 8% with OCD and 4% of controls (Grassi 2023). In a European population survey, childhood inattention was associated with lifetime hoarding symptoms (OR 6.04) (Fullana 2013), and people who hoard report more ADHD symptoms (Hartl 2005). Samples are small, and hoarding is more than ordinary clutter.

Link to this claim

No study has shown that POTS, hypermobility, mast cell disorders and ADHD form one syndrome, though some of them co-occur more than expected.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Reviews found no shared mechanism and no study properly linking mast cell disorders to POTS or EDS, though hypermobility is common in POTS. We did not find a study of ADHD with all three (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“ADHD, POTS, hypermobile EDS and MCAS are really one syndrome.”

The popular version goes further: it merges partly overlapping conditions into one, and the mast-cell link has not been shown.

What the studies found

Some overlaps are real but partial. About 31% of 91 people with POTS met hEDS criteria (Miller 2020), and neurodivergent adults were more often hypermobile, which was linked to orthostatic symptoms (Csecs 2022). In autistic people referred to autonomic clinics, most had an autonomic diagnosis and most of those had hEDS (Owens 2021). But a review found no original study combining hEDS, POTS and MCAS and no shared mechanism (Kucharik 2019), and a 2025 systematic review found no study meeting strict diagnostic criteria for mast cell disorders in POTS or EDS (Farley 2025). Mast-cell markers were abnormal in 29 of 69 POTS patients in one clinic, but single tests are not diagnostic (Kohno 2021). We did not find a study of ADHD together with all three (search last updated October 2026). Consensus-defined MCAS requires recurrent multi-system symptoms, a lab-measured rise in tryptase above the person's baseline, and response to mast-cell treatment (Valent 2020); that is different from unproven claims linking MCAS to ADHD.

Link to this claim

POTS is a physiological condition with measurable heart-rate changes on standing, not a form of anxiety.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

In small physiology studies the racing heart in POTS followed blood pooling, not anxiety, and anxiety disorders were not more common. People with POTS may worry about body sensations, and consensus states anxiety does not cause the tachycardia.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“POTS is just anxiety.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs the other way.

What the studies found

In a small physiology study, heart rate rose 39 bpm in POTS vs 19 bpm in controls during venous pooling, sham pooling caused no rise, and heart-rate responses to mental stress were the same; anxiety scores were higher in POTS but were unrelated to the heart-rate response (Masuki 2007). In 21 patients with POTS, lifetime anxiety disorders, including panic disorder, were not more common than in the general population (Raj V 2009). Raised worry in POTS is focused on body sensations rather than trauma or social anxiety (Owens 2017). The Heart Rhythm Society consensus states the tachycardia is not caused by anxiety (Sheldon 2015). Panic attacks and severe anxiety are among the conditions a clinician rules out before diagnosing POTS, which is one reason the full criteria matter (Raj 2022).

Link to this claim

People with ADHD tend to judge time less accurately than others in lab tests.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Across 55 studies, people with ADHD, mostly young people, had more difficulty judging short durations. This may help explain lost time and lateness, but we did not find a study rigorously linking it to everyday lateness (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“'Time blindness' is an ADHD symptom that explains why you're always late.”

The popular version goes further: timing differences are well shown in the lab, but their link to everyday lateness has not been tested.

What the studies found

Across 55 studies, people with ADHD had more trouble discriminating very short durations, more variable estimates of multi-second intervals, and errors consistent with a faster internal clock (Marx 2022). Timing deficits appear independent of other executive functions and are reduced by methylphenidate (Noreika 2013). But these are lab tasks, mostly in young people; we did not find a study rigorously examining the link to everyday lateness (search last updated October 2026).

Link to this claim

Sensory sensitivity is common in adults with ADHD as well as in autism, so on its own it does not indicate autism.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Sensory sensitivity is common in ADHD too, especially in women, and in one study it tracked ADHD severity rather than autistic traits. Sensory over-responsivity is a core feature of autism but is not diagnostic by itself.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“If you have sensory sensitivities, you must be autistic.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs the other way: sensitivity is not unique to autism.

What the studies found

116 adults with ADHD had more sensory hyper- and hyposensitivity than norms, linked to ADHD severity but not to autistic symptoms, and 43% of women vs 22% of men reported atypical sensitivity (Bijlenga 2017). In an online survey, 69% of people with ADHD symptoms reported light sensitivity vs 28% without (Kooij 2014). Sensory over-responsivity is more specific to autism than to other conditions (Ben-Sasson 2019) but is not diagnostic alone.

Link to this claim

ADHD medicines raise heart rate by a few beats per minute on average; their effects in people with POTS are largely unstudied.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Neither 'dangerous' nor 'safe' is established. In POTS, one dose of atomoxetine worsened heart rate and symptoms, while small studies of modafinil and methylphenidate did not show this; we did not find trials in people with both ADHD and POTS (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Stimulants are dangerous if you have POTS.”

The popular version goes further: the evidence in POTS is small and mixed, and points more clearly at atomoxetine than at stimulants.

What the studies found

Average cardiovascular effects in general ADHD trials cannot establish safety for a person with POTS. In short-term trials mostly without POTS, ADHD medicines raised pulse in adults by 4.37 bpm (methylphenidate) to 5.8 bpm (viloxazine), and stimulants did not raise it more than atomoxetine or viloxazine (very low certainty; Farhat 2025; Idrees 2023). Across 19 observational studies there was no statistically clear association with cardiovascular disease in the cited synthesis, but a modest rise in cardiac arrest or tachyarrhythmia risk could not be ruled out (RR 1.60; 95% CI 0.94-2.72), and female patients need further study (Zhang 2022). In POTS specifically, a single dose of atomoxetine raised standing heart rate (121 vs 105 bpm) and worsened symptoms (Green 2013); single-dose modafinil showed no statistically clear change in standing heart rate (Kpaeyeh 2014); and in an uncontrolled series 14 of 18 patients with refractory POTS reported improvement on methylphenidate (Kanjwal 2012). The Heart Rhythm Society advises against drugs that block the norepinephrine transporter in POTS; that class recommendation does not list individual products, and its absence of named stimulants is not evidence of safety (Sheldon 2015). Stimulants are also among the heart-rate-raising medicines that must be considered when POTS is being diagnosed (Raj 2022). We did not find trials of stimulants in people with both ADHD and POTS (search last updated October 2026).

Link to this claim

Concentration problems are very common in POTS and worsen with standing, but they differ from ADHD, which begins in childhood.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Almost everyone with POTS in surveys reports brain fog, and attention is worse while standing. ADHD-like scores in POTS were not present in childhood; we did not find a study of how often the two co-occur (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“POTS brain fog is really undiagnosed ADHD (or the same thing).”

This has been tested in a small study and the evidence runs the other way: the attention problems in POTS were not lifelong.

What the studies found

Concentration problems are near-universal in POTS: 94% of 4835 survey respondents (Shaw 2019) and 132 of 138 in a brain-fog survey, worsened by standing, fatigue and dehydration (Ross 2013). Attention and executive function were worse while standing in POTS than in controls (Miller 2020). People with POTS scored higher than controls on ADHD inattention scales, but these symptoms were not present in childhood, which ADHD requires (Raj V 2009). We did not find a study reporting how often ADHD and POTS co-occur (search last updated October 2026). Current consensus criteria require all of: a sustained heart-rate rise of at least 30 bpm (at least 40 bpm at ages 12-19) within 10 minutes of standing or head-up tilt; very frequent orthostatic symptoms, worse upright and easing on lying down, for at least 3 months; no significant orthostatic hypotension (a fall of at least 20/10 mm Hg); and exclusion of other causes of a fast heart rate, such as anaemia, dehydration, hyperthyroidism, prolonged bed rest or heart-rate-raising medicines including stimulants (Raj 2022). A wearable heart-rate reading alone cannot diagnose POTS.

Link to this claim

In small studies, adults with ADHD were on average somewhat less accurate at sensing body signals such as their heartbeat, though results are mixed.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Most small case-control studies, mainly using heartbeat tasks, found lower interoception in ADHD, but one found none. We did not find studies measuring hunger, thirst or toilet signals in ADHD, or linking interoception to noticing burnout (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“People with ADHD have poor interoception and can't feel hunger, thirst or the need to pee.”

The studies measured heartbeat sensing and self-rated body awareness, not hunger, thirst or bladder signals, and found modest, mixed differences.

“Adults with ADHD show reduced interoception, so they miss the signs of burnout.”

The popular version adds a consequence, missing the signs of burnout, that has not been studied.

What the studies found

The evidence measures heartbeat-task accuracy and questionnaire-rated body awareness, not hunger, thirst or bladder signals. These are distinct dimensions of interoception that can come apart (Garfinkel 2015), and a research consensus describes how interoception is assessed and its role in conditions such as anxiety, mood and eating disorders (Khalsa 2018). A 2025 systematic review (18 studies) found 3 of 5 case-control studies showed reduced interoception in ADHD, and lower interoception went with more ADHD symptoms in general-population studies; studies were of moderate quality with small samples (Bruton 2025). In 30 adults with ADHD vs 57 controls, heartbeat-counting accuracy, confidence and questionnaire-rated accuracy were lower even after controlling for autistic traits, alexithymia and depression (Göz Tebrizcik 2025). Earlier small studies disagreed: 14 adults with ADHD counted heartbeats less accurately than 16 controls (Kutscheidt 2019), while 24 adults with ADHD performed like 23 controls on both a heartbeat task and a questionnaire (Wiersema 2018). We did not find a study that measured hunger, thirst or toilet signals in ADHD, that reported results for women separately, or that linked interoception to recognising burnout (search last updated October 2026).

Link to this claim

N-acetylcysteine (NAC) reduced hair pulling and skin picking in single 12-week adult trials, and helped nail biting only briefly in one trial in young people.

Preliminary evidence Early, small or indirect studies. Plausible, not settled.

Each finding rests on one small trial, mostly in women and none in people with ADHD. Behaviour therapy, especially habit reversal, has larger and better-replicated effects across these repetitive behaviours. Any medicine or supplement is a decision to make with a clinician.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“NAC (N-acetylcysteine) helps hair pulling (trichotillomania).”

This matches the evidence, which comes from a single trial.

“NAC (N-acetylcysteine) helps skin picking.”

This matches the evidence, which comes from a single trial in which everyday functioning did not differ.

“NAC (N-acetylcysteine) stops nail biting.”

The only trial found a short-lived effect in children and adolescents, not that NAC stops nail biting.

What the studies found

In one 12-week placebo-controlled trial in 50 adults (45 women) with trichotillomania, 56% were much or very much improved on NAC vs 16% on placebo, with improvement first seen after 9 weeks and no adverse events reported in the NAC group (Grant 2009). Behaviour therapy had a large pooled effect for hair pulling (ES 1.41) and SRIs a moderate one (ES 0.41) across separate trials (McGuire 2014). In a 12-week double-blind trial in 66 adults with excoriation (skin-picking) disorder (59 women), NAC was associated with significant improvement in skin-picking severity vs placebo; among 53 completers, 15 of 32 (47%) on NAC vs 4 of 21 (19%) on placebo were much or very much improved, with no significant difference in psychosocial functioning (Grant 2016). A clinical review recommends behaviour therapy (habit reversal or acceptance-enhanced behaviour therapy) and lists NAC among medication options (Grant 2012). The only nail-biting trial we found was in 42 children and adolescents: nails were longer on NAC than placebo after 1 month (5.21 vs 1.18 mm) but there was no clear difference at 2 months, and two NAC participants stopped because of adverse events (Ghanizadeh 2013). We did not find a trial in adults or in people with ADHD (search last updated October 2026). In a meta-analysis of 18 studies, habit reversal therapy had a large effect across repetitive behaviours including nail biting (d=0.80; Bate 2011).

  • Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS): State of the science and clinical care from a 2019 National Institutes of Health Expert Consensus Meeting - Part 1

    US experts agree POTS mostly affects young women, often starts in the teens, sometimes after an infection, and has no approved drug; lifestyle and non-drug measures come first.

    Vernino 2021, Autonomic Neuroscience · Consensus statement

  • Canadian Cardiovascular Society Position Statement on Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (POTS) and Related Disorders of Chronic Orthostatic Intolerance

    Canadian heart specialists say POTS is a pattern with several possible causes, not one disease, and that the label is sometimes given to people who don't actually meet the criteria.

    Raj 2020, Canadian Journal of Cardiology · Consensus statement

  • Interoception and Mental Health: A Roadmap

    Experts agreed that how we sense signals from inside the body matters for many mental health conditions, and set out how to research it better.

    Khalsa 2018, Biological Psychiatry: Cognitive Neuroscience and Neuroimaging · Consensus statement

  • 2015 Heart Rhythm Society Expert Consensus Statement on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Postural Tachycardia Syndrome, Inappropriate Sinus Tachycardia, and Vasovagal Syncope

    The main international cardiology statement says POTS means a big heart-rate jump on standing plus symptoms, mostly affects young women, and should be treated first with exercise, fluids, salt and compression; drug evidence is weak.

    Sheldon 2015, Heart Rhythm · Consensus statement

  • Systemic oxidative stress in children and adolescents with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: a meta-analysis of oxidative and antioxidant biomarkers

    Children with ADHD showed somewhat higher markers of oxidative stress and lower overall antioxidant capacity on some, not all, blood and urine tests.

    Suwindi 2026, Brain, Behavior, and Immunity · Meta-analysis

  • Comparative cardiovascular safety of medications for attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder in children, adolescents, and adults: a systematic review and network meta-analysis

    All main ADHD medicines except guanfacine raise heart rate a few beats per minute on average; stimulants are not worse than non-stimulants on this.

    Farhat 2025, The Lancet Psychiatry · Meta-analysis

  • Interoception in individuals with autism spectrum disorder: a systematic literature review and meta-analysis

    Autistic adults did not differ significantly from other adults in sensing their heartbeat; findings on other kinds of body awareness are mixed.

  • The Co-Occurrence of Autism and Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID): A Prevalence-Based Meta-Analysis

    Sader 2025, International Journal of Eating Disorders · Meta-analysis

  • The effects of stimulant and non-stimulant medications on the autonomic nervous system (ANS) functioning in people with ADHD: A systematic review and meta-analysis

    ADHD medicines, especially stimulants, raise heart rate and blood pressure.

    Idrees 2023, Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews · Meta-analysis

  • Meta-analysis: Altered Perceptual Timing Abilities in Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder
