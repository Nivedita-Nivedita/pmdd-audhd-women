PMDD

What premenstrual dysphoric disorder is, how it is diagnosed, how it interacts with ADHD and autism, and what helps.

What is PMDD?

Premenstrual dysphoric disorder is a severe, cyclical mood condition. Symptoms such as low mood, irritability, anxiety and feeling out of control build in the days or weeks before a period and ease soon after it starts. It became a full diagnosis in the DSM-5 (Epperson 2012).

It is not a hormone imbalance. Hormone levels are typically normal; the brain reacts differently to normal changes (Schmidt 1998). A 2024 meta-analysis estimated PMDD in about 3.2% of women when the diagnosis was confirmed with daily tracking, about 1.6% in community samples, and about 7.7% when based on one-off questionnaires, with large differences between studies (Reilly 2024). Daily symptom ratings over at least two symptomatic cycles establish the cyclical pattern and support confirming the diagnosis (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017); a clinician can make a provisional assessment and offer care before two cycles are complete.

    Women with ADHD screen positive for PMDD more often in several studies; how much more varies with the measure and sample, and we did not find a study confirming it with prospective daily ratings.

  • Tracking your cycle with the DRSP

    How to rate symptoms daily with the DRSP for at least two cycles: the standard way to confirm a PMDD pattern. A clinician can assess you and offer care while you track.

Treatment

  • PMDD treatments: what the evidence supports

    Treatments grouped into evidence tiers, anchored to the ACOG 2023 guideline and the 2024 Cochrane review. Strongest: SSRIs, with both continuous and luteal-phase dosing helping and continuous dosing favoured on average. Moderate: drospirenone pills and short-term GnRH agonists, with more trade-offs; a small 2026 network meta-analysis found SSRIs and pills both effective but cannot rank them. Limited: CBT, surgery, and calcium, B6 and vitex, which have some PMS evidence but insufficient evidence in confirmed PMDD. No eligible trial was found in women with ADHD or autism.

Living with PMDD

  • Living with PMDD: relationships and getting taken seriously

    Self-selected surveys and interviews consistently describe years of dismissal and misdiagnosis, a 'dual identity' and strain on relationships. Daily ratings, the right clinician and partner-inclusive CBT are the practical levers. The evidence is consistent but mostly qualitative.

  • After the birth: perinatal mood when you have ADHD, autism or a PMDD history

    Women with ADHD, autistic women and women with premenstrual disorders all show higher rates of perinatal depression; this guide sets out the evidence and what to put in a perinatal care plan.

  • Contraception, postpartum & perimenopause with ADHD

    Registry evidence on hormonal contraception, postpartum and perimenopause in women with ADHD, and what remains untested.

  • Midlife: perimenopause, HRT and the ADHD or autistic brain

    What is known about ADHD and autism through perimenopause, the risk window for depression (especially with a premenstrual-mood history), and what HRT trials do and do not show for mood and cognition. Extends G23.

What the research says about common claims

PMDD is confirmed by daily symptom ratings over at least two cycles, not by a blood test or a one-off questionnaire.

Established evidence Several good studies or reviews agree.

Who was studied: Validation of daily-ratings scoring against expert diagnosis in 200 women; prevalence meta-analysis; DSM-5 workgroup review; one PMS hormone study

Blood tests and one-off questionnaires can't confirm PMDD; prospective daily tracking over at least two cycles confirms the pattern, and a clinician can assess you and offer care while you track.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“A hormone blood test (or a one-off quiz) can tell you if you have PMDD.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs the other way: one-off reports overestimate PMDD and hormone levels are usually normal.

What the studies found

PMDD is confirmed with prospective daily symptom ratings over at least two cycles; the C-PASS, one standard scoring system, matched expert diagnosis with an estimated 98% correct classification in 200 women, and retrospective reports of premenstrual worsening poorly predicted the prospective diagnosis (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). Provisional (one-off) diagnosis roughly doubles prevalence (7.7% vs 3.2% confirmed; Reilly 2024). Hormone levels are typically normal in PMDD; symptoms reflect an abnormal response to normal hormone change (Schmidt 1998), so blood tests cannot diagnose it. A clinician can still assess someone provisionally and discuss care while they track, and urgent help never waits for a chart.

Link to this claim

Combined pills containing drospirenone may modestly reduce PMDD symptoms, more for overall than mood symptoms.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Cochrane review of 5 RCTs (most participants with PMDD) and RCTs of drospirenone pills; network meta-analysis in PMS or PMDD

Some combined pills, especially those containing drospirenone, may reduce PMDD symptoms modestly, more for overall than mood symptoms; they aren't safe for everyone (clot risk, migraine with aura, smoking at 35+), and evidence is of low quality.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“The pill treats PMDD.”

The popular version goes further: only some pills have evidence, the effect is modest, and they are usually second line after SSRIs.

What the studies found

Drospirenone-containing combined pills have the best evidence: the 2023 Cochrane review (5 RCTs, 858 women, most with PMDD) found they may improve overall premenstrual symptoms (SMD -0.41; low-quality evidence) and functional impairment, with effects that may be small to moderate; placebo also had a significant effect, and they may increase adverse effects (OR 2.31) and withdrawal for adverse effects (OR 3.41). No included trial reported rare serious harms such as venous thromboembolism, and it is unknown whether the pill works after three cycles or beats other pills (Ma 2023). In the larger trial the number needed to treat was 8 (Yonkers 2005; also Pearlstein 2005). Across 9 trials, combined pills improved overall premenstrual symptoms (SMD 0.41) but not depressive symptoms specifically (de Wit 2021). A continuous levonorgestrel pill gave mixed results (Halbreich 2012; Freeman 2012). Clinical guidance places the pill second line after SSRIs (Wagner-Schuman 2023). Combined pills carry a small blood-clot risk and are not suitable for everyone (for example migraine with aura, or age 35 or over and smoking), per NHS information.

Link to this claim

In large Danish and Swedish registry studies, hormonal contraception, especially in teenagers, was linked with more later antidepressant use and depression diagnoses.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Danish and Swedish national registries of women and girls aged 15-34; observational, not trials

Large registry studies link hormonal contraception, especially in teens, with a higher chance of later depression; young women with ADHD have a higher depression risk with or without the pill. These are associations, not proof the pill causes depression.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“The pill causes depression, especially if you have ADHD.”

The popular version states cause; the studies show associations, and much of the higher risk with ADHD is present whether or not the pill is used.

What the studies found

In over 1 million Danish women, hormonal contraception users had higher rates of first antidepressant use than non-users (combined pill adjusted incidence rate ratio 1.23; teenagers 1.8) (Skovlund 2016), and current or recent use was linked to first suicide attempt compared with never-use (hazard ratio 1.97) (Skovlund 2018). In Swedish women aged 15-24, women with ADHD had a higher depression risk than women without ADHD whatever their contraception (adjusted hazard ratio 3.69); combined-pill users with ADHD had aHR 5.19 compared with non-users without ADHD and 6.10 compared with pill users without ADHD (Lundin 2023). Because every comparison uses women without ADHD as the reference, much of the excess reflects ADHD itself rather than the pill. These are observational associations, not proof the pill causes depression.

Link to this claim

In self-selected surveys and interviews, many people who report PMDD describe being dismissed or misdiagnosed by clinicians, often over years.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Self-selected surveys and qualitative interviews of people reporting PMDD; few had diagnoses made with daily ratings

Many people with PMDD report being dismissed or misdiagnosed, often for years; these surveys are self-selected, so they show the problem is common, not how often it happens overall.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Doctors dismiss PMDD.”

The popular version goes further: it treats a commonly reported experience as true of all doctors, and not every clinical disagreement is dismissal.

What the studies found

In an Australian survey of 267 people with PMDD, participants had seen an average of 5.1 providers seeking a diagnosis; 54% reported medical gaslighting, 56% misdiagnosis, and only 19% were diagnosed in line with DSM recommendations (Border 2026). Interviews with 27 women in nine countries described medical gas-lighting and diagnostic odysseys (Mosalisa 2026), and UK women described 'lost decades' of misdiagnosis (Osborn 2020). In 2,512 survey respondents, gynaecologists and psychiatrists were rated highest on PMDD knowledge (Hantsoo 2022). A scoping review lists limited provider knowledge and guideline non-adherence among barriers (Nayak 2025). All samples are self-selected and may over-represent people with poor experiences, so they show the problem is common, not how often it happens overall. Not every clinical disagreement is dismissal: a clinician may reasonably ask for prospective ratings, or consider PME or another diagnosis.

Link to this claim

In small hormone experiments, normal hormone changes brought back symptoms in women with PMS or PMDD but not in other women, pointing to brain sensitivity rather than abnormal hormone levels.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Small hormone-suppression and add-back experiments in 20 women with PMS and 12 with PMDD, plus a review

In experiments, the same normal hormone levels brought back symptoms in women with PMS or PMDD but not in other women, and the change in levels seemed to matter more than the level itself.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“PMDD is caused by low progesterone or a hormone imbalance.”

This has been tested and the evidence runs the other way: the problem appears to be sensitivity to normal hormone shifts, not low levels.

What the studies found

When ovarian hormones were switched off and then added back at normal levels, symptoms returned in women with PMS but not in women without it (Schmidt 1998). In 12 women with PMDD, symptoms came back when hormone levels rose but not once levels were steady, pointing to the change rather than the level (Schmidt 2017). Reviews describe PMDD as an abnormal brain response to normal hormone fluctuations (Hantsoo 2020).

Link to this claim

In women with mood disorders, premenstrual worsening is common and is often premenstrual exacerbation of the existing condition, which differs from PMDD.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Review of mostly small studies of women with mood disorders; daily-ratings validation study; one small RCT in suicidal outpatients

Premenstrual worsening of an existing condition is often premenstrual exacerbation (PME), which differs from PMDD. Most research is in mood disorders rather than ADHD; daily tracking plus a clinician's judgement helps tell them apart, and care or urgent help doesn't need to wait.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“If your ADHD, anxiety or depression gets worse before your period, that's PMDD.”

The popular version treats all premenstrual worsening as PMDD, when it is often a separate pattern called premenstrual exacerbation.

What the studies found

Worsening of an existing condition before menstruation is called premenstrual exacerbation (PME); around 60% of women with mood disorders report it, and treatments that help PMDD show less or no benefit for it, from sparse and mostly small studies (Kuehner 2021). Prospective daily ratings, for example DRSP ratings scored with the C-PASS, establish whether the PMDD pattern is met; telling PMDD from PME, or recognising both, still needs a clinician's judgement (Eisenlohr-Moul 2017). In 28 outpatients with suicidal thoughts, 93% had their peak around menstruation, yet none met full PMDD criteria (Eisenlohr-Moul 2022).

Link to this claim

On average, premenstrual symptoms did not worsen across the menopause transition, but women with PMS were more likely to report low mood and poor sleep.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: Midlife women with self-reported PMS in US population cohorts and daily diaries; no studies of confirmed PMDD

Women with a history of premenstrual symptoms appear more vulnerable to low mood and other symptoms in perimenopause, and irregular cycles can make patterns hard to see. We did not find a study following confirmed PMDD through the late transition (search last updated October 2026).

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“PMDD gets worse in perimenopause.”

This has been tested for premenstrual symptoms in general and the evidence mostly runs the other way; PMDD itself has not been followed through the transition.

What the studies found

In a population cohort of 436 women followed for 5 years, premenstrual syndrome decreased across the menopause transition, but women with PMS were more likely to report hot flushes (odds ratio 2.09), depressed mood (odds ratio 2.34) and poor sleep (odds ratio 1.72) (Freeman 2004, Obstet Gynecol). In daily diaries from 290 women, no clear effect of late reproductive vs early menopausal-transition stage on premenstrual symptom severity was found (Mitchell 2022). Among depressed perimenopausal women, 21% met criteria for premenstrual dysphoria vs 3% of non-depressed women (Richards 2006), and depressive symptoms are more likely during the transition (Freeman 2004, Arch Gen Psychiatry). We did not find a study following prospectively confirmed PMDD through the late transition (search last updated October 2026).

Link to this claim

SSRIs help PMDD whether taken daily or only in the luteal phase, often within the first cycle, though daily dosing was probably more effective on average.

Supported evidence Replicated, with some limits.

Who was studied: RCTs in women with PMS or PMDD pooled by Cochrane 2024, and large RCTs in women with DSM-defined PMDD

Benefit often appears in the first treatment cycle, which is why luteal-only dosing can work. The 2024 Cochrane review found daily dosing probably works better on average, and starting only when symptoms begin has weaker evidence. Which schedule suits someone is decided with their prescriber.

Read the evidence

You may have heard

“Taking an SSRI only in the two weeks before your period (or only on symptom days) works as well as taking it every day.”

Both schedules help, but pooled trials suggest daily dosing works better on average.

“SSRIs work for PMDD within days, unlike for depression.”

Benefit appears within the first cycle, but we did not find a trial reporting onset day by day (search last updated October 2026).

What the studies found

Both schedules have evidence. In the 2024 Cochrane review (34 RCTs), SSRIs beat placebo when taken continuously (SMD -0.69; 7 studies, 1055 participants) and in the luteal phase only (SMD -0.39; 6 studies, 687 participants), both moderate-certainty evidence; the review concluded SSRIs are probably more effective when taken continuously (P = 0.03 for subgroup difference) (Jespersen 2024). That is a between-subgroup comparison of placebo-controlled trials, so it describes an average, not what suits each person. In a three-arm RCT that compared schedules directly, luteal-phase sertraline did not differ from continuous dosing, both beat placebo with improvement appearing swiftly in the first month, placebo had caught up by the third month, and women with symptoms after their period ended did worse on either schedule (Freeman 2004). Luteal sertraline beat placebo in 281 women with PMDD, already by the first cycle (responders 50% vs 26%) (Halbreich 2002). Symptom-onset dosing missed its primary outcome (P = .06), though it helped on depression and irritability scales (Yonkers 2015). The 2013 version of the Cochrane review had found no clear difference between schedules (Marjoribanks 2013); it is superseded by the 2024 update. We did not find a trial reporting a day-by-day onset (search last updated October 2026).
